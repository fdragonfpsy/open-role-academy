## Why this matters

A budget or plan is only useful if you compare it with what actually happened. In controlling, this comparison of plan and actual (in German often called Soll-Ist-Vergleich) is the starting point of almost every management conversation. Reading it correctly means knowing how the difference is calculated, what its sign means and what it does not tell you.

## Calculating a variance

In this course a **variance** is defined as **actual minus budget** (or plan). The result can be shown as an absolute amount and as a percentage of the budget: percentage variance = variance / budget × 100. Always state the convention you use, because some reports subtract the other way round. A consistent convention within a report matters more than the choice itself.

## Favourable or unfavourable

The sign alone does not say whether the news is good. For **revenue and profit**, actual above budget is favourable. For **costs**, actual above budget is unfavourable. This is why many reports add a separate favourable/unfavourable (F/U) marker next to the number. Be careful with items such as an inventory build-up or an early payment: whether they are good or bad depends on the context, not only on the sign.

## A variance is a signal, not an explanation

A variance tells you where actual and plan differ. It does not tell you why. The cause may be a real change in the business, a wrong plan, a timing shift (a bill booked one month early) or a booking error. Good practice is to flag the variance, ask the budget owner and write down the explanation. A budget that stays fixed while volumes change can also make a comparison misleading, which is why later units introduce flexible budgets.

## Which variances to look at first

Nobody can explain every line. Typical filters are an **absolute threshold** (for example, larger than a set amount), a **percentage threshold** and a combination of both, so that a large percentage on a tiny line does not distract you. The thresholds are an agreement with management, not a law of nature. Persistent small variances in the same direction are also worth a look.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. The budget for team travel in March is 8,000 EUR and the actual is 9,200 EUR. Variance = 9,200 - 8,000 = +1,200 EUR, which is +15 % of the budget. For a cost this is unfavourable. The same month, budgeted revenue is 120,000 EUR and actual revenue is 114,000 EUR. Variance = -6,000 EUR, or -5 %. For revenue a negative variance is also unfavourable. With a rule "explain anything above 1,000 EUR and 10 %", only the travel line needs a written explanation; the revenue line is large in amount but below the percentage threshold, so the analyst may still decide to mention it because of its size.

## Key takeaways

- Variance = actual - budget; state the convention and show amount and percent.
- Favourable or unfavourable depends on whether the line is revenue or cost.
- A variance is a prompt to ask why, not an explanation.
- Use agreed thresholds to focus, and document the explanations.
