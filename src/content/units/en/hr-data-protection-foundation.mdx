## Why this matters

HR holds some of the most sensitive information a company has: who someone is, what they earn, when they are ill, how they perform. Handling it carelessly can harm real people and the company's trust. This unit gives you the basic vocabulary and habits. It deliberately contains no deadlines, retention periods, article or section numbers, thresholds or fine amounts, because these depend on current law and on your company's rules. Check current law, your data protection officer (DPO) or legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice, and this content is not a substitute for company policy. Context here is the EU and Germany.

## What personal data is

The German Wikipedia article on personal data describes it as all information that relates to an identified or identifiable person. A person counts as identifiable if they can be identified directly or indirectly, for example through a name, an identification number, location data or an online identifier. The article lists examples that surprise many people: a work email address and even the content of a work email count too. So in HR, nearly everything you touch is personal data: applications, contracts, absence records, performance notes, bank details.

The English article on the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) says the same in short form and adds that the regulation uses defined terms such as "processing", "controller" and "processor". Processing is almost anything you do with data: collecting, storing, changing, sharing, deleting. The controller is the organisation that decides why and how data is processed; in HR work that is normally your employer, not you personally.

## The core principles

The GDPR article names the principles that guide all processing: data must be processed lawfully, fairly and in a transparent manner, and the other principles are purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, and integrity and confidentiality. In everyday terms:

- **Lawfulness**: there must be a valid reason to process the data. You do not decide that alone; policies and your DPO do.
- **Purpose limitation**: use data only for the purpose it was collected for.
- **Data minimisation**: collect only what you need.
- **Accuracy**: keep it correct and up to date.
- **Storage limitation**: do not keep data forever; ask what your company rule is.
- **Integrity and confidentiality**: protect it from unauthorised access and loss.

## Need-to-know in daily HR work

Confidentiality is the principle you apply most often. Ask yourself: does this person need this information to do their job? The German article on the personnel file says files are to be treated as strictly confidential and secured against unauthorised access. Practical habits:

- Share information only with people who need it for their task, and only the part they need.
- Do not discuss individuals in corridors, group chats or private messaging apps; use the channels your company has approved.
- Lock your screen, clear your desk, store paper files in locked cabinets.
- Use strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication; see the BSI password page linked below.
- Be careful with email: check recipients and attachments before sending.

## Whom to ask

The English article on the data protection officer describes the DPO as the person who ensures within an organisation that data protection laws are implemented, who should be able to advise senior management directly and who is typically involved in awareness-raising among employees. Whenever you are unsure, ask your DPO or your HR lead before you act, not afterwards.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. A manager says to you: "Employee A has been off a lot. Can you send me the list of reasons so I can plan?" You do not answer from memory and you do not forward a file. Step 1: pause; health-related information needs extra care and the manager may only need to know availability. Step 2: tell the manager you will check what may be shared. Step 3: ask your DPO or HR lead. Step 4: if something may be shared, share only the minimum, through the approved channel. Step 5: note what you shared and why, as company policy requires.

## Key takeaways

- Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person; most HR data qualifies.
- Know the principles: lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality.
- Apply need-to-know: share the minimum with the people who need it, through approved channels.
- When in doubt, ask your DPO before acting.
- This is orientation for the EU and Germany; check current law, your DPO or legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice.
