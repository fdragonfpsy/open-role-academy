## Why this matters

At advanced level you steer L&D as a system: which capabilities does the organisation need, who builds them, how and at what cost, and how do you know it works? This unit uses a Germany/EU setting for context. It contains no legal specifics and no benchmark figures. Rules on funding, training time, co-determination and employee data depend on current law, collective agreements and company rules; check them or ask legal counsel.

## Link L&D to strategy

The German article on Personalentwicklung ties development measures to company goals. Practice insight: start from strategic questions (new markets, technologies, regulations, expected retirements), translate them into capabilities needed, compare with the current skill picture and then prioritise. Prioritise by business impact and urgency, not by whichever course is easiest to buy. Say clearly what you will not do.

## Formal, social and on-the-job learning

The 70:20:10 idea is a popular rule of thumb that most learning happens on the job, some through others and a small part through formal courses. Treat it as a prompt for thinking, not as measured fact: the numbers are not a precise finding for every context. The useful consequence is that courses alone rarely change work. Combine them with practice tasks, feedback from managers and peers, and mentoring, as the Mentorship article describes. Ask managers to agree on application tasks before and after a course.

## Learning culture

Learning culture is shaped by what leaders do (practice insight): time for learning in the working week, mistakes treated as a source of learning, visible support from managers, and ways for people to share knowledge, such as internal talks or communities. Without time and manager support, even good offers stay unused.

## Measure impact honestly

Use the four evaluation levels (reaction, learning, behaviour, results) but be honest about attribution: a change in a business indicator can have many causes. Practice insight: define the indicator and a comparison before the measure (for example a group that starts later), collect evidence for behaviour change, and report uncertainty. The Bildungscontrolling article stresses that education is hard to measure like a technical process. Do not invent a return-on-investment figure; if you cannot support a number, say what you know and what you do not.

## Data protection and tools

An LMS stores who learned what and when, as the LMS article describes. This is employee data. Define purpose, access and retention before introducing it, involve the data protection officer and, where applicable, the works council, and check current law. Avoid using learning data for appraisals or sanctions unless this has been clearly agreed and is lawful.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. The company plans a new data platform. You compare the capabilities needed with the current picture from skill conversations and see a gap in data literacy among team leads. Plan: a short formal introduction, a six-week application task in the leads' own area, peer pairs for feedback and a mentor from the data team. Evaluation: participant feedback (reaction), a practical check (learning), a manager conversation after two months (behaviour) and the share of reports built by the teams themselves compared with a group that starts later (results). In the report you state the comparison and its limits. You agree with the data protection officer that the LMS shows only completion to managers, not individual quiz scores.

## Key takeaways

- Derive the L&D plan from strategy and capability gaps, and prioritise.
- Combine formal courses with on-the-job and social learning; treat 70:20:10 as a prompt, not a law.
- Learning culture needs time, manager support and shared knowledge.
- Measure impact with a clear comparison and honest limits; no invented figures.
- Learning data is employee data: define purpose, access and retention with the data protection officer.
