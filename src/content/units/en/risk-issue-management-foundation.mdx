## Why this matters

Few projects are derailed by a surprise nobody could imagine. Most are hurt by a problem that someone saw coming and nobody owned. Risk management gives such concerns a place, a name and a person. It covers identifying, analysing and evaluating risks (together called risk assessment), responding to them and communicating about them, and it is an ongoing cycle of planning, acting, checking and improving rather than a one-off list.

## Four terms often confused (our own wording)

- Risk: something that may happen, with uncertain probability, and that would affect the project's goals.
- Issue: something that has already happened and needs action now.
- Assumption: something your plan takes as true without proof.
- Dependency: something the project needs from someone else.

A risk becomes an issue when the event occurs. Unconfirmed assumptions and dependencies are common sources of risk, so each one deserves a look.

## Writing a risk (our own)

Use one sentence with three parts: "Because of [cause], [event] may happen, leading to [effect]." For example: "Because only one trainer is available, the training may start late, which delays go-live." Entries such as "budget problems" cannot be rated, owned or answered. The cause tells you where to act, the event tells you what to watch for and the effect tells you how much it matters.

## The risk register

A risk register is a repository of all identified risks, with additional information about each one. A typical entry has a category, an identifier, a short description, impact and probability rated on a numeric scale, a risk score (probability times impact), mitigation measures, a contingent response, a trigger and an owner. A spreadsheet is enough. How well the register works depends on how it is used and on the culture of the organisation, not on the tool.

## Choosing a response (our own wording)

- Avoid: change the plan so that the risk cannot occur.
- Reduce: lower the probability or the impact.
- Transfer: shift the consequence to another party, for example by contract or insurance.
- Accept: do nothing now, and perhaps keep contingency for it.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. A team prepares a conference. Risk R1: "Because the venue is the only one with the required capacity, a cancellation by the venue may leave us without a room, so the event cannot take place on the planned date." Probability 2 of 5, impact 5 of 5, score 10. Response: reduce. The team obtains a written booking confirmation with cancellation terms and names a backup venue. Owner: Priya. Trigger: no signed confirmation by 1 March. Separately, a speaker has already fallen ill. That is not a risk any more; it is an issue, and it goes on the issue list with an owner who finds a replacement this week.

## Key takeaways

- A risk may happen, an issue has happened; keep them in separate lists.
- Write each risk as cause, event and effect so that it can be judged.
- Every register entry needs an owner, a response and a trigger.
- Choose among avoid, reduce, transfer and accept deliberately, and review the register regularly.
