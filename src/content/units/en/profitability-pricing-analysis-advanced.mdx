## Why this matters

At advanced level, controlling does not only calculate prices; it advises on how a price should be set and what a decision really costs. Three approaches are common, and good practice combines them.

## Three pricing approaches

- **Cost-plus pricing** adds a markup to cost. It is simple and transparent, but it ignores what customers are willing to pay, and with fully allocated costs the price depends on the volume assumed in the allocation.
- **Value-based pricing** sets the price from the value the customer perceives. It can capture more of the benefit, but needs evidence about customers and segments, and value is hard to measure.
- **Market-based (competitor-oriented) pricing** takes prices of comparable offers as a reference. It is easy to observe, but it does not show whether the resulting price is profitable.

Controlling usually provides the cost floor and the margin impact, while sales and marketing provide customer value and market information.

## Target margin and markup

To reach a target margin m on price, price = cost / (1 - m). For a cost of 70 and a target margin of 30 %, price = 70 / 0.70 = 100. The equivalent markup on cost is 100 / 70 - 1 = about 42.9 %. A markup of 30 % on cost would only give a price of 91 and a margin of about 23 %.

## Relevant costs and special orders

For a one-off decision, such as a special order below the usual price, the relevant question is which costs and revenues change because of the decision. Costs that exist anyway (existing fixed costs) are not relevant. If there is spare capacity, a price above the variable cost per unit adds to the contribution. If capacity is scarce, the contribution the capacity would earn elsewhere (opportunity cost) must be considered. Also consider side effects: other customers may demand the same price, and a one-off low price can become a reference.

## Presenting a recommendation

State the decision, the key numbers (price, contribution margin per unit, volume, break-even), the assumptions, a sensitivity range and the risks. Separate facts from judgement, and say which data you could not verify.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. Variable cost is 30 EUR per unit and the list price is 50 EUR. A customer asks for 1,000 units at 38 EUR. Spare capacity exists and no extra fixed costs arise. Contribution = 1,000 x (38 - 30) = 8,000 EUR, so the order improves profit if no side effects occur. If capacity were full and the same hours could produce another product earning 12 EUR per unit-equivalent, the order would give up more than it earns. The recommendation should also mention the risk that other customers learn of the price.

## Key takeaways

- Combine cost-plus (floor), value-based (ceiling) and market-based (reference) views.
- Price for a target margin = cost / (1 - margin); do not confuse margin and markup.
- For one-off decisions use relevant costs, capacity and side effects.
- Present assumptions, sensitivity and risks with every recommendation.
