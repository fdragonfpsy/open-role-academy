## Why this matters

Once the question is clear, two skills decide the value of your work: choosing an approach that can really answer it, and telling the result so that the audience acts on it.

## From question to hypotheses

A hypothesis is a statement that data could contradict, for example "Cancellations rose mainly among customers who had two or more support tickets in their first month." Write down what you would expect to see if it is true and what would show it is false. This keeps you from searching for patterns until something looks interesting.

## Choose an approach

- Describe: what happened (counts, trends, comparisons).
- Diagnose: why did it happen (breakdowns, comparisons between groups).
- Predict or recommend: only when the data and the decision need it.

Pick the simplest approach that answers the question. Check that the data covers the right period and population, and that you can name what you cannot conclude. Remember that a correlation is not proof of a cause.

## Structure the story

A reliable order for a short report or presentation:

- Answer: the main finding in one or two sentences.
- Evidence: two or three charts or numbers, each with a clear title that states the message.
- Limits: data gaps, assumptions and what you did not test.
- Recommendation: what the audience could do, with options where it is a judgement call.

## Know your audience

Executives want the answer and the decision needed. Specialists want method and detail, which can go into an appendix. Use the audience's words, not your tool's, and remove everything that does not support the message.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. The question is why returns increased in one product category. Hypothesis: the increase comes from one supplier's batch. The analyst compares return rates by supplier and month. One supplier's rate rose from 4 to 11 percent in two months, other suppliers stayed flat. The report opens with "Returns increased because of one supplier batch", shows one chart per message, states that the cause is likely but not proven because return reasons were free text, and recommends asking Quality to inspect the batch.

## Key takeaways

- Write hypotheses that data could contradict, and decide in advance what would count as evidence.
- Use the simplest approach that answers the question.
- Structure findings as answer, evidence, limits, recommendation.
- Adapt depth and wording to the audience.
