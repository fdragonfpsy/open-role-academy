## Why this matters

After the first months the risks change. Relying on formal authority, falling back on expert work under pressure or never reviewing your own habits can hold a team back. This unit discusses these risks at concept level. It reflects common management thinking, not proven formulas.

## Sources of influence

The OpenStax chapter on the nature of leadership contrasts managers and leaders by the sources of their power: managers commonly derive power from the organisation (rewards, sanctions, formal authority), while followers of a leader comply because they want to. The chapter also stresses that both are needed. Wikipedia notes that leadership is contested and that some authors question views of leadership as something owned by one person in a role. For you this means: formal authority gets you started, but sustained results depend on trust and on the team's willingness to follow.

A useful self-check: if you left tomorrow, would the team still know why priorities are what they are? If not, you may rely too much on position or on personal control.

## Expert work versus lead work

Under pressure, many leads fall back on what they do best, the expert tasks. Sometimes this is right, for example in a real emergency. As a habit it has costs: it removes your time from planning and development of people, and it signals that you do not trust the team. Decide deliberately which expert tasks you keep (for example those that keep your judgement current) and which you hand over, and say why.

## Style flexibility and its limits

The situational leadership article describes adapting style to situation and person, and notes that several studies do not support all prescriptions. Use the idea to ask questions, not to label people. Avoid treating a person as "low readiness" permanently; look for what has changed since you last assessed them.

## A self-review routine

Once a month, take 30 minutes: what did I decide, what did I delegate, where did I intervene without needing to, what did the team need from me that I did not provide? Collect feedback from your team and your manager, and pick one behaviour to change. Keep the notes short and private. Agree with your manager how you will be assessed so that you are not surprised.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented person. Sara leads eight people. Under deadline pressure she rewrites two analyses herself over a weekend. In her monthly review she sees that this was the third such weekend in a quarter and that her team stopped proposing improvements. She agrees with the team that urgent fixes go to a rota first and that she reviews only the final results. She tells her manager what she changed and why, and asks for feedback after two months.

## Key takeaways

- Formal authority starts a lead; lasting influence rests on trust and willingness to follow.
- Falling back on expert work is sometimes right but costly as a habit; choose deliberately.
- Use style models for questions, not labels; avoid fixed judgements about people.
- A short monthly self-review with feedback keeps your leadership improving.
