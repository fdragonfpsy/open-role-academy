## Why this matters

At practitioner level you close deals independently. The risk moves from "forgot to ask" to "closed something the company cannot deliver or has not approved". Good closing combines a clear ask, disciplined paperwork and a handover that matches the contract.

## Standard terms and deviations

Most companies sell on standard terms prepared by legal. Customers, especially larger ones, ask for changes: a different payment term, a termination right, liability limits, data processing terms. Your job is not to decide on these alone. Know which changes you may accept (for example, a small payment-term change within your approval matrix) and which go to legal, finance or management. A short internal route, with the requested change, the reason and the deal value, shortens the cycle. In Germany the content of contracts and general terms is a legal matter; the German Wikipedia article on contracts links to the statute, and for real cases you rely on your legal team, not on an encyclopedia.

## Closing without pressure

Common closing approaches work when they help the customer decide. A summary close recaps the agreed value and asks for the decision. An alternative close offers two real options ("annual or quarterly billing?"). A next-step close agrees the signature date. Pressure closes, such as false scarcity or an invented discount deadline, can damage trust and may create legal or complaint risk. If a discount really ends on a date, say so with the real reason.

## Authority and form

Check who signs for the customer. A contact who liked the demo may not be authorised to sign. Ask early: "Who signs, and what internal approvals do you need?" Also check your own side: who may sign for your company, and which deal sizes need a second approval. The general contract article describes a contract as legally enforceable rights and obligations of the parties, which is why unclear wording matters: the document, not the conversation, is what you will be held to.

## Handover

Include the contract itself, the customer's goals and success criteria, the agreed start date, special terms, risks (for example "customer expects an integration that is not in scope") and contacts on both sides. Do a short handover call with the delivery lead for larger deals, and tell the customer about the introduction.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented. A customer wants a 60-day payment term instead of your standard 30 and the right to terminate at any time. Payment terms within your matrix are fine, but termination rights go to legal. You send legal a note with the request, the deal value and the customer's reason. Legal offers a termination right after twelve months with notice. You present this as an alternative close ("12 months with notice, or 24 months at a lower price?"). The customer picks the first. You also learn that the signatory is the finance director, so you schedule the signature with her. Your handover lists the agreed payment term, the termination clause and the risk that the customer expects a reporting feature that is only planned.

## Key takeaways

- Know your approval limits; send deviations to legal or management.
- Use closing methods that support a real decision; avoid false pressure.
- Confirm who signs and who needs to approve.
- The document is what counts, so put agreed points into it.
- Hand over risks and special terms, not only contacts.
