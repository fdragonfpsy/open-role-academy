## Why this matters

At practitioner level you own a campaign from brief to review: you turn a goal into measurable targets, split a budget across channels, brief other teams or an agency, and react when numbers drift.

## From objective to KPIs

OpenStax notes that marketers typically care about objectives such as return on investment, cost per impression, cost per lead and customer lifetime value, and that in a truly integrated campaign the objectives stay the same while execution changes across channels. Choose one primary objective, then at most two or three supporting indicators. A KPI (key performance indicator) needs a target value and a date. "More awareness" is not a KPI; "5,000 visits to the landing page from tagged links by day 30" is. The numbers in this unit are invented.

## Channel mix and timeline

Select channels by where the target group can be reached and what the message needs, not by habit. Use the core message in every channel with different formats (OpenStax on integrated marketing communications). Build a timeline backwards from the launch date: when must creative be approved, when must tracking links exist, when do legal or data protection checks need to be finished? Mark dependencies, such as "email cannot go out before the consent check".

## Budget as a projected P&L

OpenStax describes the supporting marketing budget as essentially a projected profit-and-loss statement: on the revenue side the forecast number of units and the average net price, on the expense side production, distribution and marketing costs. For a single campaign this means: expected revenue or value, minus the costs. The German Wikipedia article on Budgetierung adds that budgeting includes the later check against the plan, and that it is usually short-term because longer horizons reduce forecasting certainty. Therefore split the budget into phases, keep a reserve of your own choosing and release the second part only after the first results.

## Controls and adjusting

OpenStax names controls as the last section of a marketing plan: monitor metrics during the period, and if something falls outside the desired range, analyse the root cause and adjust. Set review dates in advance (for example after week one and week three) and agree the rule: which deviation triggers which action. A comparison of planned and actual spend per activity is the simplest control.

## Working with an agency

A brief (our own working method) contains: goal and KPIs, target group, core message, budget and timeline, mandatory legal or brand rules, approvals and who decides, and what you will receive and when. Agree how results are reported and who may see customer data. If an agency will handle personal data, such as email lists, ask your data protection officer before sharing anything. Keep one contact person on each side.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A Nuremberg bicycle-lock manufacturer plans a spring campaign. Primary objective: 400 newsletter sign-ups at a maximum cost per sign-up of the amount the team calculated from its margin. Channels: landing page, social posts, an email to existing subscribers and a small paid-search budget. The budget is split into a first phase (60 percent) and a second phase released after week three. In the week-three review the paid search costs more per sign-up than expected, so the team shifts part of the second phase to the email channel.

## Key takeaways

- One primary objective plus a few supporting KPIs, each with a target and a date.
- Same core message in every channel; formats differ.
- Build the timeline backwards, with approval and legal checks as dependencies.
- The budget is a projected P&L; phase it and compare planned to actual.
- Brief agencies in writing and ask data protection before sharing personal data.
