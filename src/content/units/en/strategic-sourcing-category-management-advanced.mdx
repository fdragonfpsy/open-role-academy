## Why this matters

At this level the numbers rarely agree. The cheapest offer carries the highest running costs, consolidation lowers the price but raises dependence, and a reported saving may not survive a look at its baseline. Good category managers make the trade-off visible.

## Building a TCO model (our own structure)

No binding standard exists for total cost of ownership; models differ, and the term is often mixed up with life cycle costing. So state your model:

- Acquisition: price, delivery, installation, training.
- Operation: energy, consumables, maintenance and repair over the period you choose.
- Risk and transaction costs: effort to manage the supplier, cost of delays or quality problems.
- End of life: disposal or resale value.

Write down the period and the assumptions, and show a sensitivity: how does the result change if usage is 20 percent higher? Ask finance whether future costs should be discounted.

## Consolidation and dependence

Bundling volume with fewer suppliers strengthens your bargaining power, which suits leverage categories. For strategic and bottleneck categories the question is different: how dependent do we become, what does it cost if the supplier fails, and can we build an alternative? Criteria for make or buy include transaction costs, reliability of supply and dependence, and the cost comparison itself should use relevant, avoidable costs. A cheaper price can be a teaser rate; a supplier who cannot scale with your volume creates its own cost.

## Reporting savings

Savings numbers cause arguments when the baseline is unclear. Our own practice: state the baseline (last price paid, budget or market price), the period, and the volume used, separate price reductions from reduced consumption, and let finance confirm the figure.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented numbers, undiscounted for simplicity. A company compares two machines over five years. Machine A costs 100 thousand euro, with 22 thousand per year for maintenance, 9 thousand per year for energy, 4 thousand for disposal and 3 thousand for training: 262 thousand in total. Machine B costs 118 thousand, with 12 thousand maintenance, 6 thousand energy, 2 thousand disposal and no training: 210 thousand. B wins by 52 thousand euro although it is 18 thousand dearer to buy. The team also notes that B comes from a new supplier with a single service centre, so it asks for a service-level commitment and a spare-parts agreement before signing, and keeps A as a fallback until the first year is over.

## Key takeaways

- A TCO model is only as good as its stated period, cost items and assumptions; test a sensitivity.
- Consolidation helps leverage categories but increases dependence in strategic and bottleneck ones.
- Report savings with baseline, period and volume, and have finance confirm them.
- When cost and risk disagree, document the trade-off and the safeguards you chose.
