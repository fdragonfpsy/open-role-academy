## Why this matters

Inclusion becomes real in the details of HR processes: how a job ad is written, how an interview is run, whether a form can be read with a screen reader. This unit builds on the basics and shows practical steps. Germany/EU context: legal duties (for example on adjustments for people with disabilities) depend on current law and your situation, so confirm them with legal counsel or the responsible experts; this unit does not state them.

## Reduce bias with structure

The Implicit stereotype article describes unconscious associations that can affect hiring and notes that organisations use evidence-based strategies to reduce their effect. The Job interview article describes how interviews can be more or less structured. Practice insight: structure is one of the most practical tools.

- Define the criteria for the role before you see applications.
- Ask every candidate the same core questions in the same order.
- Score each answer against a simple rubric right after the interview, before the group discusses.
- Use more than one assessor and compare scores independently first.
- Where your process allows, review applications with identifying details hidden.
- Look at the result: if one group repeatedly drops out at one step, review that step.

## Accessibility in practice

The Accessibility article describes designing products, services and environments so that disabled people can use them, directly or with assistive technology, and distinguishes it from general usability. The German article on Barrierefreiheit describes it as the design of the environment so that all people can use it without unnecessary hindrance. Practice insight: check the whole candidate and employee journey.

- Career pages, forms and PDFs usable with keyboard and screen readers.
- Interview rooms reachable and quiet enough; offer video or phone options where they fit the role.
- Ask in invitations whether anything is needed for the process, without asking for reasons or diagnoses.
- Provide documents in clear structure and plain wording; the Leichte Sprache article describes a simplified form of language that helps many readers.

## Adjustments and confidentiality

When a person asks for an adjustment, keep the conversation on what is needed to do the work, not on medical details. Record only what is necessary, store it with restricted access and share it only with people who must act. Follow data protection rules and involve the responsible experts.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. A company notices that few applicants complete the online form. You test it with a keyboard only and find that a dropdown cannot be reached and the CV upload has no text label. You raise this with the IT team as an accessibility defect and add an alternative way to apply by email. In the same project you replace free-form interviews with a structured one: five core questions, a three-level rubric and independent scoring. After two hiring rounds you compare drop-out by step, using only aggregated numbers.

## Key takeaways

- Structure (criteria first, same questions, independent scoring) reduces the room for bias.
- Accessibility covers the whole journey: forms, documents, rooms, communication.
- Ask what support is needed, not why; keep sensitive information confidential and minimal.
- Review results by process step to find where fairness breaks down.
- Legal duties: check current law and involve experts.
