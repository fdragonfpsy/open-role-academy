## Why this matters

Financial statements are the formal records of a company's financial activities and position. Owners, managers, banks and employees use them to make decisions, so everyone in finance and accounting needs to know what is in them and how the parts fit together. The ledger work you already know (postings, reconciliations, accruals) ends up here.

## The main statements

A balance sheet lists a company's **assets, liabilities and equity** at one point in time, for example the end of the financial year. It is often described as a snapshot. The terms:

- **Assets**: what the company owns or has a right to, such as cash, receivables or equipment.
- **Liabilities**: what the company owes, such as amounts due to suppliers or a loan.
- **Equity**: the difference between assets and liabilities, also called net assets.

So assets minus liabilities equals equity. In the German balance sheet the same content is shown as two sides: Aktiva (assets, how the funds are used) and Passiva (equity and liabilities, where the funds come from). Both sides add up to the same total.

An **income statement** (profit and loss account, in German Gewinn- und Verlustrechnung or GuV) shows **revenue and expenses over a period**, such as a year. Revenue minus expenses is the profit; if expenses are higher, it is a loss. The key contrast: the balance sheet is a point in time, the income statement covers a period.

## Notes

The notes (German: Anhang) explain and add detail to the figures in the statements. Under German commercial law (HGB), the Anhang is part of the annual financial statements of corporations (Kapitalgesellschaften); the Jahresabschluss itself consists of balance sheet and income statement. Under IFRS, a complete set also includes notes, among them a summary of significant accounting policies. Which rules apply to your company is decided by your company's accountants and the law, not by this unit.

## How the statements connect

Profit is the link. A profit earned during the period increases equity on the balance sheet; a loss decreases it. The German article on the balance sheet describes this: comparing equity at the start and end of the year, after contributions and withdrawals by the owners, gives the profit or loss, and the income statement shows how that result came about.

## Worked example

Illustration only; the amounts are invented. A small company starts the year with equity of 2,000. During the year, revenue is 50,000 and expenses are 44,000. No owner contributions or withdrawals happen.

| Income statement for the year | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 50,000 |
| Expenses | -44,000 |
| Profit | 6,000 |

At year end the balance sheet shows:

| Assets | Amount | Liabilities and equity | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 4,000 | Liabilities to suppliers | 5,000 |
| Receivables | 3,000 | Loan | 7,000 |
| Equipment | 13,000 | Equity | 8,000 |
| Total | 20,000 | Total | 20,000 |

Assets (20,000) minus liabilities (12,000) gives equity of 8,000. This equals the opening equity of 2,000 plus the profit of 6,000. Both statements tell the same story from two angles. The notes would then explain the figures, for example which accounting policies were used.

## Key takeaways

- The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time; assets minus liabilities equals equity.
- The income statement shows revenue and expenses over a period; the difference is profit or loss.
- Profit changes equity, which links the two statements.
- The notes explain and detail the figures; in Germany the Anhang is part of the annual financial statements of corporations.
- Always state which rules (for example HGB or IFRS) a set of statements follows.
