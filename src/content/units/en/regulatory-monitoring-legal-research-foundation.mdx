## Why this matters

Legal and compliance staff are asked questions such as "Are we allowed to do this?" A reliable answer starts with finding the right source, not with a quick web search. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Primary and secondary sources

The Wikipedia articles on primary sources and on legal research describe the general idea: a primary source is the original material, a secondary source discusses or interprets it. In law, the **primary sources** are the legal texts themselves, such as EU regulations and directives (see the EU portal page on types of legislation) and national statutes, together with court decisions. **Secondary sources** explain them: textbooks, commentaries, articles, agency summaries and encyclopedias. Secondary sources help you get oriented, but the answer must rest on the primary text.

## Where official texts are published

The EU publishes its legal acts in the Official Journal of the European Union, and EUR-Lex gives access to it, including browsing by date. National law in Germany has its own official publication channels; look up which one applies to your question. For an overview of how texts relate to one another, the articles on sources of law (English and German) explain that legal sources have a hierarchy and different roles.

## A simple research routine

1. Write the question in one sentence and note the country or region it concerns.
2. Identify the likely primary source and find it on an official site.
3. Read the actual wording, not only a summary.
4. Use secondary sources to understand terms and context.
5. Note what you found, where, and on which date.

## Limits

Encyclopedia pages, including Wikipedia, are good for orientation only and can change at any time. If the question affects a real decision, a lawyer must confirm the result.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A junior compliance colleague at an invented software firm is asked whether a customer newsletter needs a particular wording. They first search blogs, which disagree. Then they write the question down, find the relevant EU act and the national rule on an official site, read the wording, and use a textbook summary to understand a term. In their note they list each source with a link and the date they read it, and mark the answer "for review by legal counsel".

## Key takeaways

- Primary sources are the legal texts and decisions; secondary sources explain them.
- Official journals and official portals are the place to look for the authoritative wording.
- Write down question, sources and date, so someone else can retrace your work.
- Treat summaries and encyclopedias as orientation, not as proof.
