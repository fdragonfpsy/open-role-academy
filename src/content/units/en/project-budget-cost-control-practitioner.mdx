## Why this matters

"We have spent 40 % of the budget" says nothing unless you know how much work is finished. Comparing actual cost with plan alone can look fine while the project is behind, or look bad while it is ahead. Earned value analysis (in German, Leistungswertanalyse) adds the missing figure: the value of the work that is really done.

## Three values and three derived figures

Planned value (Planwert) is the budgeted cost of the work that should be finished by today. Actual cost (Istkosten) is what the work so far has really cost. Earned value (Fertigstellungswert) is the budgeted cost of the work that is really finished. Together they describe the current cost and schedule situation and allow trend analysis. Derived figures, in our own wording:

- Cost variance = earned value minus actual cost; negative means over cost.
- Schedule variance in money = earned value minus planned value; negative means behind plan.
- Cost performance index = earned value divided by actual cost; below 1 means each euro buys less than planned.

## Forecasting

If the cost trend continues, a first forecast at completion is the total budget divided by the cost performance index. That is only one scenario. Our own rule: always set it against a bottom-up estimate, which is actual cost so far plus a fresh estimate of the remaining work by the people who will do it. If the two differ strongly, find out why before you report either. Small budget units make both figures more exact.

## Variance report (our own)

A report that supports a decision has four parts:

- Variance: what differs, in money and in percent.
- Cause: one sentence, checked with the work package owner.
- Forecast: the expected total cost at completion.
- Options: at least two, with effects on cost, time, scope and risk, plus one recommendation.

Variance analysis does not find a problem by itself; it points to where one may exist, for favourable and unfavourable variances alike. A traffic light gives a quick overview, but red does not mean "stop all work"; it means counter-steer so that the project can still finish on budget and on time.

## Corrective action and sunk cost

Money already spent is not a reason to spend more. Escalation of commitment is the tendency to stay with a poor decision even while the outcomes get worse, because staying the course feels easier than admitting a mistake. Decide on corrective action by comparing what finishing still costs with what the result is still worth.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. The budget at completion is 120 000 EUR. In week 10 the planned value is 60 000 EUR, the earned value 48 000 EUR and the actual cost 56 000 EUR. Cost variance: 48 000 minus 56 000 = minus 8 000 EUR. Schedule variance in money: 48 000 minus 60 000 = minus 12 000 EUR. Cost performance index: 48 000 divided by 56 000, about 0.86. The trend forecast is 120 000 divided by 0.857 = 140 000 EUR. The owners of the open packages re-estimate the remaining work at 78 000 EUR, so the bottom-up figure is 56 000 + 78 000 = 134 000 EUR. The gap is explained by a one-off setup cost in the first package. The report names the cause, forecasts 134 000 to 140 000 EUR and offers two options: drop the optional dashboard (saves 15 000 EUR, reduces scope) or request 14 000 to 20 000 EUR more. It recommends dropping the dashboard.

## Key takeaways

- Compare actual cost with earned value, not with the calendar or with the plan alone.
- Cost variance, schedule variance and a cost performance index follow from three values.
- Back every trend forecast with a bottom-up estimate to complete.
- Decide on corrective action by future cost and value, not by money already spent.
