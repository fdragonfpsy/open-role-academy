## Cost information as one input to a decision

Cost accounting provides managers with information for planning, controlling, evaluating performance, pricing and other business decisions. It is one input among several. Good practitioners know which cost figures fit which decision, and say so.

## Pricing support and its limits

A common starting point is **cost-plus pricing**: take the full unit cost and add a markup. It is simple and transparent, but it has limits. It says nothing about what customers are willing to pay or what competitors charge, and a full unit cost already contains allocated overhead that depends on the allocation key and on volume. A **market-based** approach starts from the achievable price and asks whether it covers the costs. Use cost information to set a floor and to test whether a target price is viable, not as the only basis for the price. The contribution margin is helpful here: according to the Contribution margin article it supports decisions about how to price a product or service and whether to add or drop a product line.

## Make-or-buy and relevant costs

In a make-or-buy decision you compare producing something yourself with buying it. Only **relevant costs** matter: future costs that differ between the alternatives. A **sunk cost** has already been incurred and cannot be recovered; it contrasts with prospective costs that may be avoided if action is taken, and should no longer be relevant to future decisions. Allocated overhead that continues unchanged either way is also not relevant. The Contribution margin article gives a related point: a line with a positive contribution margin may be worth keeping even when total profit is negative, because it offsets part of the fixed cost.

## Activity-based costing

**Activity-based costing (ABC)** identifies activities in an organisation and assigns the cost of each activity to products and services according to the actual consumption by each. Compared with conventional costing it assigns more indirect costs (overhead) as if they were direct costs. It can give a more detailed picture of where overhead arises, at the price of more data collection and effort.

## Cost-volume-profit assumptions

Contribution margin and break-even calculations belong to cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis. The basic form works with simplifications: costs can be split into fixed and variable parts, and price and variable cost per unit stay constant over the range considered. These are general simplifications, not laws of nature. Treat results as an estimate, and test them with different values rather than quoting one number.

## Communicating results

State the decision, the answer, the assumptions and the main risk in plain words. Show which costs you treated as relevant and which you left out and why.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented. A company needs 5,000 parts per year.

- Making in-house: variable cost 7 per part (35,000), a supervisor cost of 5,000 that would disappear if production stopped, allocated overhead of 15,000 that continues either way, and depreciation of 10,000 on a machine already bought.
- Buying: the supplier quotes 9 per part, so 45,000.

A naive full-cost view adds everything: 35,000 + 5,000 + 15,000 + 10,000 = 65,000, or 13 per part, and suggests buying at 9. The relevant costs of making are only 35,000 + 5,000 = 40,000, or 8 per part. Buying costs 45,000. Making is cheaper by 45,000 - 40,000 = 5,000. The allocated overhead and the depreciation are the same in both cases, so they do not affect the choice. A real decision would also consider quality, capacity and supplier risk.

## Key takeaways

- Cost information supports pricing; it does not replace looking at the market.
- In make-or-buy, compare only relevant, future, avoidable costs; ignore sunk costs.
- Full unit cost can mislead in decisions because it contains allocated and sunk amounts.
- ABC assigns overhead by consumption of activities, with more detail and more effort.
- CVP results rest on simplifying assumptions; state them and test them.
- Communicate decision, answer, assumptions and risk.
