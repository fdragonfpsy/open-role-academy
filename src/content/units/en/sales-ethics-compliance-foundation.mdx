Context: EU and Germany. This unit is orientation for sales beginners, not legal advice. For real cases, ask your legal or compliance team.

## Why this matters

Sales is built on trust. A deal won with a false promise usually comes back as a complaint, a refund, a lost renewal or a legal problem. Fair practice is therefore not a brake on selling; it is what keeps customers and the company safe. In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive sets a common frame for selling to consumers, and Germany has its own law against unfair competition (UWG). Rules for business customers differ, but the habit is the same: be honest, be clear, be fair.

## Honest claims

Say only what you can support. If a feature is planned but not available, say that it is planned. The directive treats information as misleading when it is false, and also when the overall presentation deceives, even if each fact is correct. Leaving out information a customer needs to decide, or hiding it in small print, can be misleading too. Exaggerated praise that nobody takes literally ("the best coffee in town") is a different matter from a concrete claim about results, savings or availability, which you must be able to back up.

## Pressure and false urgency

A deadline is fine when it is real: "this price applies until the campaign ends on the 30th". Saying that an offer is available only for a very limited time when it is not, in order to rush the decision, is on the directive's list of practices that are always unfair towards consumers. Harassment, threats and exploiting someone's difficult situation also count as aggressive practices. Even where the directive does not apply, such tactics damage trust.

## Gifts, hospitality and conflicts of interest

Buyers sometimes expect or are offered perks such as meals or trips. These can look like influence over a purchasing decision. Follow your company's gift and hospitality policy, never offer anything to win a decision, and when in doubt ask compliance before you give or accept something. Tell your manager if you have a personal interest in a deal.

## Contact data

People have the right to object to their personal data being used for direct marketing. If someone objects, the data may no longer be processed for that purpose, and the right must be pointed out clearly at the latest at the first contact. So record an objection or unsubscribe in the CRM right away.

## When to ask

You do not need to be a lawyer. You need to notice when something feels unclear: an unusual claim, a gift, a list of contacts of unknown origin. Then pause and ask legal or compliance.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented. A prospect asks whether your tool connects to their accounting software. The connection is planned for next year. Your colleague says: "Just say yes; it will be there by the time they sign." You instead answer: "Not yet. It is on our roadmap, and I will check with the product team what can be promised." You send a short note with the confirmed status and keep the open point in the CRM. The prospect appreciates the clear answer and asks for a call after the release. Nothing you said can later be held against you.

## Key takeaways

- Claim only what you can support; say what is planned, not available.
- Deadlines must be real. Fake scarcity is a known unfair practice.
- Follow the gift and hospitality policy and declare personal interests.
- Record marketing objections immediately.
- Unsure? Pause and ask legal or compliance.
