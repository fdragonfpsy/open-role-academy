## Why this matters

Once you know the building blocks of a compliance system, the practical task is to make them work with limited time and people. That needs priorities and clear roles. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Risk assessment first

A compliance risk assessment asks which rules apply to the business, where breaches could happen and how serious they would be. Rate each risk by how likely it is and how severe the impact would be, then focus effort on the highest combinations. Typical sources are the business activities, countries, products, partners and past incidents. The result is a short, dated list that shows why some topics get more training and monitoring than others.

## From risk to measures

Each priority risk should lead to measures: a policy or procedure that sets the expectation, training for the people affected, and a control or monitoring step that shows whether the rule is followed. A policy without training is rarely read; training without monitoring is rarely effective. Keep policies short, written for the reader and linked to concrete examples. The code of conduct is the umbrella; detailed policies deal with topics such as gifts, data handling or third parties.

## Governance and the three lines model

The articles on corporate governance describe governance as the system by which an organisation is directed and controlled, including the relationship between management, the supervisory body, owners and other stakeholders. The Wikipedia article on the three lines of defence describes a common way to divide roles: the first line is operational management that owns risks and controls; the second line consists of functions such as risk management and compliance that advise and monitor; the third line is internal audit, which gives independent assurance. The article notes that the first line knows the business well but may lack independence, while internal audit reports to the board. Compliance sits mainly in the second line: it should help the business and challenge it, but not take over the business's responsibility.

## Stakeholders and ethics

Rules do not cover every situation. The OpenStax business ethics text on stakeholder claims (link only; US-centred) points out that limited resources force priorities and that the law only partly captures obligations. A compliance function therefore also supports ethical judgement, for example by giving managers a way to discuss grey areas early.

## Reporting and learning

Make it easy to ask questions and to report concerns, and record how each case was handled. Review incidents and monitoring results at least periodically and update the risk assessment and policies. This closes the improvement loop.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A mid-sized distributor lists its risks: unclear rules for supplier gifts, inconsistent contract approvals and weak records of training. It rates gifts as most severe and likely, so it writes a two-page gift policy, trains the purchasing team, and asks the compliance contact to sample gift registers each quarter. Internal audit later reviews whether the sampling happens. The business owns the controls, compliance monitors them, and audit checks both.

## Key takeaways

- Prioritise by likelihood and severity of each risk.
- Each priority risk needs a policy, training and a monitoring step.
- The three lines model separates owning risk, overseeing it and auditing independently.
- Compliance supports and challenges the business; it does not replace management's responsibility.
- Review incidents and update the system regularly.
