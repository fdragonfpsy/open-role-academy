## Why this matters

At practitioner level the quote becomes a proposal: a document that shows you understood the customer's problem, explains the solution, states the price with its conditions and makes the decision easy. A good proposal is built from discovery, not from the price list. Examples are invented; for tax, contract law and procurement rules ask finance or legal (Germany/EU context applies but is not detailed here).

## Structure of a proposal

1. **Customer situation and goals** in the customer's words from discovery.
2. **Solution and scope:** what is included and, as importantly, what is not.
3. **Value:** the benefit in the customer's terms, with assumptions marked.
4. **Price and options,** with terms, validity and payment.
5. **Next steps:** who does what by when.

Keep it short and consistent with what you said in the meeting; the customer must not find surprises.

## Link price to value

The article on value-based pricing describes setting the price by perceived value rather than cost. For a proposal: ask in discovery what the problem costs the customer, state the estimate with its basis and compare it to the price. Do not claim returns you cannot support. The cost-plus article describes the opposite starting point; use your costs and margin to decide your floor, so that you know where a deal stops making sense.

## Options

Offering two or three options (for example basic, standard, extended) lets the customer choose scope rather than haggle over price. Make differences clear, avoid options that nobody could want and recommend one with a reason.

## Approvals and control

Know your company's discount limits and who approves above them; request approval before sending, not after. Name files with a version and date, send as PDF if your company's practice requires it and keep the sent version in the CRM. Have colleagues check the numbers: totals, quantities and the tax line. Use the template's standard terms and ask legal before changing them.

## Requests for quotation

Wikipedia describes a request for quotation (RfQ) as a business process in which a company or public body asks a supplier for a quote for specific products or services. Respond exactly to the requested format and deadline. First decide bid or no-bid: Can we meet the requirements? Is the deal attractive? Do we know the decision criteria? Ask clarifying questions in the time allowed. Public bodies may follow formal procurement rules; hand such bids to the team that knows them.

## Worked example

A logistics firm lost eight hours a week to manual returns (estimate from discovery, based on their data). You offer Standard at the list price and Extended with training and priority support, and recommend Standard with a reason. A line states assumptions and the three things that are not included. Your approval limit allows ten percent only for a longer term, so you ask your manager before sending. The proposal is saved as version 2 and logged in the CRM.

## Key takeaways

- A proposal repeats the customer's situation, scope, value, price and next steps.
- Tie price to value with marked assumptions; know your floor from cost and margin.
- Offer clear options and recommend one.
- Get approvals before sending and keep versions.
- Answer RfQs exactly and decide on bid or no-bid first.
