## Why this matters

Changes are normal; unmanaged changes are the problem. Change control is a process, formal or informal, that makes sure changes are introduced in a controlled and coordinated way. It lowers the chance that unneeded changes slip in without thought, cause faults or undo other people's work. The aim is not to say no, but to decide knowingly.

## A change process in six steps

One common description of change control uses six steps. Below is our own adaptation for a project:

- Plan and scope: write the request down with owner, reason, benefit, and wanted date.
- Assess: estimate the effect on scope, schedule, cost, risk and quality. Include related deliverables and a way back if the change fails.
- Approve: a named decision body (the lead, a sponsor, or a change board) accepts, rejects or postpones.
- Build and test: implement in a safe environment and check against the acceptance criteria.
- Implement: roll out, with the people affected informed in advance.
- Close: update the plan, budget and documents, and tell everybody.

## Match effort to risk

Assign each change a category such as high, moderate or low risk. A high-risk change needs more steps, such as management approval and stakeholder notification. A low-risk change may need only the lead's approval and brief documentation. This keeps small changes quick and large ones careful.

## Contracts and customers

A formal change request is a documented wish to change the properties of an object. Where a contract between client and contractor exists, a change can only be released jointly, and it then becomes part of the contract. If a customer talks directly to a developer, the agreement should be brought into the process, otherwise the process is bypassed.

## The people side

Change management is the process of designing and implementing change. Approaches differ: a top-down approach lets a small group design the process and instruct others, whereas an approach that involves those affected gives them an active part. A change is also deeper if it challenges basic assumptions than if it refines existing practice. Our own checklist for a change that affects people:

- Who is affected, and how?
- What stops, what starts, what stays?
- What do they need: information, training, time?
- Who can speak for them before the decision?

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. During a CRM rollout the sales director asks for an additional report. Our team logs it as change C-14. Assessment: 6 days of work, one test cycle more, no effect on the go-live date if the optional export is postponed; risk low. The lead approves within a day and records the postponed item. Two sales assistants will receive the report, so the lead invites them to a 30-minute walk-through before release.

## Key takeaways

- Log every change request and assess its effect on scope, schedule, cost, risk and quality.
- Name who decides, and route by risk so that small changes stay quick.
- Agreed changes with customers are decided jointly and recorded.
- Plan how the people affected are informed and involved.
