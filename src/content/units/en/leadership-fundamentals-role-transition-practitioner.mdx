## Why this matters

Once you have settled into the lead role, the question changes from "what is my job?" to "how do I lead different people on different tasks?". This unit introduces situational leadership at concept level and shows how to agree expectations explicitly. It is education, not a prescription.

## Situational leadership at concept level

The Wikipedia article on situational leadership theory describes the idea that effective leaders adapt their style to the situation, with no single style right for all situations. Most such models use two dimensions: task behaviour (how much direction you give) and relationship behaviour (how closely you work with the person). The article names Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard as the creators of the best-known versions and also notes that several studies do not support all of the prescriptions of the theory. So treat it as a thinking aid, not as a law.

A practical reading: the same person may need close direction on a new task and a lot of autonomy on a familiar one. Ask yourself two questions: how well can this person do this task, and how willing and confident are they? Then decide how much to direct and how much to support.

## Agreeing expectations

Unclear expectations are a frequent source of conflict. The German article on Rollenkonflikt introduces the idea that people can face conflicting demands from their role. Reduce this by making expectations explicit:

- Write down, with each person, the main areas of responsibility, the results expected and how you will both notice progress.
- Agree how decisions are made: who decides, who is consulted, who is informed.
- Review the agreement regularly, especially when the team or tasks change.

The OpenStax chapter on managing teams reminds new leaders that they balance the needs of the team with demands from outside the team. Say openly which demands you pass on, which you absorb and which you negotiate.

## Leading former peers

If you lead former colleagues, name the change openly, agree what stays the same (for example social contact) and what changes (for example you now decide on priorities and give feedback). Do not pretend nothing changed.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. Jonas leads a team of five. Lena is new to a reporting task, so Jonas sits with her for the first run, explains the steps and checks the result. Karim has done similar reports for years, so Jonas only agrees the deadline and the quality criteria and asks to be informed about risks. After two months Lena needs less direction and Jonas moves to short check-ins. He also writes a one-page summary of what he expects from each person and reviews it every quarter.

## Key takeaways

- Situational leadership: adapt direction and support to the person and the task; no single best style.
- Treat models as thinking aids; research does not support every prescription.
- Make expectations explicit: responsibilities, results, decision rights, review rhythm.
- When leading former peers, name what changes and what stays the same.
