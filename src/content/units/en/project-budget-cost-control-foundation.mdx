## Why this matters

A project without a budget cannot say "too expensive" until the money is gone. A budget turns the plan into figures that a sponsor can approve and the team can track week by week. In short, a budget is a plan expressed in value terms for a given period that carries a degree of obligation, and budgeting includes checking it later. In managerial accounting, budgets are also a formal way to communicate plans to people who monitor performance, and preparing one forces planners to write down their assumptions.

## Building the budget from the bottom

Start from the work breakdown structure and price each work package. A bottom-up budget is easier to defend than one top-level figure, because every line has an owner who can explain it. Typical cost types are internal effort (days times an internal rate), external services, material and licences, travel and training. Smaller budget units give better control: keep separate budgets for working hours, external services, travel and change requests, and compare each with its plan. Write the assumptions next to the figures (rates, exchange rates, how long a quote is valid), because a budget is only as sound as they are.

## Contingency and baseline (our own)

Estimates are uncertain, so a budget without reserve is usually too low on day one. Our own rules:

- Show contingency as its own line, never hidden inside work packages.
- Name one owner, such as the sponsor or the project lead, who decides on its release.
- Release contingency only against a documented risk or change, not for general overspending.
- Freeze the approved budget as the baseline; later changes go through change requests.

## Tracking and reading variances

Control means comparing plan with actual, finding the deviations, assessing their consequences and proposing corrective action; the German standard DIN 69901 describes project controlling in these terms. A variance is favourable when expenses are lower than budgeted and unfavourable when they are higher. A variance only shows where a problem may exist; it does not prove one, and a favourable variance can mean that costs were cut too far. Our own advice: track committed cost (orders placed) next to invoiced cost, otherwise an overspend shows up late.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A team relaunches an intranet. The work breakdown has four packages: "Content migration" (12 days at an internal rate of 500 EUR = 6 000 EUR), "Search setup" (external service, 4 000 EUR), "Training" (2 000 EUR) and "Testing" (5 days = 2 500 EUR). The sum is 14 500 EUR. A separate contingency line of 1 500 EUR, owned by the sponsor, brings the baseline to 16 000 EUR. In week six, 5 200 EUR have been invoiced for migration against 4 000 EUR planned, and the 4 000 EUR search order is placed but not yet invoiced. The variance note says: migration is 1 200 EUR over plan because the source content was worse than assumed. The sponsor releases 1 200 EUR from contingency against the documented risk "poor source content" and the baseline stays unchanged.

## Key takeaways

- Build the budget bottom-up from work packages and write the assumptions down.
- Show contingency as a separate line with an owner and a release rule.
- Freeze the approved budget as a baseline; changes go through change requests.
- A variance shows where to look, not what is wrong; track commitments as well as invoices.
