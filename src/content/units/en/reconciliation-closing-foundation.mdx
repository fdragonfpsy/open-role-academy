## Why this matters

Reconciliation means making sure that two sets of records agree, usually the balances of two accounts. Mistakes, missing entries and timing gaps creep into any set of books. Reconciling regularly finds them early, before they end up in the financial statements. It also helps to discourage fraud, because unexplained differences stand out.

## Bank reconciliation, step by step

The most common reconciliation compares the bank account in your books with the bank statement.

1. Take the closing balance on the bank statement and the balance of the bank account in the ledger for the same date.
2. Tick off every ledger entry that also appears on the statement.
3. List what is left on each side. These are the **reconciling items**.
4. Decide for each item whether it is a **timing difference** or an **error or missing entry**.
5. Correct what is wrong in your books; leave genuine timing differences listed until they clear.
6. Confirm that the adjusted balances agree, and keep the finished reconciliation as evidence.

Typical timing differences are **outstanding items**: a payment you have posted that the bank has not yet processed, or a deposit you recorded that has not yet reached the statement. They need no correction, because they clear on their own. Typical items that need a booking are bank charges or interest that appear on the statement but not yet in your books.

## Accruals and deferrals

Accrual accounting recognises revenue and expenses when they are earned or incurred, not necessarily when cash moves. At period end, two kinds of adjustment fix the timing:

- **Accrual**: the service has been received (or delivered), but nothing has been paid (or invoiced) yet. Example: December electricity is used but billed in January. You record the expense now.
- **Deferral**: cash has moved, but the benefit belongs to a later period. Example: a rent paid in advance for next year is not an expense of this year. In German bookkeeping such items are called Rechnungsabgrenzungsposten.

Both make sure the expense or revenue lands in the period it belongs to.

## The closing checklist idea

Closing a period is a list of repeatable steps: post all invoices and payments, reconcile bank and other accounts, post accruals, deferrals and depreciation, review the trial balance, and then lock the period. A written checklist makes sure nothing is forgotten and shows who did what. Rules for the annual financial statements differ by country; for Germany the Jahresabschluss is governed by commercial law (HGB), which your company's accountants apply.

## Worked example

Illustration only; the amounts are invented. At month end the bank statement shows 9,700 and the bank account in the ledger shows 9,400. Two items explain the gap: a supplier payment of 500 was posted in the books but has not yet left the bank (outstanding), and bank charges of 200 appear on the statement but not yet in the books.

| Statement side | Amount |
|---|---|
| Statement balance | 9,700 |
| Less: outstanding supplier payment (clears later) | -500 |
| Adjusted bank balance | 9,200 |

| Ledger side | Amount |
|---|---|
| Ledger balance | 9,400 |
| Less: bank charges not yet booked | -200 |
| Adjusted ledger balance | 9,200 |

Both sides show 9,200, so the reconciliation is complete. The outstanding payment needs no entry; the bank charges are booked (debit bank charges expense 200, credit bank 200). If the two sides had still differed, the remaining difference would be investigated, never ignored.

## Key takeaways

- Reconciliation checks that two sets of records agree and finds errors early.
- In a bank reconciliation, sort differences into timing differences and errors or missing entries.
- Accruals record what is owed or earned but not yet settled; deferrals move cash already paid or received to the period it belongs to.
- A closing checklist makes period-end work repeatable and traceable.
- Never leave an unexplained difference unexamined.
