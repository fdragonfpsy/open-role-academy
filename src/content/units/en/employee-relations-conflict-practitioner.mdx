## Why this matters

When a complaint reaches HR, how you handle it matters as much as the result. A fair and traceable procedure protects employees, managers and the company. This unit covers Germany/EU practice at a general level. It contains no legal specifics: deadlines, warning letters, dismissal rules, works council rights and complaint rights depend on current law, collective agreements and company rules. Check them or ask legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice.

## Grievance and complaint procedures

The Grievance (labour) article describes a grievance as a formal complaint by an employee about their treatment at work, often handled through a defined procedure. Many organisations set one out in policies or agreements. A sound procedure usually has these elements (practice insight):

1. **Receive and acknowledge**: confirm the complaint and explain the next steps and timeline.
2. **Clarify**: ask what happened, when, who was involved and what outcome the person wants.
3. **Gather facts**: talk to all relevant people, review documents, stay neutral.
4. **Decide or recommend**: based on facts, within your authority; involve the right decision maker.
5. **Communicate and follow up**: inform the parties as far as allowed and check later that things improved.

The Employee relations article describes this field as managing the relationship between employer and employees, including handling disputes. Fair treatment of both sides is central: give the accused person the chance to respond.

## Fact-finding conversations

Prepare questions. Use open questions, avoid leading ones ("Did he shout at you?" becomes "What happened in the meeting?"). Keep written notes with date, people present and what was said, in neutral language. Notes about individuals are personal data: store them securely, share them only with those who need them and follow your data protection rules.

## Mediation

The Mediation article describes mediation as a process in which a neutral third party helps the parties reach their own agreement. The mediator does not decide. Participation is generally voluntary. Mediation suits conflicts about working relations, communication and task split. It is usually unsuitable where there is a legal violation, a serious power imbalance, threats or violence. The mediator should be neutral: an HR colleague who already took a side or who is the line manager of one party is a poor choice. Internal and external mediators both exist.

## Bullying

The German Mobbing article describes bullying as repeated hostile acts against a person over a longer period. One single argument is typically not bullying. Practice insight: take every report seriously, document facts, protect the reporting person from retaliation, involve the responsible manager and, where the company has one, the complaints office or works council as the company rules say. Do not label it yourself as bullying in a legal sense without proper assessment.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. A team member reports that the team lead repeatedly criticises her in front of others. You thank her, explain the procedure and ask what she wants: the behaviour to stop. You record dates and situations. You then speak with the team lead, using open questions and giving him the chance to respond. You also speak to two colleagues who were present. The facts show harsh public criticism but no pattern against one person. You recommend, together with the responsible manager, a feedback coaching for the team lead and a clear rule to give critical feedback privately. You check back with the employee after four weeks.

## Key takeaways

- A fair procedure: acknowledge, clarify, gather facts, decide within your authority, follow up.
- Hear all sides; avoid leading questions; document neutrally and protect the data.
- Mediation is voluntary, led by a neutral person who does not decide.
- Mediation is unsuitable for serious violations or severe power imbalance.
- Legal details: check current law, your collective agreement or legal counsel.
