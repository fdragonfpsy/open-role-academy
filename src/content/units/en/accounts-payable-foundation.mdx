## Why this matters

Accounts payable (AP) is money a business owes to its suppliers; it appears as a liability on the balance sheet. The AP team makes sure that every supplier invoice is checked, approved, recorded and paid correctly and on time. Pay too early and the company loses cash it could use; pay too late and suppliers get annoyed or charge interest; pay something wrong and the money is hard to get back.

## The flow from invoice to payment

A typical purchase runs like this: a department needs something, a purchase order is sent to the supplier, the goods or services arrive and are checked, the supplier sends an invoice, the invoice is routed for approval, it is recorded in the books and finally paid. AP owns the middle and end of this chain. In an ERP or accounting system each supplier has a **supplier master record** (German: Kreditorenstammsatz) holding the name, address and bank details, so that invoices are always matched to the right supplier.

## What an invoice contains

An invoice is a commercial document from a seller to a buyer. It lists goods or services, usually with quantities, prices and the terms of sale, and often the payment terms. Many countries also require specific details on an invoice, for example for VAT. These requirements differ by country, so: invoices must contain legally required details; check your country's rules and your company's guidelines. A document is not an invoice just because it is called one: what counts is that it bills a delivery or service.

## Two checks: formal and factual

- **Formal check**: Is the invoice complete and correct on its face? Is it addressed to our company, is the supplier identified, are date, invoice number, description, amounts and tax shown, and are the required details present?
- **Factual check** (German: sachliche Prüfung): Is the content right? Did we order this, did we receive it, does the price match the agreement, and is the quantity correct?

Both are needed. A perfectly formatted invoice for something you never received is still not payable.

## Coding and due dates

**Coding** (German: Kontierung) means deciding which accounts the invoice is booked to, for example which expense account and which cost centre, according to the chart of accounts. The invoice is recorded as an open liability to the supplier. The **due date** comes from the payment terms on the invoice or the contract. Missing it can cause reminders and, depending on the country, late-payment interest. In Germany, for example, the statutory rules on default for payment claims are in the Civil Code (BGB); a general rule of thumb is not enough, so follow your company's payment policy.

## Worked example

Illustration only; the amounts are invented. A company receives an invoice for printer paper: net 100, VAT 19 (a rate chosen for the example; use the real rate for your case), gross 119. Recording the invoice:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Office supplies expense | 100 | |
| Input VAT (receivable from tax authority) | 19 | |
| Accounts payable (supplier) | | 119 |

When the invoice is paid from the bank:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts payable (supplier) | 119 | |
| Bank | | 119 |

The liability is cleared and the cash leaves the bank account. Input VAT handling depends on the country and the company's tax status.

## Key takeaways

- Accounts payable is money owed to suppliers and is a liability on the balance sheet.
- The flow: order, goods receipt, invoice, approval, recording, payment.
- Formal check: is the invoice complete? Factual check: is it right, ordered and received?
- Legally required invoice details depend on the country; check the local rules.
- Coding assigns the invoice to accounts; the due date comes from the payment terms.
