## Why this matters

A register of fifty unranked risks is a list of worries. Prioritising shows where the next hour of effort pays off most, and it gives the sponsor a short list to act on.

## Rating probability and impact

A risk matrix sets the probability of occurrence against the extent of damage in two dimensions. It is a standard tool for estimating, evaluating and ranking risks, and the position of a risk shows how urgent a response is. Rows and columns can be labelled with an index (1, 2, 3) or with descriptions, and cells are often coloured like a traffic light. Our own example scale uses 1 to 5 for both axes with written anchors, for instance impact 1 = less than one day of delay and impact 5 = launch date missed, probability 1 = very unlikely and 5 = very likely. Agree the anchors in your team; the words matter more than the numbers.

## Scores and their limits

Multiplying probability by impact gives an expected-loss style score. The factors can offset each other and lead to a false judgement: a rare but severe risk and a frequent but trivial one can share the same score. Some practitioners therefore weight impact more strongly, for example by multiplying and then adding the impact score again. Our own rule: any risk with the maximum impact is discussed, whatever its score.

## Seeing the risks

People perceive the same risk differently or not at all, and selective perception hides some risks. Collect risks in a group with different roles, and combine data such as history and checklists with expert opinion. A register can also lead to ritual decisions and an illusion of control. Used with common sense, it stimulates debate across functions.

## Risk review (our own)

In every regular project meeting, spend ten minutes on the following:

- Re-score the top five risks.
- Check the triggers: has any of them occurred?
- Close or add risks, and make sure each open risk has an owner and a next action.
- Move risks that occurred to the issue list.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. A team introduces a new HR system and rates four risks on 1 to 5 scales. R1 "Because the lead developer may leave, work may stall": probability 3, impact 4, score 12. R2 "Because legacy data is messy, the migration may contain errors": probability 4, impact 3, score 12. R3 "Because the vendor may deliver the licence late, setup starts late": probability 2, impact 3, score 6. R4 "Because data protection sign-off is missing, go-live may be blocked": probability 1, impact 5, score 5. R1 and R2 tie at 12. R4 has the lowest score but the maximum impact, so the team discusses it anyway and decides to request the sign-off early. For R2 the response is a trial migration with a sample of records.

## Key takeaways

- Rate probability and impact on written scales that the team has agreed.
- A score ranks risks but can hide rare, severe ones; discuss every maximum-impact risk.
- Collect risks with several roles and some data, because perception is selective.
- Review the top risks in every regular meeting; an unreviewed register is only a ritual.
