## Why this matters
At practitioner level you shape everyday HR processes: forms, onboarding checklists, absence handling, personnel records. This unit covers the concepts behind those decisions and contains no deadlines, retention periods, article or section numbers, thresholds or fine amounts, because they depend on current law and the case. Check current law, your data protection officer (DPO) or legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice; this is not a substitute for company policy. Context: EU and Germany.

## Legal bases and purpose limitation

The English GDPR article explains that personal data may not be processed unless there is at least one legal basis. Kinds of basis include consent, performing a contract with the person, a legal obligation of the controller and legitimate interests unless the person's rights override them. The German article on employee data protection explains that the regulation leaves room for national rules and collective agreements on employee data, and that in Germany national law applies in addition. It also notes that applicants count as employees here and that, with consent, the dependency in employment matters for judging whether it is truly voluntary. So the lesson is not "get consent for everything"; it is "know which basis your process relies on, and ask your DPO if you cannot name it".

Purpose limitation follows: data collected for payroll should not quietly be reused for another purpose, such as ranking people for promotion, unless that has been assessed.

## Data minimisation in forms and processes
Review every field in a form with one question: do we need this for the stated purpose? Remove "nice to have for later" fields, collect information only at the stage where it is needed (bank details are not needed at application stage), mark optional fields clearly and keep free text factual. Fewer data, fewer risks.

## Special categories

The German article on personal data names special categories as particularly sensitive: among them data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic and biometric data for unique identification, health data, and data on sex life or sexual orientation. It states their processing is tied to stricter conditions and generally prohibited unless specific exceptions apply. In HR, health data (sick notes, diagnoses, disability information) is the most common case. Practical rule: collect only what a documented process requires, restrict access to those who need it, and ask your DPO before any new use.

## Data subject requests: access, correction, erasure

The GDPR article describes the right of access (a copy of the data and information about how it is processed), rectification and erasure as rights of the data subject. When an employee or applicant asks for their data, to correct it or to delete it, do not decide alone and do not ignore it. Route it immediately to the DPO or the named process owner and note when it arrived. Whether and how a request must be fulfilled, and by when, depends on current law and the case. Do not promise anything before the DPO has responded.

## Retention and deletion concept
The German article on retention periods explains that retention periods come from various rules. At concept level: keep records only as long as there is a documented reason, then delete or archive them according to a deletion concept that lists record types, owners and the rule behind each. Do not invent periods yourself; take them from the approved concept.

## Secure personnel files
Apply need-to-know: role-based access, no files on private devices or private messaging channels, paper files locked away. The access control article describes restricting who may view or use resources; apply it to HR folders and systems, and review access when someone changes role or leaves.

## Worked example
Illustration only; no real data. A former employee asks what data you hold on them and wants a wrong job title corrected. Step 1: do not answer on substance. Step 2: log the date of receipt and forward the request to the DPO the same day. Step 3: list where HR holds the person's data, without sending anything out. Step 4: once a correction is confirmed, fix the source record and note the change. Step 5: tell the person only what the DPO has agreed.

## Key takeaways

- Every HR process needs an identifiable legal basis; know which one and ask the DPO if unsure.
- Purpose limitation: do not reuse data for new purposes without assessment.
- Minimise: question every field and collect data only when needed.
- Special categories, especially health data, need extra care and restricted access.
- Route access, correction and erasure requests to the DPO immediately; promise nothing.
- Keep and delete data following the approved concept; secure files with need-to-know access.
- Check current law, your DPO or legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice.
