## Why this matters

Every team spends money, and someone has to say in advance how much. A **budget** is described as a calculation plan, usually but not always financial, for a defined period, often one year or a month. It can include anticipated sales volumes and revenues, costs and expenses, assets, liabilities and cash flows. Organisations use budgets to express plans in measurable terms, to set priorities and to check later whether objectives were reached. As a finance beginner you will often prepare, update and compare these numbers.

## Budget versus forecast

The two words are often mixed up. According to the Forecasting article, a forecast is not to be confused with a budget: budgets are more specific, fixed-term financial plans used for resource allocation and control, while forecasts estimate future financial performance and allow flexibility as circumstances change. A simple way to remember it: the budget is the plan you agreed on; the forecast is your latest best guess of what will really happen. Forecasts can later be compared with actual outcomes, and good practice is to say how uncertain they are.

## Income and expense lines

A budget is built from lines. **Income lines** show expected money or revenue coming in, for example sales or fees. **Expense lines** show expected costs, for example salaries, rent, materials, software and travel. If expenses exceed income the budget shows a deficit; if income exceeds expenses it shows a surplus. Lines usually follow the structure of your income statement and chart of accounts, so that budget and actual figures can be compared line by line.

## Plan versus actual

After the period, you compare the **plan** (budget) with the **actual** figures from the books. The difference is called a **variance**: the difference between a budgeted or planned amount and the actual amount incurred or earned. It can be computed for both costs and revenues. A variance is called favourable when the actual result is better than planned, and adverse or unfavourable when it is worse. The variance is only the starting point: the next question is always why it happened.

## A simple budget from last year's figures

A common first approach is to start from last year's actual figures and adjust each line for what you expect to change. Write down every adjustment as an **assumption** (for example "salaries rise by a stated percentage"), so that others can check and update them. One caution: copying last year only repeats last year, so ask whether each line is still needed.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts and percentages are invented. A small team had these actuals last year and sets simple assumptions for next year.

| Line | Last year (actual) | Assumption | Next year (budget) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 120,000 | +5% | 126,000 |
| Salaries | 60,000 | +3% | 61,800 |
| Materials | 30,000 | +5% (moves with revenue) | 31,500 |
| Rent | 12,000 | unchanged | 12,000 |
| Other costs | 6,000 | unchanged | 6,000 |
| **Total expenses** | 108,000 | | 111,300 |
| **Surplus** | 12,000 | | 14,700 |

Next year, if actual revenue turns out to be 123,000, the revenue variance is 123,000 - 126,000 = -3,000, which is unfavourable because less came in than planned. You would then look for the reason and update your forecast.

## Key takeaways

- A budget is a fixed-term plan; a forecast is a flexible, regularly updated estimate.
- Budgets are built from income lines and expense lines for a defined period.
- A variance is the difference between plan and actual; favourable means better than planned.
- Start from last year's actuals, but write every adjustment down as an assumption.
- A variance is a question, not an answer: always ask why.
