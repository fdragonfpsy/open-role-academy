## Why this matters

At practitioner level you do not only keep a pipeline, you make it trustworthy. A pipeline where deals sit in "negotiation" because the seller hopes so is useless for planning. This unit shows how to tie stages to evidence and how to turn a sales goal into a weekly activity plan. The figures below are invented for illustration; use your own history.

## Exit criteria instead of feelings

A deal moves to the next stage when a **criterion is met that the customer can confirm**. Examples (practice insight, adapt to your product):

- Qualified: need, decision maker and rough timeline are known and written down.
- Proposal sent: the customer has described their requirements and agreed to receive an offer.
- Negotiation: the customer has reacted to the offer in substance, not just "received".
- Won: order or signed contract received.

Writing criteria down makes forecasts comparable between people and shows where deals leak.

## Fit with the buyer's side

OpenStax describes the B2B buying process in eight stages, from problem recognition through need description, specification, supplier search, proposal solicitation, supplier selection and order specification to performance review, and notes that a buying centre of several people takes part. Your stages should map to this: if the buyer is still writing a specification, an offer is premature. Sales process engineering literature also points out that a defined process reduces risk for both sides and standardises customer contact.

## Planning backwards

Start from the goal and divide by your own conversion rates. Illustration: goal 60,000 EUR of new revenue in a quarter, average deal 5,000 EUR, so 12 won deals. If about one in four proposals is won, you need about 48 proposals; if about one in three qualified conversations leads to a proposal, you need about 144 qualified conversations. Then ask whether that is feasible in the time you have. If not, raise the conversion (better qualification) or revise the goal, rather than hoping.

## Stalled deals

Look for deals without activity for longer than the typical time in that stage. Options: re-qualify (is the need still real?), find a new stakeholder, agree a clear decision date, or close the deal as lost and free your time. Closing honestly improves your data.

## Worked example

You find five deals in "proposal sent" for over 30 days while the typical stay is 10 days. You call each contact with the same question: "What is the next step on your side, and by when?" Two give a date, one says priorities changed (you close it with a reason code), two do not respond (you send one final message and then set the status to lost). Your pipeline shrinks, but the forecast becomes honest.

## Key takeaways

- Stages need exit criteria that the customer can confirm.
- Map your stages to the buyer's decision steps and the people involved.
- Plan backwards: goal, deal size, conversion rates, required activity.
- Stalled deals get a decision date or are closed with a reason.
