## Why this matters

As a lead you decide which deviations from the standard are acceptable and when counsel must be involved. A contract is a risk-allocation tool, and your job is to make that allocation visible. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Risk allocation

Every contract distributes risks between the parties: who bears delay, defects, data incidents, price changes or the failure of a subcontractor. Statutes provide a default allocation for each contract type; the contract may change it within the limits of mandatory law. When you review, compare the draft with your own standard terms and with the default: where does it shift risk away from you, by how much and what do you get in return? For standard terms, remember the content control described in the AGB article. For framework agreements, see the German article on Rahmenvertrag: the framework sets the conditions, and each individual order creates specific duties. Check that the two layers do not contradict each other.

## Cross-border questions

When parties sit in different countries, two questions arise: which law governs the contract and which court decides. EU rules exist on both, and parties can often choose, but not always and not without limits, especially with consumers. Do not decide this alone: list the countries involved and ask counsel.

## Negotiation positions

Prepare a short playbook for each clause family:

- **Must-have:** a position without which you do not sign.
- **Fall-back:** an acceptable alternative with a condition.
- **Nice-to-have:** may be traded.
- **Escalation:** who decides, and by when.

Record each concession with its reason so the next negotiation can build on it.

## Template governance

Templates age. Give each template an owner, a version, a review date and a change log. Link clauses to the sources they rely on and to the playbook entry. When statutes change, the owner reviews the template; when a deviation is approved twice for the same reason, consider changing the standard.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company and clause. A customer demands that your company accept unlimited liability for data incidents. The playbook lists a capped liability as must-have and a higher cap with insurance proof as fall-back. You offer the fall-back, document the reason for the customer's request and send the case to the escalation owner because the demand goes beyond the fall-back.

## Key takeaways

- Read a contract as a risk-allocation tool and compare it with default rules and your standard.
- Framework and individual orders must be consistent; cross-border contracts need counsel on governing law and jurisdiction.
- A playbook with must-have, fall-back, nice-to-have and escalation makes negotiations consistent.
- Templates need owner, version, review date and change log.
- This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.
