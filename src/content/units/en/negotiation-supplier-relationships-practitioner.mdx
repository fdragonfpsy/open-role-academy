## Why this matters

Practitioners negotiate regularly with suppliers they will meet again. The goal is not to win each round but to reach agreements that hold and leave the relationship usable.

## Four principles in practice

The Harvard approach has four conditions: treat people and the problem separately, focus on interests instead of positions, develop options, and insist on objective criteria such as legal rules or ethical norms. In purchasing, objective criteria can be published price indexes, benchmark quotes or a written specification. If the other side applies pressure or uses tricks, name it calmly, and interrupt the negotiation if necessary, until the discussion is factual again. For absurd demands, the approach suggests accepting hypothetically and spelling out the unacceptable consequences, asking for the other side's advice, or bringing in an independent third party.

## Trading concessions (our own rules)

- Never give a concession for free; say "if you..., then we...".
- Bundle terms into packages (price, volume, payment period, duration) rather than negotiating them one at a time.
- Give small concessions slower and smaller than the other side expects, and keep a list of what has been exchanged.
- Record agreements in writing right after the meeting.

## Power and influence

Influence tactics include promises, threats and questions. Their effect depends on relative power and on accepted norms. If a supplier is the only available source of a critical component, the threat to switch to another supplier is not credible and will hardly lower the price. In a culture where threats are unacceptable, they can have the opposite effect. Estimate your own and the supplier's alternatives honestly before you choose a tactic.

## Valuing payment terms

A cash discount (Skonto) is a price reduction for paying within a set period; in principle it passes an interest advantage from the supplier to the customer. A typical wording is "14 days 2 percent discount, 30 days net". Paying on day 14 instead of day 30 earns 2 percent for 16 days. Our own simple calculation: 2 / 98 x 365 / 16 is about 47 percent per year, which is higher than usual borrowing costs. Compare offers in money at the same date, and note that the discount base must be defined in the terms.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures and an assumed interest rate of 6 percent per year. Offer A: a 3 percent price cut, payment after 30 days. Offer B: the full price of 100,000 euros with 2 percent discount if paid within 14 days. Offer A costs 97,000 euros on day 30. Offer B costs 98,000 euros on day 14; bringing that payment forward by 16 days costs about 258 euros of interest (98,000 x 6 percent x 16 / 365), so it is worth about 98,258 euros on day 30. Offer A is cheaper by about 1,258 euros. The buyer takes A, and asks for the discount terms on top.

## Key takeaways

- Use the four Harvard principles: people and problem apart, interests, options, objective criteria.
- Trade concessions conditionally and in packages; write agreements down.
- Check your power honestly; a threat without an alternative backfires.
- Compare terms in money at the same date, for instance by converting a cash discount to a yearly rate.
