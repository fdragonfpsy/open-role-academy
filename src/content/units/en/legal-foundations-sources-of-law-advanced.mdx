## Why this matters

As a lead you review other people's assessments and decide what goes to external counsel. You need to recognise a sound argument, a missing source and a conflict between rules. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Resolving conflicts between rules

Beyond the vertical ranking you already know, legal theory uses general principles for collisions. Concept level only, since their application is a legal question:

- A higher-ranking rule generally prevails over a lower one.
- A more specific rule generally prevails over a more general one on the same topic.
- A later rule generally prevails over an earlier one of the same rank.

Real cases often combine several principles and need interpretation, so state which principle you rely on and why, and mark where it is uncertain.

## The role of case law

The English article on case law describes legal systems in which earlier court decisions can bind later courts (precedent). Germany belongs to the civil-law tradition: statutes are the central source, and judgments formally decide the case before the court. In practice, however, higher-court interpretations strongly shape how statutes are read, and companies plan with them. For EU law, the European courts interpret EU acts, and national courts follow that interpretation. Do not cite decisions from memory: look up the court, date and reference in an official database and record them.

## Structuring a legal assessment

A reviewable assessment contains:

1. **Question** in one sentence.
2. **Facts** that you know, and facts you assume (marked).
3. **Sources** by level (EU, federal, state, contract, internal policy) with link and date of access.
4. **Analysis**: conditions of each norm checked against the facts, conflicts between norms and how you resolved them.
5. **Result and confidence**, including open questions.
6. **Next step**: decision, further research or escalation.

## When to escalate

Escalate to counsel when the question involves unclear conditions, a conflict between EU and national rules, high exposure for the company, regulators or courts, or when no current official source can be found. Escalating early is a sign of quality, not weakness.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented rules and company. A colleague writes an assessment: "Rule A (general, older) and rule B (specific, newer) both cover our case. We follow B." You check: is the principle named? Is the date of each rule given? Does B really cover the facts, or only part of them? Is the source an official text or a blog? You send it back with three questions and ask for the facts to be separated from assumptions, then decide whether the exposure justifies external counsel.

## Key takeaways

- Rank, specificity and date are general tools for resolving rule conflicts; state which one you use.
- Germany is statute-centred; court interpretations still shape practice. Cite decisions only from official sources.
- A good assessment separates facts, sources, analysis, result and open questions.
- Escalate early when conditions are unclear or exposure is high.
