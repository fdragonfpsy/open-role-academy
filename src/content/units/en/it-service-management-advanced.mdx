## From running the desk to shaping the service

At advanced level you are responsible for how IT services are designed and improved — for a service desk, an operations team or a set of services. You balance user expectations, cost and risk, and you represent IT towards business departments.

## Frameworks as references, not recipes

Several frameworks and standards describe good ITSM practice. **ITIL** is the most widely used framework of ITSM practices; its trademark is owned by PeopleCert. **ISO/IEC 20000** is the international standard for IT service management against which organisations can be certified. Others include COBIT and FitSM. Use them as references: pick the practices that address your real problems and scale them to your organisation. Copying every process unchanged creates bureaucracy without benefit.

## Designing SLAs

A good SLA reflects the **business impact** of a service. Payroll may need high availability on a few days a month; a public web shop around the clock. Targets must be **measurable**, **achievable** with the available resources and **affordable** — every extra "nine" of availability costs money. Agree on reporting, regular reviews and what happens when targets are missed. Vague wording or unrealistic targets both damage trust.

## Major incidents

When a critical service fails for many users, normal ticket handling is not enough. Good **major-incident handling** includes:

- a clear **incident lead** who coordinates;
- **regular status updates** to users and management, even if there is little news;
- a strict focus on **restoring service** first;
- a **post-incident review** afterwards: why did it happen despite existing controls, and what will prevent a recurrence? Keep it blameless so that people share what they know.

## Continual improvement

Improvement works best in small, measured cycles: review data such as top incident categories, SLA trends and user feedback; choose a few improvements; implement them; measure the effect. Celebrate removed work — every incident that no longer happens is time won.

## Worked example

Jonas runs IT operations for 600 employees. The top incident category is "password locked". He introduces self-service password reset with multi-factor authentication. Three months later, these tickets have dropped by two thirds, freeing the service desk for complex cases. He reports the result to the business departments together with the updated SLA report.

## Key takeaways

- ITIL and ISO/IEC 20000 are references — adapt, do not copy.
- SLAs reflect business impact and must be measurable and affordable.
- Major incidents: one lead, regular updates, restore first, blameless review.
- Improve in small cycles based on data.
