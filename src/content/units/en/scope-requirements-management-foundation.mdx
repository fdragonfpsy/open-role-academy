## Why this matters

Most disagreements about a finished project start with a sentence like "I thought that was included". Scope is the shared answer to the question "what exactly are we doing, and what not?". Writing it down early is cheap; arguing about it late is not.

## What scope means

Scope is the defined set of features and functions of a product, or the work needed to finish a project. Project scope is oriented towards the work and methods required, product scope more towards functional requirements. Anything outside the defined scope is "out of scope" and is excluded to keep the project focused. If requirements are not completely described and nobody controls changes, scope can creep, that is, grow without a decision.

## Requirement, not solution

A requirement says what is needed and why; a solution says how. A customer's requirements document is traditionally about what something is made for and what it must achieve, while the supplier's answer describes how and with what it will be solved. Our own habit: if a sentence names a tool, ask what need lies behind it. "We need a new app" is a solution; "customers must be able to check their order status without calling us" is a requirement.

## A simple scope statement (our own template)

Our own template has five parts:

- Purpose: one sentence linking to the goal.
- Deliverables: the things that will exist at the end.
- In scope: what the team will do.
- Out of scope: what people might expect but will not get.
- Acceptance: how the sponsor will check that a deliverable is done.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A team plans an online check-in for visitors. Purpose: reduce waiting time at the reception desk. Deliverables: a web form and a printed visitor badge. In scope: the form in German and English, an email confirmation. Out of scope: integration with the building access system, a mobile app. Acceptance: a visitor can register in under two minutes in a test with five colleagues. When the facility manager later asks for door access, the project lead points to the out-of-scope list and offers to collect it as a follow-up request.

## Key takeaways

- Scope states what you will do and what you will not do.
- Distinguish the work (project scope) from the product (product scope).
- Write requirements as needs, not as tools.
- An out-of-scope list prevents silent growth; new wishes become requests.
