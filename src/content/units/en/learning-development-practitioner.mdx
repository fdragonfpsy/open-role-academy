## Why this matters

At practitioner level you no longer just organise courses. You work out what is really needed, design a fitting measure and show whether it worked. This unit uses a Germany/EU setting for context but contains no legal specifics: training leave, funding, works council rights and employee data rules must be checked against current law and your agreements.

## Analyse needs on three levels

The Needs assessment article describes a systematic way of finding gaps before choosing a solution. A common practice is to look at three levels (practice insight):

- **Organisation**: strategy, new products, regulations or tools that change what people must be able to do.
- **Task**: what the job requires, for example from a job description or process documentation.
- **Person**: who currently lacks which knowledge or skill, based on observation, feedback, appraisals and conversations.

Use more than one source. A single request from a manager is a hint, not an analysis. Ask: what should people do differently, and what stops them? If the cause is a missing tool or unclear responsibility, training will not help.

## Write observable learning objectives

The Instructional design article describes planning learning experiences around intended outcomes. Practice insight: write objectives as what learners will be able to do, using verbs you can observe: "create a monthly report with the new template", not "understand reporting". Observable objectives also tell you how to test and evaluate.

## Choose format and tools

Match the format to the objective (practice insight). Practical skills need practice and feedback, so on-the-job learning, workshops or mentoring fit well. Pure information, such as a new policy, may suit short e-learning. Consider group size, locations, time and budget. A learning management system helps with enrolment, content delivery and records, as the LMS article describes. Records of individual learning are personal data: involve the data protection officer and, where applicable, the works council before introducing tracking.

## Plan the evaluation

A widely used model (Kirkpatrick) separates four levels: **reaction** (did participants find it useful?), **learning** (did they gain knowledge or skill?), **behaviour** (do they apply it at work?) and **results** (does the team or organisation benefit?). The German article on Bildungscontrolling discusses why checking education measures is hard and why participant feedback alone says little about quality. Decide before the measure which level you will check and how, for example with a short quiz, a manager check-in after six weeks or an existing team indicator.

## Development plans

In career conversations, link the person's interests with the organisation's needs. Agree one or two concrete steps, a timeframe and who supports (manager, mentor, HR). Review progress at the next check-in.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. A customer service team receives many complaints about wrong invoice explanations. Needs analysis: complaint tickets, a conversation with the team lead and two agents, and the process description. Finding: agents know the invoice rules but the new invoice layout is confusing and there is no quick guide. Objective: "Agents can explain the three invoice sections to customers without asking a colleague." Format: a one-hour practice workshop with real examples plus a one-page guide. Evaluation: a short role-play at the end (learning), a check by the team lead after six weeks (behaviour) and the share of related complaints (results).

## Key takeaways

- Analyse needs on organisation, task and person level, using several sources.
- Write objectives as observable behaviour; they drive format and evaluation.
- Choose formats by objective, audience and budget, not by habit.
- Plan evaluation before the measure: reaction, learning, behaviour, results.
- Learning records are personal data; check data protection and works agreements.
