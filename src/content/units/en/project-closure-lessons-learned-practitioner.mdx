## Why this matters

Most teams hold a closing meeting; far fewer change anything afterwards. Writing about project practice names the same failure patterns: the same person repeats a mistake in the next project, a problem solved in one project happens again in another because nobody passed the information on, and lessons are forgotten once the team moves to the next phase. A review only pays off if its results reach the next project. That is a design question you can plan for.

## An observation is not yet a lesson

"The vendor was late" is an observation. A lesson generalises from the specific case to something others can act on. A space-agency definition asks that a lesson be significant (it has a real or assumed impact), valid (factually and technically correct) and applicable (it points to a specific process, design or decision that reduces the chance of a problem or reinforces a good result). The evaluation community likewise describes lessons as generalisations that move from the specific circumstances to broader situations. Test each candidate against three questions:

- Is it significant: would it change time, cost, quality or risk in a future project?
- Is it valid: do we know the cause, or are we guessing?
- Is it applicable: can someone name the action to take, and when?

## Running the review

An after-action review compares intended with actual results, then names what to sustain and what to improve. It looks forward, and assigning blame is contrary to its purpose. It is normally led by a facilitator, ideally someone who did not take part in the work. It can be long and chronological or short and focused on a few issues. Our own agenda for a 90-minute review:

- Goal and plan: restate what was intended, with the baseline figures.
- Results: show what actually happened, using data from the closure analysis.
- Causes: ask "why" for the three largest differences, positive ones included.
- Lessons: write each as an applicable statement and test it against the three questions.
- Actions: assign an owner and a date to each lesson.

## From lesson to action

Some experience is delicate. Critical remarks about working relationships are valuable for follow-up projects but rarely reach a company-wide database. Decide before the session what is recorded openly, and what stays within the team or goes to the lead only. Store lessons where the next project will look: in the project template, the risk checklist or the estimating guide, not in a forgotten folder. One German source suggests making it mandatory to consult earlier lessons at the start of a new project, and we agree.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. A 14-week ERP rollout finishes 3 weeks late. In the review, the team traces the delay to interface tests that began only after the data migration. Raw observation: "testing started late". Lesson, after the three questions: "Plan interface tests at the start of the schedule, because they depend on the vendor's test system, and book it 6 weeks ahead." Action: the lead adds a line to the organisation's planning template by the end of the month. The closing report lists results against plan, deviations with causes, three open items with owners, and this lesson.

## Key takeaways

- Turn observations into lessons that are significant, valid and applicable.
- Compare intended and actual results, ask why, and keep the review free of blame.
- Give every lesson an owner, a date and a place where the next project will find it.
- Decide up front which delicate remarks are shared openly and which are not.
