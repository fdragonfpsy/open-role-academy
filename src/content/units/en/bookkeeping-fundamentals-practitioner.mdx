## Why this matters

Once you can write single entries, the next task is to keep thousands of them consistent. That needs a shared structure for accounts, a clear flow from first record to report, and a habit of checking. It also needs a clean way to fix mistakes.

## Chart of accounts

The **chart of accounts** is the list of all accounts a company uses, usually grouped by type such as assets, liabilities, equity, revenue and expenses, each with a number. It is the vocabulary of the books: if two colleagues book the same purchase to different accounts, reports become unreliable. Companies tailor their chart to their business. In **Germany**, standard charts are widespread; the German Wikipedia article on the Kontenrahmen names SKR03 and SKR04 as examples that are often used directly. Other countries have their own conventions, so check which chart your company uses before booking.

## Journal, ledger, trial balance

The **journal** lists entries in the order they happen, with date, accounts, amounts and a reference to the receipt. The **general ledger** (German: Hauptbuch) collects the same entries by account, so you can see everything that happened on "Bank" or "Sales revenue". At the end of a period you can list the balance of every ledger account in a **trial balance**, with a debit column and a credit column. If all entries balanced, the two column totals are equal, and financial statements can then be prepared from the same balances.

A trial balance proves only that debits equal credits. It does **not** catch an entry booked to the wrong account, an entry made twice, or an entry missing on both sides. Treat it as a first check, not as proof of correctness.

## Accrual basis and cash basis

On a **cash basis**, income and expenses count when money moves. On an **accrual basis**, they count when they are earned or incurred, even if payment comes earlier or later. A sale invoiced in December and paid in January belongs to December on the accrual basis. Which basis applies depends on company type and national rules, so confirm it for your company.

## VAT as a concept (EU)

In the EU, businesses generally charge value added tax on sales and may reclaim it on purchases, within national rules. In the books this means a purchase is split into the net amount and the VAT amount, and VAT is usually held in separate accounts rather than in expense or revenue. The rates and special cases are set by each member state, so look them up in your country's rules; this course does not state rates.

## Correcting entries

Do not delete or overwrite a posted entry. Instead, post a **correcting entry** that reverses the wrong one and a new entry that records the right one, each with a reference and an explanation. This keeps the history visible. In Germany, orderly bookkeeping also requires that changes remain traceable, see the advanced unit.

## Worked example

Illustration with invented amounts. A cost of 80 for travel was booked to "Office supplies expense" instead of "Travel expense".

| Step | Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original (wrong) | Office supplies expense | 80 | |
| | Bank | | 80 |
| Correction | Travel expense | 80 | |
| | Office supplies expense | | 80 |

The trial balance was balanced before and after, so it would never have shown this error. Only a review of the account or the receipt would.

## Key takeaways

- A consistent chart of accounts makes books comparable; in Germany SKR03 and SKR04 are common examples.
- Journal records in time order, ledger by account, trial balance checks that debits equal credits.
- A balanced trial balance does not prove the entries are right.
- Accrual basis follows when something is earned or incurred, cash basis follows payment.
- Never erase a posted entry; reverse and re-book with an explanation.
