## Why this matters

With an angry or confused customer there is rarely a single correct reply. You weigh defensible options: calm first or facts first, short or thorough, channel switch or stay. A clear way of thinking makes those choices explainable.

## Choose the side you answer first

In Schulz von Thun's model a message has fact, self-revelation, relationship and appeal sides, and misunderstandings arise when sides are weighted differently. A practical rule, our own: if the relationship or self-revelation side dominates (the customer feels ignored or blamed), acknowledge that first in one sentence, then move to facts and the appeal. If you start with facts, the reply may be correct and still be heard as dismissive. If the fact side dominates (a plain how-to question), do not add emotional language that was not asked for.

## Noise and missing cues

OpenStax treats noise as everything that interferes with the receiver getting, decoding and answering the message. It also notes that face-to-face selling gives the richest feedback because voice and body language are visible. Chat and email lack these cues, so make feedback explicit. Reduce noise by putting the answer first, using short paragraphs, numbering steps and removing standard phrases that bury the point. On the phone, slow down when the line or the customer's emotion is unstable.

## Checking decoding

Decoding is the receiver's understanding of what was sent, shaped by their knowledge and experience. Ask a check question that requires a substantive answer: "Which message do you see after step 3?" rather than "Is that clear?". When a customer repeats a mistake, assume your wording was decoded differently and rephrase before you repeat.

## Closing cleanly

Paraphrasing returns the content of what you heard, and a good close does it once more: what was agreed, what you do, what the customer does, by when, and how they can reach you. If the outcome is not what the customer wanted, say so plainly and name the alternatives. Do not close with a promise you cannot influence, because OpenStax's communication gap shows what happens when promise and delivery drift apart.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A customer chat: "Your update ruined my whole report. You people don't test anything." The agent's first reply: "That sounds frustrating, losing report work costs time. Let's get it back: which version of the report do you see now?" The customer answers the question, the tone cools. At the end: "To confirm: I restored yesterday's version, you check the totals, and I will message you at 3 pm tomorrow either way."

## Key takeaways

- Decide which side of the message to answer first; acknowledge before you explain when the relationship side is loud.
- In text channels, make feedback explicit and reduce noise by structure.
- Check understanding with questions that need a real answer.
- Close with agreed facts, actions, times and an honest note on what you cannot do.
