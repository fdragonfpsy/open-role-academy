## Why this matters

Saying "revenue is 3,200 below budget" starts a discussion; saying "prices were higher than planned but volume fell short" moves it forward. Splitting a variance into its drivers tells the business where to act. This unit shows the standard price/volume split, the use of different comparison bases and a simple way to find causes.

## Price and volume effects

For a revenue line with budget price P0, budget volume Q0, actual price P1 and actual volume Q1, the total variance is P1 x Q1 - P0 x Q0. A common split is:

- **Price effect** = (P1 - P0) x Q1
- **Volume effect** = (Q1 - Q0) x P0

The two add up exactly to the total variance. Other conventions exist (for example, valuing the price effect at budget volume and leaving a joint effect). The order of the calculation changes how the effects are shown, so fix one convention, write it down and apply it consistently. The same logic works for cost lines, using unit cost and quantity.

## Which comparison answers which question

- **Actual vs budget** shows performance against the commitment made at the start of the year.
- **Actual vs forecast** shows how well the latest expectation held; useful for short-term steering.
- **Actual vs prior year** shows development over time, but the prior year may itself have been unusual.

State clearly which base you use. Timing differences between months often reverse later, so also look at the year-to-date figure.

## Flexible budget idea

When volume differs from plan, a fixed budget is a poor yardstick for variable costs. A **flexible budget** restates the budget for the actual volume. Comparing actual cost with the flexible budget isolates efficiency and price effects, while the difference between the original and the flexible budget is explained by volume. The treatment of fixed costs differs, because they do not move with volume in the short run.

## Finding causes

Numbers point to where to look; causes come from conversations with the business. Two simple tools help: asking "why" repeatedly until you reach something actionable, and a cause-and-effect (Ishikawa) diagram that groups possible causes by category. Check data and timing first, because many variances are booking effects. Then separate causes the manager can influence from external ones.

## Agreeing actions

An explanation without a next step is a report, not control. For each material unfavourable variance, record the cause, whether it is one-off or recurring, the corrective action, the **owner** and a **date** for follow-up. Update the forecast if the variance will persist.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. Budget: 1,000 units at 50 EUR = 50,000 EUR. Actual: 900 units at 52 EUR = 46,800 EUR. Total variance = -3,200 EUR. Price effect = (52 - 50) x 900 = +1,800 EUR. Volume effect = (900 - 1,000) x 50 = -5,000 EUR. Check: +1,800 - 5,000 = -3,200. The sales manager explains that two customers delayed orders to next month (timing) and that a price increase was accepted by the rest. Action: confirm the delayed orders, owner sales manager, follow-up at next month-end; the forecast keeps the volume in next month.

## Key takeaways

- Price effect = (actual price - budget price) x actual volume; volume effect = (actual volume - budget volume) x budget price.
- The effects must add up to the total; fix and document the convention.
- Name the comparison base: budget, forecast or prior year.
- Every material variance needs a cause, an owner, a date and, if persistent, a forecast update.
