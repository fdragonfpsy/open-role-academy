## Why this matters

In complex deals, discovery is not one conversation with one person. Several people with different goals take part, the stated requirements may have been written by someone who is not the user, and the customer's own picture of the problem can be wrong. Advanced practitioners handle this with judgement, keep the customer's interests in view and accept that sometimes there is no fit. Examples below are invented.

## Multiple stakeholders, conflicting needs

OpenStax describes the buying centre, a group of people who together describe needs, write specifications and select suppliers, within a B2B process of eight stages. Typical conflicts: operations wants speed, finance wants low cost, IT wants low risk. Treat each as a legitimate set of criteria. Map who holds which criterion and who has the final say, then check with each group separately. Where criteria collide, bring the conflict into the open ("Operations asked for X, finance for Y. How will you weigh them?") instead of silently favouring the person who likes you.

## Underlying needs without manipulation

OpenStax lists "secret needs", things a customer may not state or realise, such as status. Uncovering motives is useful, but using them to push a sale the customer would not make on reflection is manipulation. A practical test: could you say aloud what you are doing and why, in front of the customer, without embarrassment? Ask about motives openly ("What would make this a success for you personally?") and do not exploit insecurities.

## Question quality

The Fragetechnik article distinguishes question forms by how strongly they steer. Leading questions ("You would agree that manual work is a waste, right?") may feel efficient but produce answers you cannot trust, and customers notice. Prefer neutral formulations, and reflect back what you hear (see Active listening). When the customer's assumption looks wrong ("We need more licences"), test it with questions ("What happens when a colleague wants access? How often?") rather than contradicting.

## Value in the customer's numbers

Ask for the data behind impact statements, and build the value estimate together, labelling assumptions. A calculation the customer helped to create carries weight; one you invent does not. Never present estimates as guaranteed outcomes.

## Knowing when there is no fit

If discovery shows that the need is outside what you can serve, or the customer would gain more from another solution, say so and qualify the deal out. It protects trust and your time, and sometimes leads to referrals later.

## Worked example

A hospital group asks for a scheduling tool. Clinical staff want flexibility for swaps, the HR department wants rule compliance, IT insists on on-premise hosting. In separate talks you find that the actual pain is last-minute absence cover, which nobody owns. You bring the three groups together, summarise the criteria and propose a small pilot for one ward. You also find that on-premise hosting would double the cost for little benefit; you state that and let IT decide with that information.

## Key takeaways

- Map stakeholders, their criteria and the final decision rights; surface conflicts openly.
- Uncover underlying needs, but never exploit them; apply the "say it aloud" test.
- Avoid leading questions; test assumptions with neutral questions.
- Build value estimates with the customer's data and label assumptions.
- Be willing to qualify out when there is no fit.
