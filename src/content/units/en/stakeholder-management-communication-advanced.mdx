## Why this matters

When stakeholders' claims conflict and resources are limited, you cannot satisfy everyone. At advanced level the task is to rank claims openly, negotiate on interests rather than positions and put agreements in writing, so that the project keeps both its goals and its relationships.

## Prioritising with the salience model

The salience model describes stakeholders along three dimensions: power, legitimacy and urgency. Combinations give types. Dormant stakeholders have much power but no legitimacy or urgency and are not likely to get involved. Demanding stakeholders have little power or legitimacy but can make much noise. Discretionary stakeholders have legitimate claims but little power or urgency. Lists of stakeholders often exceed the time available, so focus on those who matter now, and revisit the ranking, because attitudes and urgency change.

## Weighing competing claims

All claims deserve acknowledgement, but they are not equally important, and limited resources force priorities. Legal compliance covers only part of what stakeholders can reasonably expect. Our own steps:

- State each claim as a need, not as a demand.
- Note the power, legitimacy and urgency behind it.
- Check the effect on the project goals and on other stakeholders.
- Decide together with the sponsor and explain the decision to those who lose out.

## Negotiating agreements

Negotiation is the process in which parties pursue their goals by bargaining with another party who has some control over reaching them. Its stages typically run from non-task time through information exchange to influence and persuasion. Their weight differs between cultures: where the relationship matters more than the contract, the opening phase is longer and more important. A negotiation is also an agreement about future action that balances interests. Our own preparation:

- Write down your interests and the other side's likely interests, not just positions.
- Decide what you can give, what you cannot and what your fallback is.
- Open with an exchange of information before you make demands.
- Close with a written agreement: what, who, by when and what happens if it fails.

## When no agreement is possible

Escalate to the sponsor with a short description of the interests, the options and your recommendation. Do it early and openly, never as a surprise.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. Two department heads want the same two developers in the same quarter. Sales needs a launch before a trade fair; Operations needs a fix before an external audit. Both claims are legitimate and urgent, so power does not decide. The project lead asks each side for the underlying interest: Sales needs a visible demo, not the full feature set, and Operations needs the fix, not a specific person. The agreement: one developer works half time for each side for six weeks, a contractor paid from contingency covers the rest, and the demo scope is reduced. It is recorded with owners and dates, and if the contractor is not found by 1 March, the sponsor decides.

## Key takeaways

- Rank stakeholders by power, legitimacy and urgency, and revisit the ranking as attitudes shift.
- Acknowledge every claim, but prioritise openly because resources are limited.
- Negotiate on interests and keep a fallback; in many cultures the relationship comes first.
- Write agreements down, and escalate early with options when none is reachable.
