## Why this matters

Tickets are how IT support remembers, coordinates and proves its work. A good ticket saves time for everyone: the next colleague can continue without calling the user again, patterns become visible in reports, and users trust that nothing gets lost. Handling a ticket well follows four steps: record, prioritise, inform, close.

## Record: what the next person needs

When a user reports a problem, capture:

- **What exactly** does not work — the symptom and any **error message** (a screenshot helps);
- **since when**, and what **changed** shortly before (update, new device, move);
- **who is affected** — one person, a team, everyone;
- **contact details** and when the user is available;
- what you have **already tried**.

"Computer doesn't work" is a starting point, not a ticket description. Ask open questions until you can describe the problem in one clear sentence.

## Prioritise: impact and urgency

Not every ticket is equally important. A common approach derives priority from two questions: **How big is the impact?** (how many users, how critical the service) and **How urgent is it?** (how quickly does harm occur). An order system that is down for the whole sales team outranks a single broken mouse — the mouse can still be swapped quickly. Priority depends on the situation, not on the position of the person who calls.

## Inform: no news is bad news

Users can accept delays if they know **why** and **until when**. Send short updates when the status changes or when an SLA target is at risk, and offer a **workaround** where possible. Never set a ticket to "solved" just to meet a target.

## Close: confirmed and documented

Close an incident when the service works again, the user has **confirmed** it (or an agreed period has passed) and the **resolution is documented** in a way that helps the next person with the same issue.

## Worked example

Sven receives a call: "Outlook keeps crashing." He records the error message, notes that it started after this morning's update and that two colleagues have the same issue. Because three users are affected and e-mail is critical, he sets a high priority, informs them that a fix is being prepared, offers webmail as a workaround and later closes the ticket with the confirmed fix.

## Key takeaways

- Record symptom, time, changes, scope, contact and steps tried.
- Priority = impact × urgency, not the caller's position.
- Inform proactively; offer workarounds.
- Close only when confirmed — and document the fix.
