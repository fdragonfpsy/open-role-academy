## Why this matters

In service work you see names, addresses, orders and sometimes health or payment details. This is personal data, and in the EU the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) sets the rules for it. This unit is about everyday habits, not legal advice. For legal questions, ask your data protection officer.

## What counts as personal data

Personal data is any information about an identifiable person: name, email address, phone number, customer number, order history, a call recording or the text of a chat. A note such as "customer is angry" is also personal data once it is tied to a customer.

## Principles that shape your day

The GDPR lists principles for processing personal data. Translated into service work:

- Lawfulness and purpose: use data only for the reason it was collected, for example solving this customer's case.
- Data minimisation: data must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary. Ask only for what you need.
- Accuracy: correct wrong data when you learn about it.
- Storage limitation: do not keep data longer than needed, so do not copy it into private notes or spreadsheets.
- Integrity and confidentiality: protect data against unauthorised access and loss.

The company must also be able to show that it follows these rules. Individual agents help by following the company's procedures.

## Check identity before you disclose (our own)

Our own rule of thumb: before you read out or change anything in an account, confirm the person with the questions your company has defined, such as order number plus a detail only the owner knows. Never accept "I am his wife" or "I am calling from his office" as proof. If the checks fail, say politely that you cannot share details and name the safe route, for example a message to the address on file.

## Everyday habits (our own)

- Do not paste data into tools that your company has not approved.
- Keep notes factual and short; write no health or other sensitive details unless the process requires it.
- Lock your screen, and never share logins.
- If you send data by mistake, or see suspicious access, tell your lead or the privacy contact immediately, because a company that notices a personal data breach has to act quickly (the GDPR sets 72 hours for notifying the authority, counted from when the company became aware).

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A caller says he is the husband of a customer and asks for her delivery address because "the parcel is lost". The agent explains that she cannot share account details without checking the account holder, offers to contact the account holder through the address on file and logs the call. The agent gave nothing away, and the real customer can decide.

## Key takeaways

- Customer details in your tools are personal data; the GDPR applies.
- Use data only for the case, ask for the minimum, keep it safe and short-lived.
- Verify identity before disclosing, with your company's questions.
- Report incidents at once, and ask the data protection officer when unsure.
