## Why this matters in every role

Almost every job today runs on e-mail, shared drives and cloud accounts. That makes every employee part of the company's defence — not only the IT department. Most attacks do not start with clever hacking but with a person who clicks a link, reuses a password or sends a file to the wrong address. The good news: a few habits prevent a large share of these problems.

## The three security goals

Information security is usually described with three goals, often called the **CIA triad**:

- **Confidentiality** — only authorised people can see information. Example: a salary list must not be visible to the whole department.
- **Integrity** — information is correct and complete and cannot be changed unnoticed. Example: bank details on an invoice must not be silently replaced.
- **Availability** — information and systems work when you need them. Example: the customer database is reachable during business hours.

When something goes wrong, ask yourself which of the three goals is affected. That helps you describe the problem clearly when you report it.

## Recognising phishing

Phishing messages pretend to come from a trusted sender, such as a bank, a parcel service or your own IT department. Typical warning signs are:

- **pressure and threats** ("your account will be closed within 24 hours");
- **requests to enter passwords or payment data** via a link or attachment;
- **surprising messages** you did not expect, even if logo and colours look real;
- **links whose real target differs** from the text shown — hover over the link before clicking.

Logos and design prove nothing: attackers copy them easily.

## Strong passwords and two-factor authentication

Use a **different password for every account**, so a breach at one service does not open the others. Long passwords are hard to guess; a passphrase of several random words is easy to remember. A **password manager** stores unique passwords for you, so you only need one strong master password. Wherever possible, switch on **two-factor authentication (2FA)**: a second proof, such as a code from an app or a hardware key, means a stolen password alone is not enough.

## Worked example

Amira from accounting receives an e-mail "from IT": her mailbox is full and she must log in via a link today. The sender address looks odd and the link points to an unknown domain. Amira does not click. She reports the e-mail through the company's reporting channel. IT confirms it is phishing and warns everyone.

## Key takeaways

- Think in confidentiality, integrity and availability.
- Pressure plus a request for credentials means: stop and check.
- One account, one password — ideally from a password manager — plus 2FA.
- Report suspicious messages early — a false alarm costs little, a missed attack a lot.
