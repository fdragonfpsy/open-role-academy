## Why this matters

Once volume and price are separated, a third driver often remains: **mix**, the composition of what was sold. A variance report that ignores mix can show "volume on plan, price on plan" while revenue is still short. At advanced level you also design how variances feed performance management: who is accountable, how often the numbers are reviewed and what incentives the reporting creates.

## The mix effect

With several products, split the total variance in three parts. One common convention, valued at budget prices:

- **Volume effect** = (total actual units - total budget units) x budget average price per unit
- **Mix effect** = sum over products of (actual units - actual total units x budget share) x budget price of the product
- **Price effect** = sum over products of (actual price - budget price) x actual units

Together they reconcile to actual minus budget revenue. A positive mix effect means the sales moved towards products with above-average budget prices (or margins, if you use contribution margin rather than revenue). In margin analysis the mix effect is usually more informative than in revenue analysis, because margins differ more between products than prices do. As before, other conventions exist; document which one you use.

## Controllable and non-controllable variances

Separate what the budget owner can influence (efficiency, discounts granted, staffing decisions) from what they cannot (exchange rates, a central cost allocation, a market shift). Reporting both, but judging the manager on the controllable part, keeps the conversation fair. Where the plan itself was unrealistic, say so explicitly rather than hiding it inside an operational explanation.

## Performance-management rhythm

Variances become steering only inside a regular rhythm:

1. A monthly close with a fixed calendar and an agreed threshold for commentary.
2. Written commentary by the budget owner, challenged by controlling.
3. A forecast update when the variance is expected to persist.
4. A tracked action list with owners and dates, reviewed at the next meeting.

Keep commentary short: what happened, why, what it means for the full-year outlook, what is being done. Show the bridge (budget to actual, step by step) rather than long tables.

## Behavioural side effects

Targets and variance reporting change behaviour. Heavy focus on one month can lead to pulling revenue forward or delaying costs; very tight budgets can encourage padding in the next round; a target on one KPI can push others down. Mitigate with a balanced set of measures, a focus on year-to-date and outlook, and by asking about trade-offs when numbers look unusually good.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. Product A: budget 100 units at 10 EUR, product B: budget 100 units at 20 EUR; budget revenue 3,000 EUR. Actual: A 140 units at 10 EUR, B 60 units at 20 EUR; actual revenue 2,600 EUR, total variance -400 EUR. Total units are 200 in both cases, so the volume effect is 0. Price effect is 0. Mix effect = (140 - 100) x 10 + (60 - 100) x 20 = 400 - 800 = -400 EUR. The whole shortfall is mix: customers bought more of the cheaper product. The follow-up question for sales is why, and whether the shift is lasting; if so, the forecast should assume it.

## Key takeaways

- Reconcile price, volume and mix to the total variance and document the convention.
- Mix matters most in margin analysis; check whether the average price or margin moved.
- Judge managers on controllable variances; report non-controllable ones separately.
- Embed variances in a rhythm of commentary, forecast update and tracked actions.
- Watch for incentives that distort behaviour.
