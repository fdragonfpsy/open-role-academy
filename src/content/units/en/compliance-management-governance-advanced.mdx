## Why this matters

At an advanced level the question changes from "do we have the elements?" to "do they work, and can we show it?". That needs evidence, honest reporting to the governing body and attention to culture. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Effective versus documented

A system can look complete on paper and still fail: policies nobody reads, training that is clicked through, hotlines nobody trusts. Effectiveness questions are practical. Are risks assessed from real business data? Do controls run as written? Do people raise concerns and do they see action? Are incidents traced to root causes? Look for evidence such as test results, case statistics, training completion plus comprehension checks and follow-up on findings, rather than for the mere existence of documents.

## Proportionality

Measures should fit the size, activities and risk exposure of the organisation. A small firm needs a simple code, a few clear rules and a named contact; a large international group needs more structure, including regional roles and consistent reporting. Over-engineering burdens the business and produces paper; under-engineering leaves risks open. Record why a design was chosen so the reasoning can be reviewed.

## Culture and incentives

The corporate governance articles stress that governance concerns how an organisation is directed and controlled, including the incentives set for management. If bonus targets reward results at any cost, a compliance policy will lose against them. Check whether targets, promotion and consequence management support the stated rules, and whether similar breaches are treated consistently regardless of seniority. Tone from the top is demonstrated by decisions, not slogans.

## Roles and independence

The three lines model describes the business as owner of risk, compliance and risk management as oversight, and internal audit as independent assurance. For an advanced compliance professional, the design question is access and independence: does the compliance function have a direct way to the governing body, enough resources and the authority to escalate? Where it takes on operational tasks, assurance by the third line becomes more important, because the second line would then be reviewing its own work.

## Reporting to the governing body

Report in a way that supports decisions: the main risks and how they changed, key indicators, significant cases in anonymised form, status of improvement actions and open resource needs. Avoid long statistics without interpretation. Explain limits of the data. Which duties of the board or supervisory body apply in your legal form is a question for counsel and the current statutes.

## Improvement cycle

Plan, implement, check, improve: schedule a periodic review of the risk assessment, test selected controls, update policies and refresh training with lessons learned. Keep an action list with owners and dates and report progress.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. Training completion at a manufacturer is 98 percent, yet monitoring shows repeated unregistered supplier gifts. The compliance lead investigates: the training was a generic video, the gift register is hard to find and a sales bonus rewards fast onboarding of suppliers. The fix is not another policy: the lead proposes scenario-based training, a one-page register in the procurement tool, a review of the bonus rule with HR and a quarterly report to the management board with the trend of registered versus found gifts.

## Key takeaways

- Judge effectiveness by evidence, not by documents.
- Fit the design to size, activities and risk; record the reasoning.
- Incentives and consequences shape behaviour more than slogans.
- Independence and access to the governing body are design questions for the compliance function.
- Report decision-relevant information and run a documented improvement cycle.
