## Why this matters

Knowing the sources is the first step. In practice you must also decide which rule wins, whether an EU act applies directly or through national law, and how to apply a rule to a case. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Hierarchy of norms

The Wikipedia articles on the hierarchy of norms (English) and Normenhierarchie (German) describe a ranking of legal rules in which a lower rule must stay within the frame of a higher one. For Germany, the usual picture is: constitution, then federal statutes, then regulations and lower-level rules; state law has its own constitution and statutes. Where federal and state law collide on the same question, federal law generally prevails. Contracts and company policies are not part of this ladder of state law, but they must respect it.

## EU law in the national system

The article on the law of the European Union explains that the EU has its own legal order, with treaties as primary law and acts such as regulations and directives as secondary law. At concept level: a regulation applies directly and does not need a national act to take effect; a directive addresses the EU countries, which must pass or adapt national law to reach its goal. This is why one topic can involve both an EU text and a German statute. Where EU law and national law truly conflict, EU law generally takes precedence. Whether a conflict exists is a question for legal counsel.

## Reading a norm

Most legal norms have two parts: conditions ("if ...") and a legal consequence ("then ..."). To apply a norm:

1. Identify the facts of the case without judging them.
2. Find the norm that might apply and split it into conditions and consequence.
3. Check each condition against the facts; this step is called subsumption.
4. If all conditions are met, the consequence follows; if one is unclear, mark it as an open question.

Many terms in norms need interpretation. Courts interpret them, so check how they are usually read, and do not rely on wording alone.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented rule and company. Suppose a fictional statute says: "A shop owner must display prices (condition: the goods are offered to consumers; consequence: prices must be shown)". A company sells only to other businesses. Condition check: are the goods offered to consumers? No. The consequence does not follow from this fictional norm. Note the open points: does another norm, perhaps at EU level, apply to business customers? You record the question and escalate instead of guessing.

## Key takeaways

- A lower rule must stay within the frame of a higher one; federal law generally prevails over state law, EU law over national law.
- Regulations apply directly; directives work through national law, so you often need both texts.
- Apply a norm by splitting it into conditions and consequence and checking each condition against the facts.
- Mark unclear conditions as open questions and escalate.
