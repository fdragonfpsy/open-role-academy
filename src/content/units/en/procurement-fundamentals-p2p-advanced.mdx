## Why this matters

At advanced level the question changes from "does each purchase follow the steps?" to "does the whole system work, and where should we intervene?". Specialist staff and a formal process exist to keep spending from being wasteful or fraudulent; automation can save cost, but only a process that people actually use produces data worth steering by.

## Metrics for the process (our own selection)

Define each metric in writing before you report it:

- PO-before-invoice rate: share of invoices whose purchase order was issued before the invoice date.
- On-contract share: share of spend bought under an agreed contract or catalogue.
- Invoice exception rate: share of invoices that stop for a mismatch.
- Requisition-to-order time: how long approved needs wait before an order goes out.

A metric without a definition invites arguments. Also look at who feeds it: if purchasing staff keep the data themselves, test it against accounting records.

## Control versus speed

Every approval step costs time, and a slow process drives people to buy around it. Our own design principle: put heavy control where value or risk is high, and make low-risk purchases easy through catalogues or framework contracts. Segregation of duties stays in every path. A process that blocks routine needs will be bypassed, and a bypassed process produces neither control nor data.

## Spend under management

Spend under management is commonly defined as the share of total spend (direct and indirect) that procurement manages or influences; a related measure uses only addressable spend, the spend that could be influenced at all. A narrower definition counts only spend through preferred contracts with negotiated terms. Published averages exist, but they depend on the definition and the sample, so never benchmark your figure against one without matching the definition.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A mid-sized firm receives 4,000 supplier invoices a month; 2,600 (65%) have a purchase order issued before the invoice. The other 1,400 (35%) look like a problem, so the team reviews them: 600 are legitimate contract-based payments such as rent and utilities, and 800 (20% of all invoices) are retroactive orders where someone bought first and ordered later. Loosening the invoice tolerance would not touch this. The team creates a contract-based path for the 600 so they leave the statistic, and introduces a simple catalogue plus a clear rule for the 800. After three months it measures the 800 again.

## Key takeaways

- Define each process metric in writing and check its data source.
- Separate legitimate no-order payments from true bypassing before you act.
- Control where value and risk are high; make routine purchases easy, and keep roles separated.
- Spend under management has several definitions; compare only like with like.
