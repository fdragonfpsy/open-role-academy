## Why this matters

Many meetings fail not because of the agenda but because nobody said how the decision would be made. People then argue, assume or wait. As a lead, you decide the decision method before the discussion and tell the group.

## Four common approaches

These labels are a practical simplification, not a fixed taxonomy from the sources.

1. **Leader decides:** you decide alone. It is fast and fits urgent or clearly owned decisions, but you lose the group's information and commitment.
2. **Consult, then decide:** you hear the team, then you decide and explain why. Good when you stay accountable but need input.
3. **Majority vote:** the option with most votes wins. It is quick and clear, but the minority may feel unheard.
4. **Consent or consensus:** the group looks for a proposal everyone can live with.

The Wikipedia article on consensus decision-making explains that consensus differs from unanimity. It describes a decision rule as the level of agreement needed to finalise a decision, and mentions options such as standing aside or blocking. The German article on the Konsensprinzip says decisions are made without a vote against, but abstaining is possible. It warns that every "no" blocks the process, so members must weigh whether their objection is important enough. The German article on Soziokratie distinguishes the consent principle from consensus: not everyone must agree, but everyone gives consent to a solution that is suitable under the circumstances. An objection helps refine the proposal.

## Why involve people

The OpenStax chapter on group decision-making lists advantages: different perspectives, more options, and, because affected people take part, better understanding and stronger commitment. It also lists disadvantages: more conflict, more work and groupthink.

## Groupthink

The Wikipedia article on groupthink describes it as a phenomenon where the desire for harmony or conformity leads to a dysfunctional decision, because the group reaches consensus without critical evaluation. OpenStax names one counter-measure: a devil's advocate who deliberately points out flawed logic. The German article on Gruppendenken also mentions this role, the Advocatus Diaboli. Another simple step is to let people write down their view before anyone speaks, so the first speaker does not set the tone. This second step is common practice, not from the sources.

## Record the decision

Minutes focus on decisions, as the Wikipedia article on minutes says. Many teams keep a short decision log, a list of entries with date, decision, reasons, alternatives considered and owner. This is common practice, not a standard from the sources. When a similar question returns months later, the log answers "why did we do this?" without a new debate.

## Synchronous or asynchronous

The Wikipedia article on computer-mediated communication describes synchronous communication as real-time, with all parties engaged at once (video chats, audio calls), and asynchronous communication as taking place when the parties do not communicate in unison, such as email. For deciding, a simple rule of thumb: use a live meeting for contested or emotional topics, and a written proposal with a comment deadline for clear, low-risk ones.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. Priya leads a support team that must choose a new shift pattern. She announces: "I will decide after consulting you, by Friday." She posts a written proposal with a comment deadline, then holds a 30-minute live session on the two points that drew objections. Before the session she asks Lars to argue the case against the proposal. Priya decides and writes the decision log entry: date, chosen pattern, reasons, the alternative that was rejected, and a review date.

## Key takeaways

- Say how the decision will be made before the discussion starts.
- Consultation, majority and consent each trade speed against buy-in.
- Guard against groupthink, for example with a devil's advocate.
- Record decision, reasons and owner, and choose live or written discussion on purpose.
