## Why this matters

Practitioners are expected to turn a vague worry about suppliers into a short, ranked list with named owners. They also face the trade-off between cost and safety: protection against disruption costs money while everything runs smoothly.

## A supplier risk register (our own layout)

- Risk: one sentence, for example "sole supplier of component X is located in one region".
- Likelihood and impact, on the same simple scale across the team.
- Early warning sign: what you would see first, such as late confirmations or a drop in payment behaviour.
- Response: reduce, accept, transfer or avoid, plus a concrete action.
- Owner and review date.

Scores such as financial or operational risk scores are fine as a first pass to decide where to look closer. They do not replace judgement, because a score cannot see everything.

## Reducing dependency

- Single sourcing: one supplier. Simple and often cheap, but one failure stops you.
- Dual sourcing: two suppliers, often with an unequal split. More resilient, more effort and a smaller volume per supplier.
- Multiple sourcing: more than two suppliers, useful for standard items.
- Other measures: safety stock, an approved alternative part, a second production site at the supplier, or an earlier warning in the contract.

Do not decide by category label alone. Ask which items would stop your production, how long a switch would take, and what it costs to qualify a second supplier.

## Resilience and time to recover

Risk management is cause-oriented: it looks at what might go wrong. Resilience asks how well the chain copes whatever the cause. A useful measure is time to recover, the time it takes to restore full output after a major disruption. It forces you to think about re-sourcing and re-qualifying, not only about the event.

## Sustainability at suppliers (our own approach)

- Put expectations in a supplier code of conduct and refer to it in the contract.
- Use a self-assessment questionnaire to find where to look closer, and keep audits for the riskiest suppliers.
- When a problem appears, agree a corrective action plan with dates, and check it.
- Remember that the chain does not end at your first-tier suppliers; ask where the risk sits further upstream.

## Due diligence laws

The German LkSG and the EU directive 2024/1760 both deal with due diligence for human rights and environmental risks in supply chains. Scope, deadlines and national transposition have been moving, so check with Legal or Compliance what your company must do, and which documents purchasing must supply. Do not tell suppliers that a law requires something unless Legal has confirmed it.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. A plant buys a seal from one supplier for 120,000 euros a year. A stoppage costs an estimated 30,000 euros per day of idle production. Qualifying a second supplier would take about 10 weeks and cost 25,000 euros, and the second supplier would take 30 percent of the volume at a 4 percent higher price: 36,000 euros x 4 percent = 1,440 euros extra a year. If the single supplier fails for only 3 days, the loss is 90,000 euros, so dual sourcing pays for itself after one short outage, assuming the plant can run on stock while the second source is qualified.

## Key takeaways

- A register with an owner, a response and a review date makes risks manageable.
- Single, dual and multiple sourcing trade resilience against effort and unit price.
- Resilience and time to recover complement likelihood-and-impact scoring.
- Use a code of conduct, questionnaires and corrective action plans for supplier sustainability, and ask Legal about due diligence duties.
