## Why this matters

At advanced level the aim is not a thick report but a better decision. Start from the decision and work backwards: what would we do if the answer were A, and what if it were B? If both answers lead to the same action, the research is not needed.

## Designing backwards

Write three lines before any fieldwork: the decision, the options, and the result that would change your choice. Then choose the cheapest design that can produce such a result. OpenStax counts selecting the data collection method, collecting and analysing data and presenting the results among the steps of a research plan; the advanced point is to settle at the start how they will be used.

## Triangulation

One source rarely settles a question. Combine, for instance, internal sales data (what people did), interviews (why they say they did it) and an external industry report (the wider market). When sources agree, confidence rises. When they disagree, that is an insight in itself: ask what each source can and cannot see. OpenStax notes that secondary data can be outdated and may be available to competitors, and that primary data may not generalise beyond the research question. Say so in your report.

## Correlation, causation and experiments

Customers who open our newsletter buy more, so the newsletter raises sales? Not necessarily: interested customers may both open and buy. OpenStax names experimental (causal) research with A/B tests as the way to go beyond correlation. If an experiment is feasible, randomly assign who sees which version and compare outcomes; if it is not, describe the result as an association.

## Reporting with judgement

State the finding, the evidence, the limits (sample, timing, who is missing), your confidence and the recommended action. Do not hide uncertain results; do not oversell small differences. Keep raw data and wording of questions for later review. For competitor information, use publicly available sources only.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A B2B software firm wonders whether to build a mobile app. Decision rule written first: build only if at least a clear share of key accounts say field staff need it and a pilot shows use. Triangulation: support tickets (few mobile requests), eight customer interviews (field teams say they would use it for photos), a competitor feature check from public websites (two competitors have apps). Both limits and conflicts are listed. Recommendation: a four-week prototype for three accounts instead of a full build, with agreed usage criteria.

## Key takeaways

- Define the decision and the result that would change it before collecting data.
- Triangulate sources and state each one's blind spots.
- Use experiments for causal claims; otherwise write 'associated with'.
- Report limits, confidence and a concrete recommended action.
