## Why this matters

Evaluating every supplier equally deeply wastes effort, and judging suppliers only by past performance can miss the real exposure. Supplier management covers three things: evaluating and selecting suppliers, developing their performance, and deciding how deeply a supplier is integrated into your value chain.

## Evaluate in proportion to importance

Use a segmentation such as the Kraljič matrix, which also works for supplier types, to set the depth of evaluation. A strategic supplier gets a full review including cooperation and innovation, joint targets and regular meetings. A supplier of non-critical goods gets a light check on a few indicators. Our own rule: the higher the impact of a failure, the more you invest in knowing the supplier.

## Closing a performance gap

A gap analysis compares the actual performance in quality, delivery time, cost and technology with the target. Respond with a staged path that is fair and known in advance (our own ladder):

- Share the data and ask for the supplier's analysis.
- Agree an improvement plan with measures and dates.
- Limit new business until the plan delivers.
- Replace the supplier, once an alternative is qualified.

Do not skip to the last step when the supplier is hard to replace; the cost of a failed switch can exceed the cost of the gap.

## Dependence, risk and legal duties

A good score does not remove dependence. Ask about the points that a scorecard hides: sole-source status, a single production site, financial stability and a failing supplier's effect on your own deliveries. Buying from a supplier means depending on it for quality, timely delivery and cost control; contracts with price or capacity commitments reduce the exposure. Supplier selection and evaluation may also have to consider legal due diligence rules; German sources mention the Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (Lieferkettensorgfaltspflichtengesetz). Whether and how such a rule applies to your company depends on its situation, so ask legal counsel and do not rely on this text.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented suppliers. Supplier X has an excellent scorecard (98 percent on time, 200 ppm) but is the only source of a bottleneck part, from one plant. The team does not reward the score; it qualifies a second source and builds a safety stock. Supplier Y is strategic, but quality drifts to 2,500 ppm. The team shares the data, agrees a six-month improvement plan with a joint root-cause analysis and monthly checks, and limits new part numbers until the figures recover. Replacement is prepared as a fallback, not started.

## Key takeaways

- Scale evaluation depth to supplier importance; segment first.
- Use a known, staged path from feedback to replacement, and do not skip to the end for hard-to-replace suppliers.
- A strong scorecard can hide dependence; check sole sourcing, sites and financial stability.
- For legal supply chain duties, involve legal counsel.
