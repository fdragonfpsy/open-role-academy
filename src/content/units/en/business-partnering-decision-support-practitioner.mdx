## Why this matters

At practitioner level you are asked not only to explain numbers but to shape a decision. Decisions are made under uncertainty, with limited time and with people who have their own interests. A clear memo and a disciplined handling of assumptions make the decision better and more traceable.

## The decision memo

A short memo (one page) usually contains:

1. **Decision needed** and by when.
2. **Context**: the goal and the relevant baseline.
3. **Options**, including "do nothing", each with cost, benefit, risks and key assumptions.
4. **Recommendation** with the reason, and what would change it.
5. **Next steps** and an owner.

Lay out trade-offs side by side: no option is best on every criterion. State clearly what is a fact (from the ledger), what is an assumption (a forecast) and what is your judgement.

## Challenging assumptions

Managers often present figures that rest on hopeful assumptions. Ask neutral questions: What must be true for this to work? What is the evidence? What happened in the last comparable case? Typical weak spots are volume, price, timing and cost savings. Research on decision-making names barriers such as bounded rationality, time pressure, uncertainty, personal bias and escalation of commitment, that is, sticking with a failing course because effort was already spent. Naming them helps to keep the discussion about the facts and not about people.

## Sensitivity and scenarios

A **sensitivity analysis** changes one input at a time and shows how strongly the result reacts. It points to the assumptions that matter most. **Scenario planning** goes further: it describes several internally consistent possible futures, for example base, optimistic and pessimistic, and checks how each option performs. The goal is not to predict the future but to see which option holds up across scenarios and which signals would tell you that a scenario is arriving.

## Independence under pressure

Stakeholders may ask you to soften a number or drop an inconvenient scenario. Weigh their claims, but keep the numbers true. If you have a stake in the outcome, disclose the conflict. Document what was asked and what you decided; escalate if you cannot agree.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. A plant manager wants a new machine and presents savings of 200,000 EUR per year. The controller writes a memo with three options: buy the machine, lease it, keep the old one. She tests the saving with three scenarios for output volume. In the pessimistic scenario the machine pays off only after a long time; leasing performs acceptably in all three. She recommends leasing and states that buying becomes attractive if volume stays above a named threshold for two quarters.

## Key takeaways

- Write memos with options, trade-offs, a recommendation and its limits.
- Mark facts, assumptions and judgement separately.
- Use sensitivity and scenarios to test which option is robust.
- Stay independent: keep numbers true, disclose conflicts, document.
