## Why this matters

HR touches every stage of the employee lifecycle: job ads, selection, onboarding, pay, development and exit. At each stage, decisions can treat people fairly or unfairly. This unit gives you the basic vocabulary for a Germany/EU setting. It stays general: the exact legal duties, deadlines and remedies depend on current law and your company's rules, so involve legal counsel or the responsible experts for specific cases. HR staff do not give legal advice.

## Diversity, equity, inclusion

The Wikipedia article on diversity in a business context describes diversity as including people from a variety of backgrounds and identities in an organisation. It notes that characteristics can be legally protected (such as religion) or not (such as social class or educational level). Practice insight: **diversity** is about who is in the organisation, **inclusion** is about whether people can take part and be heard, and **equity** is about giving fair opportunities, which can mean different support for different people. The Equal opportunity article discusses the idea that people should have fair chances to compete; it also notes that some people are better placed than others to use the same opportunity. That is why equal treatment on paper may not be enough.

## The legal frame in Germany, in brief

The German Wikipedia article on the Allgemeines Gleichbehandlungsgesetz (AGG) describes it as a federal law that aims to prevent and remove disadvantage based on race or ethnic origin, sex, religion or belief, disability, age or sexual identity. It also covers job applicants, not only employees, and the article links its adoption in 2006 to EU anti-discrimination directives. Practice insight: you do not need to memorise paragraphs, but you should know that this law exists, that it applies from the job ad onwards, and that questions about it go to legal counsel.

## Unconscious bias

The Implicit stereotype article describes unconscious associations that can influence behaviour even when someone consciously rejects stereotypes, for example in hiring. Practice insight: good intentions are not a safeguard. Structured processes, such as the same criteria for every candidate, protect better than good will alone.

## What you can do

- Use neutral, clear wording in job ads and descriptions.
- Judge candidates against job-related criteria that were set before the interviews.
- Do not ask about protected characteristics unless legal counsel confirms it is allowed and needed.
- Treat every report of unfair treatment seriously, document facts and pass it to the responsible contact.
- Keep personal information confidential.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. A hiring manager says about a candidate: "She will probably go on parental leave soon, let's pick someone else." You do not argue about the person. You calmly say that the decision must rest on the role's requirements and that this reason is not job-related. You offer to compare both candidates against the agreed criteria in a short table, and you note that you will involve legal counsel if the manager wants to base the decision on such a reason.

## Key takeaways

- Diversity is who is present, inclusion is who can take part, equity is fair opportunity.
- Equal treatment rules in Germany/EU protect against disadvantage on grounds such as sex, age, disability, religion or belief, ethnic origin and sexual identity; check current law for details.
- Unconscious bias is normal; structure reduces its effect.
- Decide on job-related criteria, not on assumptions.
- Pass concerns to the responsible contact and ask legal counsel for legal questions.
