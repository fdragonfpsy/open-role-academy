## Why this matters

Theories do not tell you what to do, but they give you better questions. This unit introduces two well-known ones and applies them to workload and wellbeing. It is concept-level education, not medical advice.

## Self-determination theory

The Wikipedia article describes self-determination theory (SDT) as a macro theory of motivation developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan. It proposes three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. The article says more autonomous forms of motivation have been linked to better outcomes in areas such as education and health care, and also notes criticism, including debate over whether rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation. The German article describes the same three needs as autonomy, competence and social relatedness. For leads, the questions are: Does this person have a say in how they work? Can they build skills and see progress? Do they feel connected to the team?

## Two-factor theory

Wikipedia and OpenStax (Organizational Behavior, chapter on content theories) describe Frederick Herzberg's motivator-hygiene theory. In the Wikipedia article, factors related to the work itself, such as achievement, recognition and responsibility, drive satisfaction, while separate factors, such as working conditions and salary levels, relate to dissatisfaction, and the two operate independently. The German article names these motivators and hygiene factors. The practical point: fixing a poor environment prevents dissatisfaction but does not by itself create enthusiasm, and meaningful work does not make up for unfair conditions. Treat it as a lens; the theory is old and debated.

## Workload and wellbeing

Check capacity before adding work. Make trade-offs visible: if something new comes in, ask what moves back. Watch overtime patterns. Ask in one-to-ones about workload and recovery, and listen for changes over time.

## Burnout awareness

The Wikipedia article on occupational burnout says that, according to the WHO, burnout is a work-related phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with symptoms described as energy depletion or exhaustion, increased mental distance from one's job or negativism, and reduced professional efficacy. It also says the WHO does not classify it as a medical condition and that its status is debated. A lead does not diagnose or treat. A lead notices change, opens a conversation, reduces avoidable pressure and points to support such as HR, occupational health services or the person's own doctor.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. Elif, a skilled analyst, no longer volunteers for new topics. Her lead Marco asks about autonomy, competence and relatedness in turn. Elif says she feels she only does routine reports and has little say. Marco gives her ownership of a small analysis, with agreed outcome but free choice of method, and schedules a talk about her development. Later he sees her working late again, asks about workload and moves a report to another colleague.

## Key takeaways

- SDT: autonomy, competence and relatedness support motivation; it is debated in detail.
- Two-factor theory: environment issues cause dissatisfaction, work content drives satisfaction.
- Make workload trade-offs visible and watch for sustained overload.
- For burnout signs: notice, talk, reduce pressure, refer; do not diagnose.
