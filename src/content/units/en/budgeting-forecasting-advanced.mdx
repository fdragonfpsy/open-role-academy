## Variance driver analysis

Variance analysis is described as a tool of budgetary control and performance evaluation that assesses differences between the budgeted or standard amount and the actual amount. The source explains that a materials cost variance can be divided into a **price variance** and a **usage variance**, labour into a **rate variance** and an **efficiency variance**, and that mix and yield variances can also be calculated. Splitting a variance into drivers turns "we are 2,300 below plan" into something an owner can act on.

For sales, the general method is the same. The **volume effect** asks: what if only the quantity had changed, at the budgeted price? The **price effect** asks: what changed because the price differed, at the actual quantity? The **mix effect** arises when you sell several products with different margins: a different proportion of products changes the average result even if total volume is on plan. Order matters; state in the report which order you used.

## Scenarios and sensitivities

**Sensitivity analysis** is the study of how uncertainty in the output of a model can be allocated to different sources of uncertainty in its inputs. In a budget model: change one assumption, such as price or volume, by a stated amount and see how the result moves. This shows which assumptions matter most. **Scenario planning** is a strategic planning method that some organisations use to make flexible long-term plans; the source points out it differs from sensitivity analysis. In practice scenarios are coherent stories, for example "weak demand", in which several assumptions change together. Use sensitivities to find the critical inputs and scenarios to prepare for different possible futures.

## Forecast accuracy review

The Forecasting article defines the forecast error as the difference between the actual value and the forecast value for the period: error = actual - forecast. It lists measures such as mean absolute error and mean absolute percentage error, and notes that if the errors have a mean other than zero, the forecasts are biased. A simple review: after each period, record forecast, actual and error for key lines; look for the size of the errors and for a systematic direction. The article also says accuracy can only be judged on data not used to fit the method. Use the findings to improve assumptions, not to blame people.

## Process design and behavioural side effects

*Practice insight, not from a cited source.* Budget design choices change behaviour. If budgets are used both for planning and for rewards, owners may have an incentive to propose low targets, a habit often called **sandbagging**, or to spend remaining money at period end so the next budget is not cut. Mitigations people use: separate the forecast (best estimate) from the target, challenge assumptions with data, review variances for learning, and keep the calendar short enough to be useful. The Wikipedia article on zero-based budgeting lists benefits, such as questioning momentum from the previous year, and costs, such as time and effort. Choose a design for your company's size and need.

## Communicating variances to non-finance stakeholders

Lead with the message, then the number, then the cause, then the decision needed. Avoid jargon and sign confusion: write "costs were 400 above plan because of travel", not "(A) 400". Show a few drivers instead of every line, say how sure you are, and state what you will do next.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented. Budget sales: 1,000 units at 50 = 50,000. Actual: 900 units at 53 = 47,700. Total variance = 47,700 - 50,000 = -2,300 (unfavourable).

| Effect | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | (900 - 1,000) x 50 | -5,000 |
| Price | (53 - 50) x 900 | +2,700 |
| Total | | -2,300 |

Message for the sales director: "Revenue was 2,300 below plan. Fewer units cost us 5,000; higher prices recovered 2,700. We need to understand whether the price rise caused the volume loss." This sentence gives the answer and the next question.

## Key takeaways

- Split variances into drivers such as price, volume and mix to find who can act.
- Sensitivities vary one assumption; scenarios change several together.
- Track forecast error and look for systematic bias, then fix the assumptions.
- Budget design affects behaviour; sandbagging is a practice insight, so separate forecast from target where possible.
- Report to non-finance colleagues: message, number, cause, decision.
