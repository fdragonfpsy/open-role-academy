## Why this matters

Companies create and use texts, images, software, brands and inventions every day. Knowing the main types of protection helps you spot when permission is needed. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Intellectual property in one view

The Wikipedia articles on intellectual property describe it as a group of legal rights over creations of the mind. Different rights protect different things, so the first question is always: what exactly is it?

## Main rights at a glance

- **Copyright** protects original works such as texts, images, music and software code. It protects the expression, not the underlying idea. The German article on Urheberrecht describes that in Germany protection arises with the creation of the work and does not require registration.
- **Patents** protect technical inventions and are granted by an office after an application. The articles on patents describe that a patent gives its owner the right to exclude others from using the invention for a limited time.
- **Trade marks** protect signs, such as names and logos, that distinguish goods or services of one business from those of others. The article on trademarks describes that protection depends on use or registration, depending on the country.
- **Designs** (industrial design rights) protect the visual appearance of a product, such as its shape or pattern.

There are also other protected information, such as trade secrets, which are covered in the next level.

## Licences

If you want to use a work that someone else owns, you generally need permission. A licence is that permission, with conditions: what you may do, for how long, where and for what purpose. Open licences such as Creative Commons grant standard permissions to the public. According to the Creative Commons site, the licences let creators share their work legally on stated conditions. Typical conditions are attribution, no commercial use, no changes or share-alike. Read the exact licence: the conditions differ and some forbid commercial use or changes.

## Practical rule

Do not assume that something found online is free to use. Look for the author, the licence and the source. If you cannot find the licence, treat the item as not cleared and ask the legal team or choose a different item. Always credit authors as the licence requires.

## Who to ask

Ask your legal department or the person responsible for brand and content before using third-party material in a publication, a presentation for customers or a product.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A project assistant wants to put a nice photo from a web search into a customer newsletter. There is no licence note and no author name. The assistant does not use the photo and searches for pictures with a clear open licence instead. They find one under a Creative Commons licence that requires attribution, and add the required credit with the author name, source link and licence. If the licence had forbidden commercial use, the newsletter, which promotes the company's services, would likely not qualify, and a different picture would have been needed.

## Key takeaways

- Copyright, patents, trade marks and designs protect different things.
- Copyright protects expression, not ideas, and in Germany arises without registration.
- A licence is permission with conditions; open licences also have conditions.
- Found online does not mean free to use; check author and licence.
