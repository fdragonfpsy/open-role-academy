## Why this matters

In large deals many issues and people are involved: price, volume, term, service levels, liability, payment and exclusivity, plus your internal approvers and the customer's buying group. Judgement matters more than technique. The examples are invented.

## Map the negotiation

List issues, who decides on each on both sides and the dependencies. Rank the issues by how much each matters to you and, as far as you can tell, to the customer. Differences in priorities create room for mutual gain: you give what costs you little and matters to them, and receive what the opposite holds. The Harvard-Konzept article emphasises options for mutual gain and interests over positions; this is the same idea. Agree internally in advance on your limits, who can approve exceptions and how fast.

## Power and alternatives

The BATNA article notes that alternatives are hard to evaluate without understanding the other side and that parties can act in bad faith to distort assumptions about their true interests. So treat statements of the other side ("we have a cheaper offer") as claims to test: ask what it includes. Be equally honest about your own: a weak BATNA calls for improving it (filling the pipeline) before the negotiation, not for bluffing during it.

## Manipulative tactics

You will meet tactics: a sudden new demand at the end, a good-cop-bad-cop pattern, artificial deadlines, "this is our standard policy". Name them calmly, return to interests and criteria, and ask for time. Do not use them yourself. Besides the harm to trust, misleading statements, pressure and unfounded claims can breach rules on fair business practice; ask legal or compliance to check your standard terms and statements.

## Walk away, escalate, document

Walk away when the terms fall below the limit agreed internally. Escalate when the customer's demand exceeds your authority, with a short summary of the issue, options and your recommendation. At the end, summarise the agreement in writing and have both sides confirm, so that later interpretation does not depend on memory. Pass details on to the contract stage.

## Worked example

A customer in a large procurement asks for a lower price, longer payment terms and unlimited liability. You split the package. On price, you refer to a total-cost comparison. On payment terms you offer 60 days if the order volume rises. On liability you say you cannot decide alone, explain the usual limits in your company's terms and involve legal. The buyer says a competitor has offered the same for less; you ask for the scope, find it excludes support, and the point disappears. You then document the agreed package and send it for confirmation.

## Key takeaways

- Map issues, stakeholders, approvals and priorities.
- Trade across issues where priorities differ.
- Test claims; improve your alternatives rather than bluffing.
- Counter manipulative tactics calmly and do not use them.
- Know when to walk away or escalate; document the result.
