## Why this matters

At advanced level the difficulty shifts from a single team to the space between teams: the expert who serves three projects, the supplier whose staff you do not manage, the line manager who holds the people you need. In many organisations a project lead has little say in who is assigned, so influence, clear agreements and good escalation decide the outcome.

## Shared specialists

When one person is split across projects, the plans of those projects rarely add up. Each plan assumes the person at the agreed share, and no one sees the total. Our own approach:

- Agree the share in writing with the person and the line manager, per period.
- Ask for the person's total load across all projects, not only yours.
- Schedule the specialist's tasks early and keep slack around them, because delays here spread.
- Name a deputy for each critical expert.

## External contributors

A supplier's staff follow their own management. Treat the relationship as an interface: what is delivered, by when, in which form, who accepts it, and who is the contact on each side. A short delivery agreement per handover, plus a fixed rhythm for status, replaces assumptions with commitments. Include the supplier in your responsibility matrix, so that gaps and overlaps show.

## Competing demands

A lead who manages a team also has to balance demands from people outside it. New managers often discover that the challenge is not only their direct reports but all stakeholders who want a say. When two projects claim the same resource, do not solve it quietly in the corridor. Bring it to the sponsor or the body that sets priorities, with two or three options, each with its consequence in time, cost and scope, and your recommendation.

## Single points of failure (our own check)

For each critical task ask: if this person is absent for two weeks, what happens? Where the answer is "the project stops", reduce the dependence: pair work, documentation of key steps, or a trained deputy.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented portfolio. Two projects each plan a security engineer at 60 percent for May. The line manager's view shows 120 percent. The lead of project A presents three options to the steering group: shift project B's review by three weeks, hire a contractor for 160 hours, or reduce A's test scope. The group chooses the contractor, with the engineer as acceptance owner, and a named deputy is trained during the first week.

## Key takeaways

- Look at a specialist's total load across projects before you plan them in.
- Treat external contributors through explicit interfaces and handover agreements.
- Escalate resource conflicts with options and consequences, not with complaints.
- Test every critical task for a single point of failure and prepare a deputy.
