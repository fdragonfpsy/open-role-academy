## Why this matters

Projects rarely fail on tasks alone. They fail when someone with a stake in the outcome was never asked, never told or never convinced. Identifying stakeholders early and planning how to talk with them is cheap compared with fixing resistance late.

## Who is a stakeholder

A stakeholder is a person or group with a legitimate interest in the course or the result of a project. A broad definition covers anyone who can affect the achievement of the goals or is affected by it. Typical project stakeholders are the sponsor, the project lead, team members, customers and users, line managers, suppliers and consultants, and any group affected by the project while it runs or once it is finished. Also include people who could influence completion positively or negatively.

## Mapping interest and influence

Stakeholder analysis assesses the interests and the influence of the parties involved, so that the project plan can address them. It is usually done in preparation and can be repeated regularly to track changes in attitude. An influence-interest grid plots each stakeholder by their influence over the project and their interest in it. Our own reading of the four areas:

- High influence, high interest: involve closely and often.
- High influence, low interest: keep satisfied with short, relevant updates.
- Low influence, high interest: keep informed.
- Low influence, low interest: monitor with minimal effort.

## A basic communication plan

Good project communication makes sure that every target group receives the information that matters to it, at the right time and in the right form. A simple plan, in our own format, has four columns:

- Who: the stakeholder or group.
- What they need: information, or a decision from them.
- How: the channel, such as a short call, an email or a newsletter.
- When and who sends: the frequency and a named owner.

## Clarifying roles

A responsibility assignment matrix (RACI) lists, for each task or deliverable, who is Responsible (does the work), Accountable (is ultimately answerable and signs off), Consulted (gives opinions, two-way) and Informed (is updated, one-way). According to some approaches, there should be only one accountable person per deliverable.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A project introduces a new time-tracking tool for 200 employees. The stakeholders are the sponsor (HR director), line managers, employees, the payroll team and IT support. The payroll team has high influence, because it must approve the data export, and high interest, so the project lead meets it for 15 minutes every week; Sam owns that channel. Employees are many, with low influence each and moderate interest: they receive a monthly note and a short guide two weeks before go-live. In the RACI for "export format approved", the payroll lead is accountable, IT is responsible, the sponsor is informed and line managers are informed.

## Key takeaways

- A stakeholder is anyone who affects or is affected by the project, not only the sponsor.
- Map interest and influence early and repeat the analysis as attitudes change.
- A communication plan states who needs what, through which channel, when and from whom.
- One accountable person per deliverable keeps roles clear.
