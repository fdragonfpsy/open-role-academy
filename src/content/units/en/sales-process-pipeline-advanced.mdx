## Why this matters

At advanced level you are responsible for how the process itself works. The Sales process engineering article describes treating sales as a process that can be designed and improved with metrics, drawing on quality-management ideas. Its practical core is simple: a well-defined process, measures to judge improvement and continuous adjustment. The hard part is judgement: what to measure, and how not to distort the very data you rely on. Figures below are invented.

## Choose metrics that answer a decision

Useful pipeline measures (practice insight): conversion between stages, average time in each stage, win rate, average deal size and sales-cycle length. For each metric, name the decision it informs. Time-in-stage informs where to coach; win rate by lead source informs where to invest in prospecting. Metrics without a decision are noise.

## Coverage from your own data

Teams often quote a fixed ratio of pipeline value to target. Treat such ratios as a starting guess, not as a fact. Derive coverage from history: if your win rate on opportunities of this type is 20 percent, a target of 100,000 EUR needs roughly 500,000 EUR of qualified pipeline, before accounting for timing. If win rate varies by segment, calculate per segment. Also check quality: a large pipeline made of unqualified deals gives false comfort.

## Distortions

- **Stage inflation:** deals are pushed forward to look good. Counter with evidence-based exit criteria and spot checks of the evidence.
- **Sandbagging:** deals are held back to beat a forecast. Counter by reviewing forecast accuracy as a team habit, not as punishment.
- **Metric gaming:** if only the number of calls is rewarded, calls rise and quality falls. Pair activity measures with outcome measures.

## Standardise where it helps

A common process makes handovers, coaching and forecasting easier. But a one-size process can fail for different segments: a small repeat order and a multi-stakeholder project need different steps. The B2B buying process described by OpenStax shows why: simple re-buys skip stages that new purchases need. Practical resolution: one shared language for stages, separate playbooks per deal type.

## Worked example

Your data show that deals stay 45 days in "proposal sent" and win rates are 12 percent when no second stakeholder is involved by then, but 31 percent when one is. You add an exit criterion for "proposal sent": a second stakeholder is named. You review the effect after a quarter by comparing win rate and cycle time, and you keep the old stage report for comparison.

## Key takeaways

- Every metric should inform a decision; process design is guided by data.
- Compute coverage from your own win rates per segment.
- Anticipate stage inflation, sandbagging and gaming; use evidence and paired measures.
- Standardise the language, not necessarily every step for every deal type.
