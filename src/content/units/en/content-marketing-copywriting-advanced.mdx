## Why this matters

Content marketing earns trust because it looks like information. That is also its risk: if readers cannot tell that a company paid for or wrote a piece to sell, trust and legal compliance are at stake. Advanced practice means deciding where the line runs.

## Disclosure

Annex I of Directive 2005/29/EC lists practices that are always considered unfair in business-to-consumer dealings. Point 11 covers using editorial content in the media to promote a product where a trader has paid for the promotion without making that clear in the content or by images or sounds clearly identifiable by the consumer (advertorial). Article 7(2) adds that failing to identify the commercial intent of a practice, if not already apparent from the context, can be a misleading omission. The German Wikipedia article on content marketing makes the same point for German competition law: paid pieces that appear unlabelled on third-party websites can count as hidden advertising. The safe working rule: label paid or sponsored content clearly and visibly, in the language of the reader. For the national rules and for influencer or affiliate set-ups, involve legal counsel.

## Helpful or promotional

A piece can inform and still sell, but the balance decides how it is perceived. A test, our own: remove the brand name; is the article still useful? If not, it is an advert in disguise and should be labelled and planned as one.

## Evidence for claims

Treat every factual statement in copy ('reduces effort', 'used by many clinics') as something a reader or authority could ask you to prove. Keep a claims register with a source and an owner for each claim, and drop claims without evidence. Do not invent numbers or quotes for effect.

## Drafting with AI

If a draft was produced with AI support, a human must check facts, the tone against the voice guide, and the claims register before publication. The tool can make errors that read fluently, so the review is not optional.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A payroll software vendor commissions a guest article on a trade portal. The article is useful but ends with praise for the product, and the portal does not mark it as paid. The content lead insists on a visible 'Sponsored content' label, removes the claim 'the most accurate payroll software' because no evidence exists, and replaces it with a factual feature description.

## Key takeaways

- Paid content that looks editorial must be clearly identified; this follows from the EU directive and national law.
- The 'remove the brand name' test shows whether a piece is useful or disguised advertising.
- Keep a claims register with sources and owners.
- Human review of AI-assisted drafts covers facts, claims and tone.
