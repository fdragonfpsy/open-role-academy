## Why this matters

In complex deals the presentation is not one demo but a series of conversations and tests with different people. At advanced level your decisions are about judgement: what to prove, to whom, and what not to claim. Examples below are invented.

## Design the evidence for the buying group

OpenStax describes selling to organisations in which several people take part. From discovery, build a short matrix: who decides on which criterion (cost, risk, usability, integration), what they would accept as evidence and who can block. Then choose the format per criterion: a live demo for usability, a reference call for trust, a written technical answer for integration, a pilot for results. A single large presentation seldom serves all of them. Present conflicting criteria openly and let the group set priorities.

## Proof of concept and pilot

A pilot lets the customer test claims with their own data. Agree in writing before starting: purpose, scope, duration, who participates, measures of success, what you provide, what the customer provides and what happens afterwards. Define data handling at the start; if personal data is involved, involve both data-protection contacts and keep to the agreed purpose. Without agreed success measures a pilot drifts and the customer decides on feelings. Also decide in advance what you will do if the measures are not met.

## What to promise

When the product does not meet a criterion, you have three honest choices: say it is not available, show an existing alternative, or describe a planned development as a plan with no guarantee. Promising a feature to win the deal is a risk for the customer and you: claims in presentations can become part of the commercial discussion and may later be held against your company. Your company's rules on roadmap statements apply; if none exist, ask. A fair claim is one you can substantiate.

## When it goes wrong

A failed demo is not the end. Name the problem briefly, do not blame the customer's network or colleagues, switch to the fallback (recording, slides, a separate session) and follow up on the same day. If a weak fit appears in the pilot, say so early; a transparent "no" protects trust and often your reputation for the next deal.

## Worked example

A hospital group with three site IT managers, a procurement lead and a clinical sponsor evaluates your scheduling tool. In your matrix, IT wants security evidence, clinical staff want swap flexibility, procurement wants cost clarity. You offer: a security questionnaire with named answers for IT, a live swap demo with clinical staff, a priced pilot proposal for procurement. The sponsor asks about integration with a payroll system you do not support. You say that it is not available, describe the export alternative and note that a connector is under consideration without a date. The pilot on one ward is agreed with three measures and a review meeting at the end, including a clear statement of the conditions for ending it.

## Key takeaways

- Map stakeholders, criteria and acceptable evidence; choose the format per criterion.
- Agree a written scope, measures and exit conditions for pilots.
- Treat personal data in pilots under the agreed purpose.
- Say plainly what is not available; present plans as plans.
- Recover from failures openly and promptly.
