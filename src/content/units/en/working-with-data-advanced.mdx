## Decisions rest on data — and its limits

At advanced level you use data to make or prepare decisions, and others rely on your analyses. The technical part is rarely the hardest. The real skill is to know **what the data can and cannot tell you**, and to present it honestly.

## Correlation is not causation

If teams using a new tool have higher customer satisfaction, the tool *might* be the reason — or experienced teams adopt new tools earlier *and* satisfy customers better. A third factor can drive both; the direction can also be reversed. Before you claim an effect, ask:

- Could another factor explain both observations?
- Could the cause run the other way?
- Is there a fair comparison, ideally planned in advance (for example a pilot group and a comparison group)?

Communicate associations as associations: "teams with the tool have higher scores" rather than "the tool raises scores".

## Honest charts

Charts can mislead without containing a single wrong number. Typical traps:

- **Truncated axes in bar charts:** values 96 and 98 on an axis starting at 95 look like a threefold difference. Start bar axes at zero.
- **Cherry-picked time windows** that hide the longer trend.
- **3D effects and area symbols** that distort proportions.
- **Missing context** — no unit, no comparison, no source.

## Data quality at the source

Errors are cheapest to prevent where data is entered. Define each field and its owner, use input rules such as drop-down lists and range checks, and add automated plausibility checks before a report is published. Cleaning the same errors every month is a sign that the process, not the spreadsheet, needs fixing.

## Samples and representativeness

A survey answered by 40 of 2,000 customers — mostly those who had just complained — says something about these customers, not about all. Small and **self-selected samples** are biased. State such limitations openly and, if the decision matters, collect better data.

## Worked example

A dashboard suggests that remote workers produce fewer errors. Before the management meeting, Lukas checks: remote work was mainly approved for experienced staff. He presents the association with this caveat and proposes a three-month comparison of similar teams.

## Key takeaways

- Association ≠ cause; look for third factors and fair comparisons.
- Honest charts: zero-based bars, full context, no 3D.
- Fix data quality where data is entered.
- Name sample limitations openly.
