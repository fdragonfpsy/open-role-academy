## The short-term liquidity plan

A **liquidity plan** (cash forecast) sets all expected cash inflows against all expected outflows over a planning period. The German source describes the horizon as usually from one day to twelve months, and calls it a typical example of **rolling planning**: the plan is continuously updated rather than made once. A common practitioner format is a weekly plan for the next several weeks that you update every week: drop the week that has passed, add a new one at the end, and replace forecasts with actuals.

Good inputs come from early indicators: the same source names incoming orders as an early indicator of later revenue and purchase orders as an early indicator of later payments. Depreciation is not a cash item and does not belong in the plan. Shortfalls found in the plan can be covered by committed credit lines; both a surplus and a deficit should be avoided.

## Working capital

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. The accounts managers influence most directly are cash, accounts receivable, inventory and accounts payable. The management of working capital means managing inventories, receivables, payables and cash, so that the company can meet short-term debt and operating expenses. Money tied up in receivables and inventory is not available for payments; payables work the other way, because you hold the supplier's money until you pay.

The **cash conversion cycle** measures the days between paying out cash and collecting cash. It is built from the inventory period, the receivables period and the payables period (days inventory outstanding, days sales outstanding, days payables outstanding): inventory days plus receivables days minus payables days. A shorter cycle means cash is tied up for less time.

## Payment timing and discounts

Payment timing is a lever: paying on the due date keeps cash longer than paying early. A supplier discount (Skonto) changes the calculation: it rewards paying within a short period. Compare the saving with your own financing costs, and note that the discount deadline, not the net due date, then drives the payment date. Do not pay late by habit: a policy of strict collections and lax payments is described as not always sustainable.

## Payment runs and approvals

A payment run bundles due invoices into payments. For cash planning, the run dates are your outflow dates, so the plan should list each run with its total. Approvals matter twice: they prevent wrong payments, and an invoice stuck in approval can make the plan wrong. Use the four-eyes principle for release, and agree which person may release which amount according to your company policy.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented. Week 1 opening balance: 50,000. Expected inflows: 35,000 from customers. Planned outflows: a payment run of 28,000 on Wednesday and salaries of 30,000 on Friday. Closing balance: 50,000 + 35,000 - 28,000 - 30,000 = 27,000. In week 2 a customer who owes 20,000 announces a delay of one week. Replace the forecast and check the lowest balance of the week. If it is too low, move a non-urgent payment to its due date or ask for a bank line before you need it.

## Key takeaways

- A liquidity plan is a rolling plan; update it with actuals regularly.
- Orders and purchase orders are early indicators of cash movements; depreciation is not cash.
- Working capital links cash, receivables, inventory and payables.
- The cash conversion cycle adds inventory and receivable days and subtracts payable days.
- Payment runs and approvals set your outflow dates; use discounts only after comparing financing costs.
