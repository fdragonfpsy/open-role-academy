## Why this matters

Financial accounting reports a company's results to people outside it. **Cost accounting** looks inward. According to the Cost accounting article, it is the process of recording, classifying, analysing, allocating and reporting an organisation's costs, mainly to give managers information for planning, controlling operations, evaluating performance, pricing and other decisions. In finance and accounting you will often be asked "what does this really cost?", and this unit gives you the basic vocabulary to answer.

## Fixed and variable costs

Costs can be classified in several ways. One of the most common splits is by how they react to volume.

- **Variable costs** change as the quantity of goods or services produced changes. Typical examples are materials and the energy used per unit produced.
- **Fixed costs** do not depend on the level of output in the period considered. Typical examples are rent, interest and depreciation of equipment.

Together, fixed and variable costs make up the total cost. A useful habit: ask "if we produced twice as much next month, would this cost change?" If not, it behaves as a fixed cost in that period. Over a long enough time almost any cost can change, so the split always refers to a defined period and a defined activity level.

## Direct and indirect costs

The second common split is by how easily a cost can be traced to something. **Direct costs** can easily be associated with a particular cost object; **indirect costs** cannot, and are often called overheads. Be careful not to mix up the two splits: according to the Variable cost article, not all variable costs are direct costs. For example, variable manufacturing overhead is variable but indirect.

## Cost centres and cost objects

A **cost centre** is an activity or department to which costs can be apportioned or allocated. Cost centres such as an IT department or a warehouse do not sell anything directly; they cause costs, and their managers are responsible for those costs. A **cost object** is whatever you want to know the cost of, such as a product, a service or a customer order. In German cost accounting these are called Kostenstellen and Kostenträger. A simple way to remember: the cost centre answers "where did the cost arise?", the cost object answers "what was it for?".

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented. A small bakery bakes 1,000 loaves in a month.

| Cost | Amount | Fixed or variable | Direct or indirect for "loaves" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour and yeast | 600 | variable | direct |
| Oven electricity | 150 | variable | indirect (shared with rolls) |
| Shop rent | 900 | fixed | indirect |
| Baker's salary for loaves only | 1,200 | fixed | direct |

Total cost for the month is 600 + 150 + 900 + 1,200 = 2,850. Variable costs total 750 and fixed costs total 2,100. If the bakery bakes 2,000 loaves next month, the variable costs would roughly double while the rent stays the same, so the cost per loaf is not simply constant. The rent would sit in a cost centre such as "Shop", and the cost object is the loaf.

## Key takeaways

- Cost accounting serves internal management: planning, control, performance and pricing.
- Fixed costs stay the same with output in a period; variable costs move with it.
- Direct costs trace easily to a cost object; indirect costs (overheads) do not.
- Fixed/variable and direct/indirect are different splits; a cost can be variable and indirect.
- A cost centre shows where costs arise; a cost object shows what they are for.
