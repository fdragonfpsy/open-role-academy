## Why this matters

Once you know the vocabulary, daily questions follow: may we process this data, what do we do with a request from a person, and who is allowed to handle data on our behalf? This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## Lawful bases

The GDPR requires a lawful basis for each processing purpose. At concept level the regulation names six: the person's **consent**, performance of a **contract** with the person (or steps before it), a **legal obligation**, protection of **vital interests**, a **task in the public interest**, and **legitimate interests** of the controller or a third party, balanced against the person's interests. Choose and record the basis before processing, per purpose. Consent has strict conditions: the EDPB guidelines on consent (German version linked) describe that it must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and that withdrawing must be as easy as giving it. Special categories of data, such as health data, have additional conditions.

## Data subject rights

People whose data you process have rights, in concept: to be informed, to access their data, to have errors corrected, to erasure in certain cases, to restrict processing, to receive data in a portable form and to object in certain cases. Internal handling, as good practice: have one intake channel, log the date of receipt, verify identity in a proportionate way, involve the data protection contact early, follow the response period named in the regulation text (check it there) and keep a record of the reply.

## Processors

When another organisation processes personal data for you, a written agreement is required that fixes subject matter, duration, kind of data, the processor's duties and your right to give instructions. The German article on Auftragsverarbeitung describes the relationship. The processor may engage sub-processors only under conditions. Your tasks: choose carefully, sign the agreement, keep it on file and review it when the service changes.

## Record of processing activities

Controllers generally keep a record describing, for each processing activity, purposes, categories of data and persons, recipients, retention and security measures. Use it as the base for answering questions, reviewing bases and finding which processors you have.

## Germany adds detail

The German federal data protection statute (BDSG) and state laws supplement the regulation, for example on employee data and on supervisory authorities. Check which authority is responsible for your organisation.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented person and company. A former customer writes: "Please delete everything you have on me." Steps: log receipt, check identity, find the systems that hold data (use the record), check whether any legal obligation requires keeping some data, involve the data protection contact, answer with what was deleted, what was kept and why, and file the reply. You do not decide edge cases alone.

## Key takeaways

- Each purpose needs one lawful basis; record it before processing.
- Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and easy to withdraw.
- Handle requests through a defined process; check deadlines in the regulation.
- Processors need a written agreement; keep a record of processing activities.
