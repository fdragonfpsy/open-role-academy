## Why this matters

When you lead topics or shape a team's process, privacy stops being a list of dos and don'ts and becomes a design question: who can see what, for how long, and what happens when something goes wrong. This unit stays at topic level for the EU. Decisions about legal bases, retention periods and notifications belong to the company's data protection officer and legal team; you provide the facts and a workable process.

## Design the process, not only the person

The GDPR asks controllers to build data protection into processing and to use privacy-friendly defaults: by default, only data necessary for each purpose is processed, which covers how much is collected, how widely it is processed, how long it is stored and who can access it. Security of processing must fit the risk. In a service team this translates into design questions:

- Access: do all agents need to see payment data, or only a trained subset? Can tools show masked values?
- Retention: how long do tickets, recordings and chat logs stay, and who deletes them?
- Forms: does each field have a purpose, or was it added "just in case"?
- Suppliers: when a ticket tool or outsourced contact centre processes customer data for you, the company uses a processor and needs a proper arrangement. Raise it with the privacy team whenever a new tool is proposed.

## Triage of a suspected breach

A personal data breach is a breach of security leading to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration or unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data. Examples: an email with a customer list sent to the wrong person, a stolen laptop, access by someone without a role. The company must notify the supervisory authority without undue delay and where feasible within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless a risk to people is unlikely; if the risk is high, the people affected must also be informed in clear language. Every breach must be documented. Service staff are often the first to notice, so our own triage note has five facts: what happened, when it was noticed, which data and how many people, what has been done already, and who knows. Send it at once; do not wait to understand the cause fully, and do not try to hide or repair the mistake silently.

## Identity checks proportionate to risk (our own)

Our own principle: the more harm a wrong disclosure could cause, the stronger the check. Reading out an order status needs less than changing a bank detail or an email address. For requests about personal data, the company may ask for further information where doubts remain, but should not ask for more data than needed for the check. A request log, listing date received, channel, type, owner, due date and outcome, keeps the one-month period visible; review it weekly.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A new agent sends a refund list with 40 customer names and IBAN endings to the wrong external address. The agent sends the five-fact note to the lead within minutes. The privacy team assesses the risk, decides on notification and documents the case. Later the team lead moves the export function to a role with training and replaces the list with a masked view. The quick message saved time in the 72-hour window; the redesign reduced the chance of a repeat.

## Key takeaways

- Build privacy into defaults: limited access, short retention, purposeful forms.
- A suspected breach is reported at once with five facts; others decide on notification.
- The authority notice has a 72-hour target counted from the company's awareness.
- Match the strength of identity checks to the possible harm.
- Keep a request log so deadlines stay visible; ask the data protection officer on legal questions.
