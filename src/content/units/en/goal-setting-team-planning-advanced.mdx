## Why this matters

With experience, the hard part of planning is no longer writing a goal but aligning goals across levels, saying no and changing plans in a controlled way. This unit looks at these topics at concept level. It describes common practice; for company-specific rules (for example bonus systems) follow your organisation's policy and involve HR.

## Aligning across levels

The OpenStax chapter on goals describes goals in a hierarchy. The German article on OKR describes how objectives are first set at company level and then derived by each unit and team. In practice cascading is rarely perfect: team goals may conflict with those of another team. Make dependencies visible: list what your team needs from others and what others expect from you, and discuss conflicts early with the peer or the manager who can decide.

## Agreeing versus setting goals

The German Wikipedia article on Zielvereinbarung distinguishes a goal agreement (both sides agree on the goals) from a Zielvorgabe (the manager sets them unilaterally), and notes that in Germany an employer's right to give instructions can cover one-sided goal setting. Do not treat this as legal advice: rules on goals linked to pay, works councils and fixed agreements differ and need HR or legal input. As a rule of thumb, goals that people have helped shape are easier to commit to, while some goals simply come from above and need an honest explanation.

## Measures and their risks

Numbers make progress visible but can be misleading. People tend to optimise what is measured, even at the cost of what is not. Wikipedia's OKR article stresses that key results should be measurable without grey areas. Combine a number with a short check on quality: if the reply time improves, also look at whether answers are still helpful. Treat numbers as questions to ask, not as a verdict on people.

## Trade-offs and changing plans

The article on capacity planning shows that capacity is limited by constraints. If new work arrives, make the trade-off explicit: which goal slips, which work stops. Stopping work is a legitimate result of planning. Change the plan when facts change (a priority shifts, capacity drops, an assumption proves wrong), not at every request. Record the change, tell the team and the people affected, and adjust expectations.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented organisation. Halfway through the quarter, a key colleague leaves Dana's team, reducing capacity by about a sixth. Two goals compete for the rest. Dana presents her manager with options: delay the new help-centre goal or accept a slower reply time. They decide to postpone the help-centre goal to next quarter. Dana informs the team, the affected stakeholders, and updates the written plan.

## Key takeaways

- Make dependencies and goal conflicts across teams visible early.
- Agreeing goals differs from setting them; pay-related rules in Germany need HR or legal input.
- Numbers need a quality check; use them as questions, not verdicts.
- Change plans when facts change, make trade-offs explicit and communicate them.
