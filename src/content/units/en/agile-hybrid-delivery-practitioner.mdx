## Why this matters

Few real projects are purely agile or purely plan-driven. Contracts have dates, hardware has lead times, regulators have deadlines, and at the same time the details of what users need appear only along the way. A hybrid approach combines elements on purpose. The skill is to decide where each fits and to keep the pieces connected.

## Where each approach fits (our own checklist)

| Question | Leans to a fixed plan | Leans to iteration |
|---|---|---|
| How certain are the requirements? | Well understood | Unclear or changing |
| How expensive is a change later? | Very expensive | Cheap |
| Can users give early feedback? | Hardly | Yes |
| Is there a fixed external date? | Yes | No |

Answer the questions per part of the project, not for the whole. A common split: the frame (budget, final date, approvals, procurement) is planned, while the content of the work inside the frame is organised in iterations. Some descriptions of agile working argue that more detailed planning gets you what was planned, not necessarily what is needed. Planning still has value where decisions are hard to reverse.

## Where planning happens

When there is much uncertainty and rapid change, planning and controlling move closer to the people who carry out the work, who rely more on their own judgement and less on a distant hierarchy. In a hybrid setting this means: the sponsor and you set goals, limits and milestones; the team plans and steers its iterations inside them.

## Connecting the two

- Milestones: express them as outcomes ("booking usable by staff"), and let the backlog show which items lead there.
- Budget: fund the team for a period and review after each iteration, instead of fixing every item's price.
- Reporting: report what was delivered and accepted per iteration and what remains in the backlog, in place of percent complete.
- Changes: new items enter the backlog; the sponsor decides when the frame itself must change.

## Reviews and retrospectives

The review checks the product against real feedback from stakeholders. The retrospective checks the way of working: choose one or two improvements, assign owners and check them next time. A retrospective without a follow-up is a meeting that changes nothing.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. A bank branch must launch a customer portal before a regulatory date in October (fixed). Interface to the core system has a procurement lead time of eight weeks (planned). Screens and flows are unclear, so they are built in two-week iterations with customer feedback. Reporting to the sponsor shows the milestone "portal live by 1 October", the delivered and accepted backlog items, and the risk that the interface arrives late.

## Key takeaways

- Decide per part of the project whether a fixed plan or iteration fits.
- Plan the frame, organise the content in iterations, and link them through outcome-based milestones.
- Report delivered and accepted outcomes, not only effort spent.
- A retrospective counts only if it leads to owned, checked improvements.
