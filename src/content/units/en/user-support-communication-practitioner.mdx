## From answering to guiding

At practitioner level you handle situations where plain questions and answers are not enough: the user must do something on their own screen, the fix takes time, or the person is already upset. The skill is to stay clear and calm while someone else is doing the work.

## Guiding users remotely

**Remote desktop software** lets a technician see and, with permission, control another computer over a network. It is often the quickest way to fix a problem, but it requires trust, because you can see everything on the screen.

- **Ask for consent** every time and say what you will do: "May I connect to your screen to check the printer settings? You can end the session at any time."
- Ask the user to **close private or confidential content** before you connect.
- **Narrate** what you are doing so the user is not left staring at a moving mouse pointer, and say when you are done.
- Do only what you said. Do not browse around, and end the session when finished.
- Follow your organisation's rules on which tools are approved and how sessions are recorded.

When guiding by phone or chat instead, give **one step at a time** and wait for confirmation: "Click the Start button in the lower left corner. What do you see now?" Name things as they appear on the user's screen, not as you know them. Confirm each result before the next step, and check that you and the user are looking at the same thing.

## Manage expectations

Frustration often comes less from the problem than from not knowing how long it will last. Tell users:

- **What you can do and what you cannot do** (for example, you cannot recover a file that was never saved).
- **A realistic time frame**, based on the agreed priorities and service levels where they exist, rather than the most optimistic guess.
- **What they can do meanwhile**, such as a workaround.
- **When you will next be in touch.** Then do it, even if the news is "still working on it".

A promise you cannot keep is worse than an honest "I do not know yet, I will tell you by 3 pm".

## Frustrated users

An upset person is usually upset about the situation, not about you. A reliable sequence:

1. **Listen** without interrupting; let the person finish.
2. **Acknowledge** the effect: "That sounds frustrating, especially before a deadline."
3. **Stay calm** in tone and pace; do not match the other person's volume and do not argue about who is to blame.
4. **Move to action:** say what you will do now and what happens next.
5. **Know your limits:** if the person is abusive, follow your organisation's policy and involve a team lead.

These steps follow ideas from active listening and from conflict resolution: take the other person's concerns seriously and look for a shared next step rather than winning the exchange.

## Write a short how-to

When the same question returns, write a how-to. Title it with the user's goal ("Connect to the office printer"), list prerequisites, give numbered steps with one action each, say what the user should see after key steps, and add what to do if it does not work. Test it by following it yourself, ideally by having someone else try.

## Worked example

Mr Becker calls: his VPN fails, a customer presentation starts in 30 minutes, and he is angry. Lena first listens, then says: "I understand, with a presentation in 30 minutes that is stressful. I would like to connect to your screen to check the VPN settings — is that okay?" He agrees; she says what she does while she works, finds an expired certificate, renews it and asks him to try. It works. Afterwards she writes a how-to, "What to do when the VPN certificate expires", and links it in the ticket.

## Key takeaways

- Remote access needs consent, narration and restraint.
- Step-by-step guidance: one action, one confirmation.
- Honest time frames and a named next contact moment reduce frustration.
- Listen, acknowledge, stay calm, move to action.
- Turn repeated questions into tested, numbered how-tos.
