## Why this matters

In Legal & Compliance, almost every question starts with "which rule applies?" Before you can answer, you need a map of where rules come from. This unit gives you that map for Germany and the EU. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## What a source of law is

A source of law is the place a legal rule comes from. The English Wikipedia article on sources of law describes them as the origin of rules that bind people and organisations; the German article on Rechtsquelle makes the same point and separates written from unwritten sources. For orientation, think of five groups.

## The main groups

- **The constitution.** In Germany this is the Grundgesetz. It sets the frame for all other law.
- **EU law.** Primary law (the treaties) and secondary law such as regulations and directives. According to the EU portal, a regulation is a binding legislative act that must be applied in its entirety across the EU, while a directive sets a goal that EU countries must achieve and leaves it to each country to decide how, usually through its own national law. A decision is binding on those it is addressed to; a recommendation is not binding.
- **Statutes.** Laws passed by the federal parliament (Bundesrecht) or by a state parliament (Landesrecht), plus regulations issued by authorities on the basis of a statute.
- **Case law.** Court decisions. In Germany they are not a formal source of law in the same way as statutes, but courts interpret statutes, and their reasoning guides everyday practice.
- **Contracts and internal rules.** Agreements between parties bind those parties. They sit below statutes: a contract cannot override mandatory law.

## Rank matters

When two rules point in different directions, the higher-ranking rule generally prevails. EU law ranks above national law, and federal law ranks above state law. A company policy or contract sits at the bottom of this ladder. You will meet this idea again in the next units; for now remember: always ask "which level is this rule on?"

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company, no real figures. A colleague says: "Our supplier contract says we may store customer data indefinitely, so we are fine." Step 1: the contract is a rule on the lowest level. Step 2: ask whether higher-ranking rules, such as EU data protection law and national statutes, set limits that the contract cannot remove. Step 3: if so, the contract clause does not settle the question. Step 4: note which sources you checked and hand the case to legal counsel.

## Key takeaways

- Rules come from several levels: constitution, EU law, federal and state statutes, case law, contracts.
- An EU regulation applies directly in its entirety; an EU directive sets a goal that each country puts into its own law.
- Higher-ranking rules generally prevail over lower-ranking ones.
- Always name the level of a rule before you rely on it, and cite the official source.
