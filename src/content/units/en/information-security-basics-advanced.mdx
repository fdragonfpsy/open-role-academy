## Security as a management task

At advanced level you lead topics, teams or processes. Information security then becomes a question of **decisions under uncertainty**: which risks matter most, which controls are proportionate, and how the organisation learns from incidents. You do not need to be a security specialist, but you need to work with them in a structured way.

## Thinking in risks

A risk combines two things: the **likelihood** that a threat exploits a vulnerability, and the **impact** if it happens. Impact is more than hardware cost — it includes data loss, downtime, legal consequences and loss of trust. A simple risk matrix (for example low/medium/high for both dimensions) is often enough to prioritise.

For each relevant risk there are four basic options: **reduce** it with controls, **avoid** it by not doing the risky activity, **transfer** part of it (for example via contracts or insurance), or **accept** it consciously. Accepting a risk is legitimate, as long as the right person decides it knowingly and the decision is documented.

## Management systems: ISO/IEC 27001 and IT-Grundschutz

An **information security management system (ISMS)** turns security into a managed, repeatable process: clear responsibilities, risk assessment, selected controls, regular review and continuous improvement. Widely used frameworks are the international standard **ISO/IEC 27001** and the **IT-Grundschutz** methodology of the German Federal Office for Information Security (BSI). According to the BSI, IT-Grundschutz covers technical, organisational, infrastructural and personnel aspects and is compatible with ISO/IEC 27001. No ISMS can guarantee that incidents never happen — its value lies in handling security systematically.

## Defence in depth

No single control is perfect. **Defence in depth** layers independent controls so that one failure does not immediately lead to compromise. Against account takeover, for example, you might combine phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, e-mail filtering, an easy reporting button for staff, monitoring of unusual logins and a rehearsed response process. Think in three functions: **prevent, detect, respond**.

## Learning from incidents

Incident handling is commonly described in phases from preparation through identification, containment, eradication and recovery to **lessons learned**. The last phase is the one most often skipped. A **blameless review** asks what happened, why the controls did not stop it, and what will change. Blaming the person who clicked discourages future reporting — and early reporting is one of your best detection controls.

## Worked example

After a phishing incident, team lead Sara runs a review. The finding: the compromised account had far more rights than needed, and the report came late because staff feared consequences. Actions: access review, phishing-resistant MFA for admin accounts, a one-click report button and a clear message that reporting is always welcome.

## Key takeaways

- Prioritise by likelihood × impact; decide consciously how to treat each risk.
- An ISMS makes security a continuous management process.
- Layer controls: prevent, detect, respond.
- Close every incident with a blameless lessons-learned review.
