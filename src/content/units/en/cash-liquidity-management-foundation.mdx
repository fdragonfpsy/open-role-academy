## Why this matters

A company can be profitable and still run out of money. Working capital is described as the operating liquidity available to a business, and the same source notes that a company can have assets and profitability but fall short of liquidity if its assets cannot be readily converted into cash. **Liquidity** here means: can we pay what is due, when it is due? Cash and liquidity management is the daily work of answering that question.

## Cash versus profit

**Profit** comes from the income statement: revenue minus expenses, recorded when they are earned or incurred. **Cash** is the money actually in the bank and in the till. The two differ in timing. An invoice you send in March raises revenue in March, but the cash arrives only when the customer pays. A supplier invoice raises an expense and a liability when you receive the goods, but the cash leaves later when you pay it. So profit says how the business performed; cash says whether it can pay its bills today.

## Inflows and outflows

A cash inflow is money coming in, for example customer payments, loan proceeds or interest received. A cash outflow is money going out, for example supplier payments, salaries, rent, taxes and loan repayments. The difference over a period is the **net cash flow**. Timing is key: many small outflows on the same day can exceed the balance even if the month as a whole is positive.

## The bank balance and the daily cash position

Banks offer balance reporting to business customers: online access to account and transaction information, sometimes including several accounts. In many companies someone starts each day by looking at the bank balances and builds the **cash position**: the money available today, plus expected inflows and minus payments that are certain to leave. Be careful with what the balance includes. A booked balance may not yet show payments you have issued or amounts still being collected. Comparing the bank statement with your own records (bank reconciliation) explains the differences and shows whether the books are right.

## Why liquidity matters

One definition of funding liquidity risk is the risk that liabilities cannot be met when they fall due, or can only be met at an uneconomic price. It can arise from sudden unexpected cash outflows. In practice: late salaries, late supplier payments and expensive emergency borrowing. Good liquidity management makes these surprises rare.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented. On Monday morning the bank balance is 40,000. Customers are expected to pay 15,000 this week. Payments due this week: salaries 30,000 on Thursday and suppliers 18,000 on Wednesday.

| Day | Inflow | Outflow | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday (start) | | | 40,000 |
| Wednesday | | 18,000 | 22,000 |
| Thursday | | 30,000 | -8,000 |
| Friday | 15,000 | | 7,000 |

The week ends positive, but Thursday is short by 8,000. The team would act early: ask to bring the customer payment forward, move a supplier payment to its due date, or talk to the bank about an agreed overdraft, rather than discover the gap on Thursday.

## Key takeaways

- Profit and cash differ because of timing; a profitable business can run short of cash.
- Inflows and outflows should be looked at by day, not only per month.
- The daily cash position starts from bank balances and adds known payments.
- Reconcile bank and books so the numbers you plan with are right.
- Liquidity means being able to meet payments when they fall due.
