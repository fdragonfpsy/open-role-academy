## Why a method beats guessing

When something breaks, it is tempting to try things at random — restart, reinstall, replace. Sometimes that works, but often it wastes time, destroys clues or creates new problems. Structured troubleshooting is faster on average and gives you something to learn from. At foundation level, four habits make the biggest difference.

## 1. Reproduce the problem

A problem you can **trigger on demand** is much easier to isolate — and to confirm as fixed. Ask: Which action causes it? With which file, which account, at what time? Does it happen on another device or for another user? Write down **expected behaviour** ("the invoice opens") and **actual behaviour** ("error 0x80 after 5 seconds"), including the exact error message.

## 2. Ask what changed

If it worked yesterday and fails today, something changed: an update, a new cable, a moved device, a changed setting, a new password. Recent changes are strong leads. Treat them as **hypotheses to test** — something that changed at the same time is not automatically the cause.

## 3. Check the simple and likely causes first

Start with checks that are quick, cheap and often the reason: Is it switched on? Are cables firmly connected? Is the right input or network selected? Is the user logged in with the right account? Only then move on to complex causes. Experienced technicians are not embarrassed by simple checks — they do them first because they so often solve the problem.

## 4. One change at a time — and take notes

When you try a fix, change **one thing**, test, and note what you did and what happened. If you change three things at once and the problem disappears, you do not know what helped — and if it gets worse, you do not know what to undo. Notes also help when a ticket is handed over.

## Worked example

Paul cannot print since this morning. Mara asks him to print a test page from another programme: same error. A colleague on the same floor can print. Mara notices that Paul's laptop received an update overnight and that his default printer has switched to "PDF printer". She resets the default, prints a test page and notes the cause in the ticket.

## Key takeaways

- Reproduce the problem; note expected vs. actual behaviour and the exact error.
- Ask what changed — and test it, do not assume it.
- Simple and likely causes first.
- One change at a time; write it down.
