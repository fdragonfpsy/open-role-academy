## Why this matters

Corruption cases often begin with small, ordinary-looking situations: a gift, a dinner invitation, a favour for a friend. Recognising them early is the first line of prevention. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## What bribery and corruption are

The Wikipedia article on bribery describes bribery as offering, giving, receiving or asking for something of value to influence the actions of someone in a position of trust or authority. The German article on Korruption describes corruption more broadly as the abuse of an entrusted position for personal advantage. Both usually involve a benefit that is not due and a decision that should have been made on its merits. Bribery can involve public officials or private business partners.

## Typical red flags

- **Gifts and hospitality:** valuable, frequent, or offered shortly before a decision (for example a tender or a contract renewal).
- **Cash or unusual payments:** requests to pay to a different person, country or account, or without a proper invoice.
- **Conflicts of interest:** the articles on conflict of interest describe a situation where a private interest could influence professional duties. Typical examples are a relative among suppliers or a side job with a business partner.
- **Third parties:** agents, consultants and intermediaries who act for the organisation can create risk if their tasks or payments are unclear.
- **Pressure and secrecy:** requests to avoid documentation or to decide fast without review.

## What to do

Do not accept or offer anything that could look like influence. Check the gift and hospitality policy and the code of conduct. Disclose potential conflicts of interest to your manager or compliance contact and step back from the decision until it is clarified. Document what happened and ask if unsure.

## Speaking up is protected

The European Commission page on whistleblower protection says that Directive (EU) 2019/1937 sets minimum standards for protecting people who report breaches of EU law. It describes that Member States must ensure effective channels to report confidentially, inside an organisation and to authorities, that reports are followed up, and that whistleblowers are protected from retaliation. Which concerns are covered in Germany, and how, is set by the national implementing law; ask your compliance or legal contact. If you see a red flag, use the internal reporting channel your organisation offers.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A buyer receives an invitation from a tendering supplier to an expensive weekend event; the invitation arrives two weeks before the tender decision. The buyer recognises the red flags: high value and close timing. The buyer declines politely, tells the compliance contact and records the invitation. The supplier stays in the tender, but the invitation is noted in the file. The buyer did not need to prove anything; it was enough to notice, decline and report.

## Key takeaways

- Bribery involves giving or receiving an undue benefit to influence a decision.
- Gifts, hospitality, conflicts of interest, odd payments and third parties are typical red flags.
- Disclose and step back instead of deciding alone.
- EU law protects people who report breaches in good faith through proper channels.
