## Why this matters

At advanced level you design and improve the journey, not just run it. You decide what to measure, how to hand over knowledge, how to treat difficult exits and how to protect the data that all this produces. This unit contains no deadlines, notice or probation periods, thresholds, statistics, benchmarks or section numbers: these depend on current law, collective agreements, works agreements and the case. Check current law, your works agreement or legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice.

## Designing and improving the journey

Map the journey from first contact to the end of the relationship and ask at each step: who owns it, what does the person need, what could go wrong? The German onboarding article describes psychological phases in which expectations meet reality and may lead to disappointment; the German article on Einarbeitung calls this a "reality shock" risk and says evaluation should include surveying new employees, especially after early exits. Use feedback from new joiners, managers and leavers as input for changes.

Use feedback data carefully: aggregate it, avoid identifiable quotes and tell people how it is used.

## Measuring process quality without benchmarks

Do not import numbers such as "good companies achieve X". Instead, define your own process measures and compare yourself with yourself over time: Was the workplace ready on day one? Did the newcomer have a named contact? Were check-ins held? Was access removed on the agreed date? Were all returned items recorded?

## Knowledge transfer on exit

The offboarding article suggests documenting procedures, contact lists, file locations and status reports on ongoing work, and recommends doing this throughout employment rather than only at the end. The knowledge-transfer article describes transfer as moving facts or practical skills from one party to another, depending on the type of knowledge. Practically: separate what can be written down (procedures, locations, contacts) from what is learned by doing (judgement, relationships). For the second kind, plan time for shadowing, joint work or conversations, and name who receives the knowledge.

## Difficult exits

Dismissals, conflicts and disputes need extra care. The Betriebsrat article describes works-council participation in personnel matters such as dismissals. Whether and how a works council, a data protection officer or legal counsel must be involved depends on your country, agreements and the case. Your role: stay factual, document facts neutrally, involve the right people early, protect the person's dignity, and do not promise outcomes.

## Data protection in onboarding and exit data

The Arbeitnehmerdatenschutz article says processing of employee data is permitted only as far as it is necessary for the employment relationship, that personnel files are to be kept carefully, that health data does not belong in the regular part of the file, and that applicants and former employees count as employees for protection purposes. It also says the voluntariness of consent needs special checking because of the dependency in employment. The data protection officer article describes a DPO as someone who advises on data protection obligations and monitors compliance. For onboarding and exit data this means: collect only what you need, keep exit-interview notes separate from the personnel file unless a rule says otherwise, restrict access to those who need it, and check retention and deletion with your DPO and legal counsel.

## Consistency and inclusion across locations and remote work

Different sites, teams or remote setups should still offer comparable quality. Agree on a standard core (welcome, access, plan, check-ins, exit steps) and allow local differences where law or practice demands them; ask local contacts what applies. For remote joiners, send equipment early, schedule video introductions and name a buddy so that nobody learns the unwritten rules only by chance.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real figures. You review the onboarding journey in one department. A short survey of recent joiners and a conversation with their managers suggest that access requests arrive late for remote joiners. You do not conclude "onboarding is bad"; you check cases, find that the request form is not linked from the checklist, and adjust the process. At the same time, an exit interview note has identified a person: you move it to a restricted location and summarise only themes. Before a conflict-driven exit, you inform your HR lead and involve legal counsel; the practical steps follow their guidance.

## Key takeaways

- Design the journey end to end, with owners and feedback at each step.
- Use feedback data carefully: aggregate, protect identities and treat results as signals.
- Measure your own process questions over time; do not invent or import benchmarks.
- Knowledge transfer: separate writable from learned-by-doing knowledge and plan time for the latter.
- Difficult exits: facts, dignity, the right people early; legal steps belong to those responsible.
- Onboarding and exit data are personal data: minimise, restrict, check with your DPO.
