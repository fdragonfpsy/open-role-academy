## Why this matters

Good goals still fail if the team has no time to reach them. This unit connects goals with capacity and a regular rhythm of planning and review. It stays at concept level and does not prescribe a tool.

## OKR at concept level

The Wikipedia article on objectives and key results describes OKR as a goal-setting framework that combines an objective (a significant, concrete, clearly defined goal) with a few measurable key results (it mentions three to five). It attributes the development to Andrew Grove at Intel in the 1970s and notes his 1983 book High Output Management. The German article describes OKR as a method that splits goals into a qualitative objective and quantitative key results and mentions that cycles are typically three months, with progress and confidence reviewed weekly. Use the idea selectively: one inspiring objective, a few numbers that show progress, and a regular review. Whether you call them OKR, SMART goals or something else matters less than clarity.

## Capacity planning

The Wikipedia article on capacity planning distinguishes design capacity (the maximum amount of work possible in a period) from effective capacity (what is possible given constraints such as quality problems and delays). The distinction helps a lead: your team's effective capacity is lower than the hours on the contracts.

Simple steps:

1. List the available working time for the period, minus holidays, training, meetings and known absence.
2. List the work: planned goals, recurring tasks and an allowance for unplanned requests.
3. Compare. If the work is larger than the capacity, remove or postpone items rather than hoping for the best.
4. Keep a buffer; plans at 100 percent break at the first surprise.

## A planning rhythm

Pick a rhythm that fits your work, for example a short weekly planning, a monthly review of goals and a quarterly re-planning. In each, look at what was done, what is blocked, what changed and what to adjust. Short, regular reviews are cheaper than large corrections late. The OpenStax chapter on the planning process describes planning as steps from awareness of the present situation to outcomes and actions.

## Prioritising in a conflict

When new work arrives, ask: what is the value, what is the deadline, who decides? Put the trade-off in front of the person who owns the priority, and write down the decision.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. A team of five has about 600 hours in the quarter on paper. After holidays, training and meetings, Ravi estimates roughly 450 hours; he sets aside about a fifth for unplanned requests. The goals he and the team had in mind need about 500 hours. Instead of squeezing, he shows the numbers to his manager, they postpone one goal, and the team reviews progress every Monday for 15 minutes.

## Key takeaways

- OKR: one clear objective with a few measurable key results, reviewed regularly.
- Effective capacity is lower than nominal capacity; plan with realistic numbers and a buffer.
- If work exceeds capacity, decide what to drop or delay, with the owner of the priority.
- Use a regular rhythm of short plans and reviews.
