Context: EU and Germany. This unit trains judgement; it is not legal advice. The texts linked are primary sources (EU) and overviews (German Wikipedia). Decisions in real cases belong to legal, data protection and compliance.

## Why this matters

At advanced level, you set examples and shape how a team sells. The difficult cases are rarely obviously illegal. They are grey: a free pilot and a hospitality invitation during an evaluation, a claim backed only by the best installations, a request about data that touches several teams. Good judgement means three things: seeing the issue early, knowing who decides, and leaving a record.

## Judging grey zones

Ask four questions. Is it true and complete? Could it influence a decision unfairly, now or later? Would it look acceptable if the customer, a competitor or a journalist read about it? Is there a written rule or approval path? If any answer is uncertain, escalate before acting; it is far cheaper than repairing it afterwards. Timing matters: during a tender or contract evaluation, gifts and hospitality carry more risk, so compliance should decide before you reply, not after the deal.

## Claims and scripts

Review claims against their evidence. The EU directive on unfair commercial practices treats information as misleading when the overall presentation deceives, even if each fact is correct, and it also covers omissions of material information. It does recognise that exaggerated statements not meant literally are legitimate advertising practice, but a concrete claim about savings or results is not in that category. The directive covers consumers; for business customers other rules, such as national unfair-competition law (in Germany, the UWG), can apply, so a script used for both groups should meet the stricter standard. Small print that contradicts the headline does not repair a misleading claim.

## Data requests and objections

Customers can object to direct marketing, and they have a right of access to their data. Sales should not answer these requests from personal notes. Route them quickly to the data protection contact, keep records complete and honest, and never delete or edit notes to avoid embarrassment. Concentrate on clear processes: one place for objections, one owner for requests, one audit trail.

## Incentives and culture

Compensation shapes behaviour. A commission-only structure can motivate, but it also carries a risk that the sales force uses aggressive practices to make sales. As a lead you can reduce that risk by combining bookings with quality measures such as retention and complaints, by reviewing unusual discounts and by making it normal to escalate. Corruption in Germany is addressed in several criminal offences, including bribery in business dealings; your role is to make the internal rules known and used, not to interpret the statutes.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented. During a tender evaluation, a prospect asks for a 12-month free pilot and also hints that its team would enjoy an expensive hospitality event. You answer neither on the spot. The pilot is a commercial term; you propose to offer it on written terms that other bidders could also get, and you ask your manager to approve it. The event request goes to compliance, which declines it until the evaluation ends. You record both decisions in the CRM. Later, a review of your team's script finds the claim "saves a third of your costs". The evidence covers the best installations only. You change the wording to what the data shows and ask legal to review it before further use.

## Key takeaways

- In grey zones, escalate early; compliance decides before you act, not after.
- Match each claim to its evidence; use the stricter standard for mixed audiences.
- Route data requests to the right owner and never alter records.
- Incentives need counterweights such as retention and complaint measures.
- Leave a written trail of approvals and decisions.
