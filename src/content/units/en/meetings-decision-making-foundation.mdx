## Why this matters

Meetings use the time of several people at once. A meeting without a clear purpose costs that time and often produces nothing anyone can act on. This unit covers the basics at concept level: purpose, agenda, roles, minutes and follow-up.

## Start with the purpose

The English Wikipedia article on meetings describes a meeting as a gathering with a specific agenda rather than people simply talking, and notes that meetings can serve as a form of group decision-making and as a setting for sharing information. The German article on the Besprechung, citing the educator Josef W. Seifert, distinguishes three kinds: exchanging information, preparing a decision, and solving a problem. Before you invite anyone, write one sentence: "After this meeting we will have ...". If your sentence is only "everyone knows X", a short written update may do the job. This is common practice, not a rule from the sources. If you need a decision or a joint solution, a conversation is worth the time.

## Prepare an agenda

The Wikipedia article on agendas explains that an agenda lists the items of business for a meeting and ideally reaches participants beforehand, so they can prepare. Items may be "time-boxed", which means each gets a fixed amount of time. The German article on the Tagesordnung adds that it helps a meeting stay efficient and goal-oriented and can prevent conversations from running on endlessly. A useful agenda item names the topic, the goal (inform, discuss or decide), the owner and the time.

## Roles

The German article on the Besprechung describes a meeting leader who prepares content, method, organisation and participants, invites people in good time and sets the agenda. It also mentions a person who writes the Protokoll (minutes) while the meeting runs. For a team meeting three roles are enough:

- **Lead or facilitator:** keeps to the agenda and the time and makes sure everyone can contribute.
- **Note-taker:** records decisions and actions.
- **Participants:** come prepared and stick to the topic.

You can rotate the note-taker role so that it does not always fall to the same person.

## Minutes and follow-up

The Wikipedia article on minutes calls them the written record of a meeting and says they are not a transcript. They mainly record what was decided, not what was said. The German article on the Protokoll names three components: when something happened, who was involved, and what happened. For a team meeting a few lines are enough: decisions, and for each action who does what by when. Send the notes soon after the meeting and open the next meeting by reviewing the open actions. Without follow-up, even a good meeting loses its value.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. Karin leads a five-person logistics team and wants a decision on the order of three process improvements. Her purpose sentence reads: "After the meeting we will have ranked the three ideas." She sends a three-item agenda the day before, each item with a goal and ten minutes, and asks Ole to take notes. In the meeting she notices that one topic is a pure information item and moves it to a short written note. The team ranks the ideas, and Ole records: "Decision: idea B first. Action: Amira drafts the steps by Thursday." Karin opens the next meeting with that action.

## Key takeaways

- State the purpose in one sentence; if no discussion or decision is needed, consider a written update instead.
- Send an agenda with topic, goal, owner and time beforehand.
- Give the meeting a lead and a note-taker.
- Minutes record decisions and actions, not a transcript, and the next meeting starts with them.
