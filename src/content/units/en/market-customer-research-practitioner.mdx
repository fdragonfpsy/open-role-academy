## Why this matters

Once the problem is defined, the method decides whether you can trust the result. OpenStax describes four research types: exploratory, descriptive, experimental (causal) and ethnographic. At practitioner level you pick the type that fits the question and run it cleanly.

## Matching method to question

- **Exploratory, qualitative**: you do not yet know what matters. Use interviews or focus groups. Results give context and wording but cannot be reduced to statistics.
- **Descriptive, quantitative**: you want to describe how common something is. A survey is the most common method.
- **Experimental**: you want to know whether A causes more than B. An A/B test, for example two versions of a page, compares groups under different treatments.
- **Ethnographic**: you observe behaviour in its natural environment, for example how people actually use a product.

Often the best sequence is qualitative first to find the right questions, then quantitative to measure them.

## Survey quality

Wikipedia's article on survey methodology covers sampling and questionnaire design. Practical rules:

- Ask one thing per question ('How satisfied are you with price and delivery?' mixes two).
- Avoid leading wording ('How much do you enjoy our excellent service?').
- Offer answer options that cover all cases, including 'does not apply'.
- Pre-test the survey on a few people.
- Ask who actually answered. If only your happiest customers reply, you measure happiness of repliers, not of the market.

## Personal data in research

If you collect data that identifies people, EU rules apply. The GDPR names principles in Article 5(1) such as purpose limitation and data minimisation: collect only what you need for the stated purpose. Anonymous surveys are simpler. If you need contact details, say why and for how long you keep them, and ask your data protection contact before launching. This unit does not give legal advice.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A meal-box service wants to learn why subscribers cancel. Round one: eight short interviews with former subscribers; common themes are 'too much food' and 'hard to skip a week'. Round two: a five-question survey to all former subscribers with those themes as answer options, one open box for 'other', and no contact details collected. The team notes that 30 of 200 replied and that replies skew towards recent cancellations, so it treats the findings as indications and plans a test of a skip-a-week function.

## Key takeaways

- Qualitative methods explore; quantitative methods measure; experiments test cause.
- Good questions are single, neutral and complete.
- Check who answered before you generalise.
- Collect only the personal data you need and ask your data protection contact first.
