## Why this matters

At advanced level the hard part is rarely the arithmetic. It is that goals conflict, that numbers rest on guesses and that a business case written in January describes a world that has changed by June. Your job is to keep the case honest and the decision revisable.

## Goals: official and operational

Organisations state general aims in public statements and set operational goals that say concretely what people should pursue. Official aims tend to be ambiguous; operational goals reflect management's specific intentions. Goals guide effort, motivate, shape planning and provide a yardstick for judging progress. Units inside an organisation may pursue goals that conflict, for example innovation in one department against efficiency in another. Our own check: take the project goal and ask which other team's goal it works against, then name that conflict in the case instead of hoping it does not appear.

## Assumptions and sensitivity (our own method)

A business case multiplies estimates. Our own method has three steps:

- List every assumption that a benefit or cost depends on.
- Give each a range (low, expected, high) instead of one value.
- Change one at a time and see which flips the decision.

The assumption that flips the decision deserves a test before approval, not a footnote. The cost-benefit logic stays the same: compare options on a common basis, including the time value of money.

## Stage decisions

A business case is a basis for decisions, and decisions can be taken in stages. Our own pattern: approve a small first stage with a fixed budget, define what must be learned, and bring an updated case to the next decision. The project order can record this by naming the review date and the criteria for continuing or stopping. Stopping a project whose case no longer holds is a good result, not a failure.

## When goals cannot be agreed

Planning can start from a specific goal, or from a direction in which many acceptable goals lie. Direction-based planning is typically chosen when flexibility is needed, when people cannot agree on goals, when the environment is uncertain or when an organisation is in transition. For a project this suggests a short exploratory first stage with a clear learning question instead of a detailed plan that pretends certainty.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A sponsor wants a new customer portal and expects payback within three years. The case rests on one assumption: 40 % of customers will use it. The team gives a range of 15 to 50 %. At 15 % the payback is about eight years. Instead of arguing about the number, the sponsor approves a three-month pilot with a fixed small budget and the criterion "at least 30 % of pilot customers use it weekly". The updated case is due at the next steering meeting, and the project order records both the criterion and the date.

## Key takeaways

- Name goal conflicts with other units in the case.
- Use ranges for assumptions and test the one that flips the decision.
- Approve in stages with criteria for continuing or stopping.
- When goals are disputed, start with a short exploratory stage.
