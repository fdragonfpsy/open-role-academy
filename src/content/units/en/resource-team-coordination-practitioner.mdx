## Why this matters

Once the work is assigned, two problems keep recurring: some people are asked for more than they can give, and a group of capable individuals does not automatically behave like a team. Both can be managed.

## Spotting and resolving over-allocation

Over-allocation means that more resources are needed in a period than are available. It shows up as a peak in a week-by-week load chart of a person or role. Resource leveling is the process of resolving such conflicts: tasks are moved so that demand fits supply, and this is usually paid for in time, cost or scope. Our own checklist of options, from cheapest to most expensive:

- Move a task with slack to a quieter week.
- Split the task or hand part of it to someone else with the skills.
- Add capacity: another person, overtime for a short period, an external provider.
- Reduce scope or accept a later finish.

Writing down which of the three constraints you gave up keeps the trade-off visible to the sponsor.

## Stages of team development

Bruce Tuckman proposed four stages in 1965: forming, storming, norming and performing. A fifth, adjourning, was added in 1977 for the end of a project. In forming, people are polite and look to the lead for structure. In storming, they compete for influence and frustration about goals and progress can grow. In norming, ground rules take hold and responsibility is shared. In performing, the team works largely self-directed.

| Stage | What the lead does (our own suggestion) |
|---|---|
| Forming | Give clear goals, roles and contact rules |
| Storming | Keep discussion on the task, make decisions visible, bring disagreement into the open |
| Norming | Confirm the agreed rules, hand over more responsibility |
| Performing | Remove obstacles, step back from daily steering |

The stages are not a straight line. A new member or a confusing new task can send the team back to an earlier stage, so expect it and plan a short reset conversation.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. Two weeks into a migration, the load chart shows the database specialist at 130 percent in week 6. The lead checks the options: one task has three days of slack and moves to week 5; a second part is handed to a colleague who has the skill. Load drops to 95 percent at the cost of the second task finishing a week later, which the lead writes into the plan. In the same week a new tester joins. Discussions about who decides get heated again, so the lead holds a half-hour session to restate roles and decision rules.

## Key takeaways

- Over-allocation is visible in a load chart; leveling resolves it at a price in time, cost or scope.
- Name the price you pay, so the sponsor can decide.
- Teams usually pass through forming, storming, norming and performing; expect friction in storming.
- Change in membership or tasks can set a team back; agree rules again after it.
