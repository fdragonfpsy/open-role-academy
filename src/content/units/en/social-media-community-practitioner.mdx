## Why this matters

At practitioner level you run a presence over months: you set goals, decide how to react to criticism, work with paid reach and influencers, and keep within platform and legal rules. This unit gives a working structure for those decisions.

## From goal to metric

The German Wikipedia article describes a social media strategy as the basis from which operative measures follow, such as building a community, monitoring and customer contact. Turn this into goals you can measure. OpenStax names clicks, likes and shares as engagement metrics and notes that traffic by source can be tracked, for example by tagging links, so you can see which visitors came from social media. A practical rule from this course (our own working method, not from the sources): give every goal one main metric. Awareness maps to reach, interest to clicks to your site, demand to conversions such as sign-ups. Likes alone rarely show business value.

## Paid reach and personal data

OpenStax describes paid social as paying platforms to show sponsored advertisements to targeted customers. It also explains that platforms can build lookalike audiences from an uploaded customer list. Uploading customer data and tracking visitors involve personal data. At topic level: the GDPR requires a lawful basis and data minimisation, and in the EU marketing and tracking rules are strict. Before you upload lists or add tracking, ask your data protection officer or legal counsel. Do not decide this alone.

## A reply protocol for comments and criticism

Because you do not control what users write, prepare. A short protocol (our own working method) has four parts: sort the comment (question, praise, criticism, abuse); answer factual questions publicly and briefly; move personal or account-related matters to a private channel; hide or report abuse according to your published community rules. Never delete justified criticism, answer it. When many critical comments arrive at once, stop scheduled promotional posts, agree one holding statement with the responsible person, and name one person who answers.

## Influencers and disclosure

The German Wikipedia article lists influencer marketing as one tactic. At topic level, the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive treats paid promotion that is not made clear as unfair: Annex I point 11 lists paid editorial content presented as editorial without making that clear, and Article 7 covers omitting the commercial intent of a practice if it is not apparent from the context. How exactly posts must be labelled in Germany depends on national law (the UWG) and case law. Ask legal counsel before a campaign starts and write the labelling rule into the briefing. Also, the German Wikipedia article notes that fake followers and likes can be bought. Do not buy them: they make your metrics worthless.

## Imprint and platform rules

Business profiles in Germany may need imprint information. Whether and how this applies to your profile is a question for legal counsel. Platform terms and advertising policies change often, so read the current ones before launching paid campaigns.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A Munich software provider for dental practices runs a professional-network page. Goal: 40 sign-ups for a webinar. Main metric: sign-ups, tracked via tagged links. A guest dentist posts about the webinar with a clear paid-partnership label, as agreed with legal counsel. A critical comment says the demo is confusing; the community manager thanks the person publicly, answers in two sentences and offers a call. The next demo is revised.

## Key takeaways

- Derive each social goal from the strategy and give it one main metric.
- Paid targeting and customer-list uploads involve personal data: involve data protection first.
- Prepare a reply protocol; answer criticism, never delete it without cause.
- Paid or incentivised promotion must be clearly identifiable; labelling details are legal questions.
- Fake followers and likes distort every metric.
