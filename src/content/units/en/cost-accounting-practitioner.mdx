## From classification to calculation

At foundation level you learned to classify costs. Now you use that classification to calculate. Cost accounting records, classifies, analyses, allocates and reports costs; this unit covers the "allocate" and "analyse" steps with small, transparent calculations.

## Allocating overheads with an allocation key

Indirect costs cannot be traced to a cost object directly, so they are **allocated** using an **allocation key**: a measurable quantity that you believe is related to how the cost arises. Examples are machine hours, floor area, headcount or number of orders. The rule is simple: choose a key, compute a rate, apply it.

Rate = overhead amount / total quantity of the key. Allocated amount = rate x quantity used by the cost object.

Be aware that every key is a simplification. Two reasonable keys can give different results, so state which key you used and why. The German Deckungsbeitrag article makes the same point: an allocation will never be perfect, and the more costs are allocated, the less transparent it becomes whether a product still covers its costs.

## Unit cost

A **unit cost** is the cost of one unit of a product or service. A simple version is:

Unit cost = (direct costs + allocated overhead) / number of units.

## Contribution margin and break-even

The **contribution margin** per unit is the selling price per unit minus the variable cost per unit. It is the part of revenue that is not used up by variable costs and therefore contributes to covering fixed costs. This is a key building block of break-even analysis. The **break-even point** is where total cost and total revenue are equal, so there is neither profit nor loss. For a single product with a constant price and constant variable cost per unit:

Break-even quantity = fixed costs / contribution margin per unit.

## Cost reports

A cost report for a cost centre usually shows actual costs by cost type next to the plan, the variance, and a short comment on causes. Because cost centre managers are responsible for their costs, the report should only show what they can influence, and should separate allocated amounts from their own costs.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented.

**Overhead allocation.** A workshop has 12,000 of maintenance overhead in a month. Product A used 300 machine hours and product B used 100. Total key quantity is 400 hours, so the rate is 12,000 / 400 = 30 per hour. Product A receives 300 x 30 = 9,000 and product B receives 100 x 30 = 3,000.

**Unit cost.** Product A had 1,500 units that month and direct costs of 12,000. Direct cost per unit is 12,000 / 1,500 = 8, allocated overhead per unit is 9,000 / 1,500 = 6, so the unit cost is 14.

**Break-even.** Product A sells for 20 per unit. Assume the variable cost per unit is 12 and fixed costs are 4,000 per month. Contribution margin per unit = 20 - 12 = 8. Break-even quantity = 4,000 / 8 = 500 units, which corresponds to revenue of 500 x 20 = 10,000. Selling 600 units would leave a profit of 100 x 8 = 800.

## Key takeaways

- Indirect costs are allocated with a stated key; every key is a simplification.
- Rate = overhead / total key quantity; allocation = rate x use.
- Unit cost = (direct costs + allocated overhead) / units.
- Contribution margin per unit = price - variable cost per unit.
- Break-even quantity = fixed costs / contribution margin per unit, under constant price and cost assumptions.
- A good cost report shows plan, actual, variance and cause, and separates controllable from allocated costs.
