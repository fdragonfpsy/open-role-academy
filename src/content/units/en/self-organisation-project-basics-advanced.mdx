## From running tasks to leading projects

At advanced level you lead larger projects or several small ones, often with people who do not report to you. Success depends less on tools than on **structure, risk awareness, the right approach and the will to learn**.

## Structure the scope

A **work breakdown structure (WBS)** decomposes the total scope hierarchically into deliverables and **work packages**. Each package can be estimated, assigned and scheduled — the WBS is therefore the basis for time and cost planning. A good rule: everything in scope appears in the WBS, and nothing outside it does.

## Manage risks, not only tasks

A **risk** is something that *might* happen and would affect the project; an **issue** has already happened. Keep a **risk register** with, for each risk: a description, likelihood, impact, planned measures and an owner. Review it regularly — for example in every steering meeting — because risks change as the project moves on.

## Engage stakeholders

Stakeholders are everyone who affects or is affected by the project. Identify them early, understand their interests and influence, and plan how you involve them: decision-makers need options and trade-offs, affected users need early information and a chance to give feedback. Surprises late in a project are usually stakeholder problems that started early.

## Choosing the approach

- **Plan-driven** approaches work well when requirements are stable and well known, for example when they are fixed by regulation.
- **Iterative, agile** approaches deliver in small steps and learn from feedback; they pay off when requirements are uncertain.

Many projects combine both: a fixed frame (budget, deadline, milestones) with iterative delivery inside it.

## Closing and learning

A project is not finished at go-live. A short, structured **lessons-learned** session asks what worked, what did not and what will change — and documents it where future projects will find it. Blaming individuals discourages honesty; adding blanket buffers without analysis hides the real causes.

## Worked example

Clara leads an office move for 200 people. Her WBS has five work packages, each with an owner. The risk register flags "IT network not ready" as likely and severe; she schedules an early network test. Works council and team leads are involved from week one. After the move, the lessons-learned session produces a checklist that the next location uses.

## Key takeaways

- WBS: scope broken into work packages — the basis for planning.
- Risks before they become issues: register, owners, regular review.
- Involve stakeholders early and deliberately.
- Fit the approach to the uncertainty; close with lessons learned.
