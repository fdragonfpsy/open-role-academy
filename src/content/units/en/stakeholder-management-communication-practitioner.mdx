## Why this matters

Many conflicts on projects come from different expectations, not from different facts. Tailoring what you tell each group, checking that it arrived and ending every report with the decisions you need prevent most of them.

## Groups and their needs

Stakeholders with similar information needs can be handled as one group. Sponsors and the project team need changes to the mandate and the plan, and regular news on status, progress, risks and cost. Affected business units mostly care about consequences for their own unit, such as resource needs, operating costs and interruptions. Users need to hear what changes, may need training, and should know what active support the project needs from them. Support staff need details on the product and the rollout schedule. Specialist bodies and suppliers have individual needs and are usually approached one by one. The general public needs only basic facts.

## Will the message arrive?

In a simple model of communication, a sender encodes an idea into words and a receiver decodes them. Both steps are shaped by earlier experience and by the situation at the time, and feedback closes the loop. A clear email can therefore still be understood differently. Our own habit: ask for feedback in the receiver's words, for instance "What will you do differently on Monday?"

## A status report that supports decisions (our own)

One page is enough:

- Overall status in one word with a one-sentence reason, and the trend since the last report.
- Progress against milestones, measured in finished results rather than effort.
- The top three risks and open issues, each with an owner.
- Decisions needed: what, from whom and by when.

## Meetings that produce results (our own)

- State the purpose and the expected result in the invitation; no result, no meeting.
- Share an agenda with times and invite only people who decide or contribute.
- Record decisions and actions (owner, date) during the meeting and send them within a day.
- Start the next meeting with the open actions.

## Managing expectations

Write down what is in scope, what is not and what a date means (planned, forecast or promised). Tell people the bad news early and together with options. Targeted, regular communication is what makes stakeholders feel involved instead of surprised.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. A finance system rollout has shown "green" for months, although two milestones have slipped. The new one-page report shows "amber: two milestones are two weeks late because test data arrived late; trend: worsening". Top risks and issues are listed with owners. The decision needed is for the sponsor to choose by Friday between moving go-live by two weeks and dropping one report from the first release. The weekly steering meeting lasts 30 minutes, uses this page as its agenda and ends with two recorded actions.

## Key takeaways

- Different groups need different information; plan content and channel per group.
- A message only counts once feedback shows that it was understood.
- A one-page status report ends with the decisions you need.
- Meetings end with decisions and actions with owners; tell stakeholders bad news early.
