## Why this matters

In larger setups work crosses teams, results depend on several people and things go wrong. A lead then needs shared vocabulary for accountability and a calm way to deal with failure. This unit builds on the practitioner level. It describes common management practice, not a legal analysis.

## Shared definitions

The Wikipedia article on accountability describes it as an account-giving relationship: A is accountable to B when A is obliged to inform B about actions and decisions and to justify them. It also links accountability to proper records. The responsibility assignment matrix names one accountable role per deliverable and makes clear that other roles do, advise or are informed. In your own team, agree on simple definitions and use them consistently: one person is accountable for a result, several may be responsible for parts of the work. If two people are "accountable" for the same result, ask who answers if it fails.

## Accountability without authority

The Wikipedia article on micromanagement describes a pattern in which accountability for failure is passed down without the authority to take alternative action. Check your delegations: does the person have the access, budget and decision rights that the result requires? If not, either extend the authority or reduce the expectation.

## When delegation goes wrong

The Wikipedia article on delegation lists "interpreting failure as a key to learning" among factors of effective delegation. Practical steps:

1. Stabilise first: limit damage and inform those affected.
2. Take your share: ask whether the briefing, the level of freedom or the check-ins were adequate.
3. Hold a short, factual review with the person: what happened, what helped, what changes.
4. Keep the task with the person if that is realistic; take it back only with a clear reason and say so.

## Delegation as development

Use delegation to build the team: hand over tasks slightly above current skill, with a named point of contact, and plan visible recognition. Watch workload: delegating more to your most reliable person can overload them. Keep a simple overview of who holds what and review it each quarter.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. A supplier report that Mehmet delegated contains a wrong total and reaches a customer. His lead Eva first has it corrected and informs the customer contact. In the review she finds that Mehmet had no access to the final data check and that the first check-in was set only after the deadline. They agree that Mehmet gets access, a pre-deadline check-in is added, and Eva will explain the correction to her own manager as the one accountable for the team's output.

## Key takeaways

- Define accountability (answering for a result) and responsibility (doing the work) consistently; one accountable person per result.
- Never give accountability without the authority and means needed.
- Handle failure by stabilising, reviewing your own part and learning, not by blame or reflexive take-over.
- Use delegation deliberately for development, and watch the workload.
