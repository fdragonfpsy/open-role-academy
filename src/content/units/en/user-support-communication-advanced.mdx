## From one conversation to a communication system

At advanced level you shape how the whole support team communicates: how problems are handed over, how many people are informed at once, and how what you learn flows back into better service.

## Escalation communication

An escalation passes a ticket to someone with more skill or authority. It fails when the next person has to start over. A good hand-over contains:

- **Summary in one or two sentences:** who is affected and what is impossible for them now.
- **Impact and urgency** in business terms, as defined by your priority scheme.
- **What was observed and tried,** with times and results, including what did not help.
- **What the user has been told,** so that the next contact does not contradict earlier messages.
- **What you need,** and by when: a decision, a fix, access rights.

Tell the user about the escalation too: who has the ticket now, what that means for timing, and that they do not have to explain everything again. Stay the user's point of contact if that is your team's rule; otherwise say clearly who is.

## Communicating during a major incident

A major incident affects many users or a critical service. In ITSM it is handled with extra priority and coordination. Communication has its own plan:

- **Audiences:** affected users, managers, the service desk, possibly customers. Each needs different detail.
- **First message early,** even if you only know "we are aware of the problem and are investigating". Silence makes people flood the service desk.
- **Fixed cadence:** announce when the next update will come and keep to it, even if there is no news.
- **Content:** what is affected, what users can do meanwhile, what is being done, when the next update comes. Leave out speculation, internal blame and unconfirmed causes.
- **One source of truth** (a status page or a pinned message) that the service desk also uses, so answers do not differ.
- **Closure:** announce the restoration, what users should check, and that a review will follow. Share conclusions afterwards in suitable detail.

Check the plan against agreed service levels, because commitments about response and resolution times shape what you may promise.

## Coaching users and knowledge articles

Good support makes itself less necessary. Coaching means explaining the why briefly ("this setting saves your files to the cloud, which is why they appear on your other laptop") and letting users do the next step themselves while you watch, instead of doing it for them. Do this when the user has time and interest; during a deadline emergency, fix first and offer the tip afterwards.

A **knowledge base** holds structured information that others can find and reuse. Keep articles useful by:

- writing them from the user's search words and the symptom, not from the internal cause;
- assigning an owner, a review date and a link to the tickets where they helped;
- retiring or updating articles when the system changes;
- keeping them short, with numbered steps and results to expect.

## Feedback loops

Treat communication as measurable. Short satisfaction questions after closure, the comments users write, reopened tickets and recurring questions all show what is unclear. Review a small sample regularly, look for patterns (the same article that does not help, the same message that causes confusion), change one thing, and see whether the signal improves. Share findings with the team without blaming individuals.

## Worked example

On Monday at 8:10 email is unavailable for the whole company. Samir opens a major incident, posts on the status page at 8:20 — "Email is currently unavailable. We are investigating. Next update at 8:50." — and briefs the service desk with the same text. At 8:50 he reports that a cause is not yet confirmed and suggests the web mail workaround, and at 9:30 he announces restoration. Afterwards he adds a knowledge article, "Email outage: what to do", and reads the 30 free-text comments from the satisfaction survey. Many say "I did not know where to look for news" — so he adds the status page link to the sign-in page.

## Key takeaways

- A good escalation lets the next person continue without asking the user again.
- Major incidents: early first message, fixed cadence, one source of truth, a clear closure.
- Coach when there is time; fix first when there is not.
- Knowledge articles need an owner, a review date and the user's words.
- Use feedback to change one thing and check the effect.
