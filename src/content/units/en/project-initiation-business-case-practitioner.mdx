## Why this matters

When three people propose three solutions, the loudest voice often wins. A short, fair comparison and a signed project order replace opinions with decisions that can be traced later.

## Cost-benefit view

Cost-benefit analysis is a systematic approach to estimating the strengths and weaknesses of alternatives. Benefits and costs are expressed in money and adjusted for the time value of money, so that flows in different years become comparable. It has two main uses: judging whether one investment is sound, and comparing options by their total expected cost and benefit. There are related techniques, such as cost-effectiveness analysis, where benefit is measured in outcome units instead of money. In Germany, public bodies are obliged to carry out an economic efficiency study before spending (Bundeshaushaltsordnung, section 7); this is context for public-sector projects only.

## Benefits you cannot price

A compelling business case captures quantifiable and non-quantifiable characteristics. Do not force a euro value onto everything. Our own approach: list non-monetary benefits separately, say who would notice them and how, and let the sponsor weigh them.

## Feasibility (our own four checks)

- Technical: can it be built or bought with what we know?
- Organisational: do the people who must change their work support it?
- Financial: is the budget realistic and available?
- Time: does the deadline allow for the work and for approvals?

## Decision criteria

Agree on criteria before comparing. Our own simple method: three to five criteria, each with a weight, and a score per option. Treat the result as a conversation aid, not as the verdict.

## The project order

The project order formally confirms that the project exists. It is usually signed by a higher-level manager, and it often names the project lead and releases the budget. Terms and contents are not standardised. Typical contents are the project name, the sponsor, start and end, a short description with business need and goals, deliverables, budget, project lead and team, assumptions and constraints, resource allocation and deadlines. Without an order, the start of a project often cannot be reconstructed later, which causes problems with billing and responsibility.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company and numbers. Three options for answering customer questions faster: A) shared mailbox rules (cost 2,000, expected benefit 6,000 over three years), B) a ticket tool (cost 14,000, benefit 20,000), C) do nothing (cost 0, benefit 0, but complaints continue). Weights: benefit 40 %, risk 30 %, effort 30 %. A scores highest on effort, B on benefit. The sponsor chooses A as a first step and writes a review date into the project order, because B's benefit estimate rests on one assumption nobody has tested.

## Key takeaways

- Compare at least two options and always the do-nothing option.
- Express money in comparable terms and list non-monetary benefits separately.
- Check technical, organisational, financial and time feasibility.
- Agree criteria first; the project order records who decided what.
