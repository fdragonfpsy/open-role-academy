## Why this matters

AI tools are now part of daily work: chatbots, translation, résumé screening, spam filters. Legal and compliance staff are asked whether using them is allowed and what must be checked. This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.

## The risk-based idea

The European Commission's page on the AI Act describes it as a set of risk-based rules for developers and deployers of AI regarding specific uses. The idea is simple: the higher the risk of a use of AI to health, safety or fundamental rights, the stricter the rules. The Commission page names four levels:

- **Unacceptable risk:** uses that are considered a clear threat and are banned.
- **High risk:** uses that can pose serious risks to health, safety or fundamental rights and are subject to strict obligations.
- **Transparency risk:** cases where people must be informed that they deal with AI, for instance with a chatbot.
- **Minimal or no risk:** no specific rules; the page gives AI-enabled video games and spam filters as examples and says most AI systems in use fall here.

The page also covers general-purpose AI models separately. Dates, obligations and details change as the rules are applied and clarified, so always read the current official page instead of relying on memory or on this unit.

## Other digital rules

AI is not regulated in isolation. The GDPR applies when personal data is processed, including by AI tools; see the GDPR competency in this role. The Digital Services Act (DSA) sets rules for online services such as marketplaces, social media networks, app stores and travel platforms, according to the Commission's DSA page, which also says that the Digital Markets Act complements it for large "gatekeeper" platforms. Which rules apply depends on who you are (for example, a developer, a deployer or a platform) and what you do.

## What a beginner can do

Collect the tools your team uses, ask what each one does with data, check the official page for the risk level of the use, and pass anything unclear or high-risk to the legal team.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. An invented HR team wants to try a chatbot that answers staff questions about holidays. A compliance colleague asks: What does it do? Does it process personal data? Are users told that it is an AI system? She finds that the use looks like a transparency case, notes that GDPR questions need a data protection review, and writes up the open points for legal counsel instead of giving a final verdict.

## Key takeaways

- The EU AI Act is risk-based: stricter rules for riskier uses.
- The Commission names four levels: unacceptable, high, transparency and minimal or no risk.
- GDPR and platform rules such as the DSA can apply alongside AI rules.
- Official pages change; verify before relying on any summary.
- This unit is education, not legal advice. It describes concepts for Germany and the EU only, with no section numbers, deadlines or amounts; check the current official texts and involve legal counsel for individual cases.
