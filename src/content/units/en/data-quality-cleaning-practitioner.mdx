## Why this matters

Cleaning decisions change results. Deleting rows, filling gaps or removing outliers are analytical choices, not housekeeping, and each needs a stated reason.

## Profile first

Before changing anything, describe the data: number of rows, share of empty values per column, distinct values for categories, minimum, maximum and typical values for numbers. A profile shows where the problems are and which ones matter for your question.

## Missing values

First ask why a value is missing: not entered, not applicable, or not yet known? A blank, a zero and "unknown" mean different things. Options, with their trade-offs:

- Leave it and say so: honest, but some tools skip those rows silently.
- Flag it as its own category ("unknown"): keeps the row and shows the gap.
- Fill it with a justified value, such as the median for a group: keeps the row, but invents precision; the filled share should be reported.
- Remove the row: simple, but biased if the missing rows differ from the others.

Pick one rule per column and write it down.

## Duplicates

There are two kinds. Identical duplicates agree in all values and can be removed without losing information. Non-identical duplicates describe the same real object with differing values, such as two spellings of a name; they cannot simply be deleted, since the values must first be consolidated. Detection needs rules or similarity checks, so review matches before merging.

## Outliers

An outlier is a value far from the rest. Common statistical rules, such as flagging values far outside the middle half of the data, only point to candidates. Investigate: is it a typing error, a unit mix-up or a real rare case? Correct errors, keep real cases and report them separately if they distort averages.

## Standardise and document

Use one format for dates, one spelling for categories, one unit per column. Keep a change log with what, why, how many rows and who decided.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented figures. Among 40 orders, 39 are between 50 and 200 euros and one is 12,000. The analyst checks the source system: the order was entered as 12000 but the invoice says 120.00, a typing error with two extra zeros. The value is corrected, and the log records "order 1032: 12000 corrected to 120.00 per invoice". Had the invoice also said 12,000 for a genuine bulk order, the row would stay and be reported separately, because it would distort the average order value.

## Key takeaways

- Profile before cleaning and decide on one stated rule per column.
- Missing values have different causes; the treatment must fit the cause.
- Identical duplicates can be removed; non-identical ones must be consolidated.
- Investigate outliers before removing them, and log every change.
