## Why this matters

At practitioner level you turn a general idea of the brand into material that colleagues, agencies and freelancers can use without asking you each time.

## Three ways to position

OpenStax distinguishes three bases. Positioning on an **attribute** highlights one feature; it is easily copied by competitors. Positioning on a **benefit** shows how the offer solves the customer's problem, which suits convenience products. Positioning on **values** links to the customer's personal beliefs and, according to OpenStax, is the most robust. The more you move towards values, the more the claim has to be credible in everything you do, otherwise it reads as empty.

## Message hierarchy

A practical structure, our own working method:

1. **Core promise**: one sentence for the whole audience.
2. **Supporting messages**: two or three points that make the promise believable, each with proof such as a fact, a customer result or a certification you really hold.
3. **Audience variants**: the same promise phrased for each target group, with the language of that group.

Writers can then pick the right layer instead of inventing new claims.

## Naming criteria

OpenStax lists that a strong brand name evokes the intended feeling, helps people see the benefit, translates well into other languages and is unique to the product. It also notes that brands use trademarks to protect names and logos. Which protection applies in which country is a legal question; ask the legal team before you commit to a name.

## Voice guide

A brand voice guide is a one-page agreement: three adjectives (for example 'warm, plain, precise'), two 'we say / we avoid' lists, and how formal the address is. For German-language work, decide between 'du' and 'Sie' once.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A regional repair service for bicycles finds that 'fast service' (attribute) is also promised by two competitors. The team shifts to a benefit: 'Back on your bike the same day.' Supporting messages: workshop slots reserved for commuters, a visible price list, a customer quote with permission. The audience variant for families stresses safe brakes. The voice guide says 'direct, friendly, no jargon'.

## Key takeaways

- Attribute claims are easy to copy; benefits and values are harder to copy but must be credible.
- Build messages in layers: promise, proof, audience variant.
- Check names for feeling, benefit, translation and uniqueness, and involve legal for protection.
- A one-page voice guide keeps many authors consistent.
