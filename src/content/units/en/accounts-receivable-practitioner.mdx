## Why this matters

At the practitioner level you no longer just post payments; you decide what a payment belongs to, spot which customers are late, run a fair reminder process and resolve disputes. The goal is to collect what is owed while keeping the customer relationship intact.

## Matching and allocating payments

**Matching** (also called cash application or allocation) means assigning an incoming payment to the open items it settles. Typical cases:

- **Exact payment with reference**: one payment, one invoice number; clear the item.
- **Partial payment**: the customer pays less than the invoice. Clear only the amount received and leave the rest open; find out why (dispute, deduction, mistake).
- **Overpayment**: the customer pays more. Do not just keep it: record it as a credit on the customer account, then agree whether it is refunded or offset against the next invoice.
- **Combined payment**: one transfer covers several invoices. Allocate it item by item, usually oldest first unless the customer states otherwise.
- **Unreferenced payment**: no usable reference. Post it to a clearing account (unallocated receipts), research via amount, sender name and bank details, and ask the customer if needed. Never allocate on a guess.

## The aging list

An **aging list** (German: Fälligkeitsliste or Altersstrukturliste) groups open items by how long they are due or overdue, in time buckets your company defines. It shows where attention is needed: items just past due need a friendly nudge, old items need escalation. Review it on a fixed rhythm, for example weekly.

## Reminders and dunning levels

**Dunning** (German: Mahnwesen) is the process of reminding customers of overdue invoices. Many companies use several levels: a friendly payment reminder first, then a firmer reminder, then a final notice before further steps. How many levels, what timing and which fees or interest may be charged depends on your company policy and on the law of your country. Check your country's rules; do not assume them.

A respectful tone keeps customers willing to pay: state the facts (invoice number, amount, due date), assume an oversight first, offer help (a copy of the invoice, a contact person) and name a clear next step. Check before sending that the invoice is really still open, because a reminder for an already paid invoice damages trust.

## Disputes and credit notes

If a customer disputes an invoice (wrong price, missing delivery, damaged goods), flag the item as disputed, involve sales or logistics, and pause automatic reminders for that item while you clarify. If the customer is right, issue a **credit note** (German: Gutschrift or Stornorechnung depending on the case) that reduces or cancels the receivable; never just delete the invoice. Undisputed parts of an invoice stay due.

## Worked example

Illustration only; the amounts are invented. A customer has an open invoice of 1,190. The bank shows a transfer of 1,000 with the correct invoice number. You clear 1,000 and leave 190 open. A phone call reveals a short delivery worth 119 for which the customer deducted money. The remaining 71 is simply unpaid. You issue a credit note of 119 after sales confirms the short delivery, and send a friendly reminder for the remaining 71.

| Step | Effect on the open item |
|---|---|
| Invoice | 1,190 open |
| Payment received | 190 open |
| Credit note (short delivery) | 71 open |
| Customer pays 71 | 0 open |

## Key takeaways

- Matching assigns payments to open items; partial, over-, combined and unreferenced payments need different handling.
- Never allocate on a guess; use a clearing account and research.
- The aging list shows which items need action; review it regularly.
- Dunning levels and fees depend on policy and country; check your country's rules.
- Stay factual and polite, and handle disputes with a credit note, not deletion.
