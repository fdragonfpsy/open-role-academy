## Why this matters

Appraisals shape development, pay discussions and trust. If ratings depend more on who the manager is than on what people achieved, the process loses credibility. This unit covers bias, consistency, data protection and process improvement. It contains no legal specifics: check current law, your works agreement or legal counsel, and ask your data protection officer (DPO) where personal data is concerned.

## Common rating biases

The Performance appraisal article names the most common errors in judgmental ratings as cognitive biases and statistical errors such as leniency errors, central tendency errors and halo errors. It also mentions the anchoring effect, relying too heavily on initial information. The Halo effect article describes the halo effect as the tendency for a positive impression in one area to influence opinion in another; the term was coined by Edward Thorndike. The German Wikipedia article on Beurteilungsfehler also lists primacy and recency effects, where first or most recent performance is overweighted. Practice insight: managers often describe these in words like "I give everyone a good rating" (leniency), "everyone gets the middle" (central tendency) or "I mostly remember last month" (recency).

## Reducing bias

The Wikipedia article says multi-source feedback from peers, subordinates and customers may improve rating accuracy by reducing leniency and central tendency bias, and that rater training is often used but may be costly, time-consuming and may not produce meaningful results. Practice insight, not from a cited study:

- **Evidence first**: ask managers to bring concrete examples from the whole period.
- **Multiple inputs**: collect more than one viewpoint where your rules allow.
- **Calibration**: managers compare ratings and reasoning against the same criteria before they are final, and discuss outliers.
- **Clear criteria and anchor examples**: reduce room for personal impressions.

Note that the article says employee-comparison methods remove central tendency and leniency errors but still allow halo errors, and it mentions forced distribution. Treat such methods carefully and check the rules and the works council.

## Consistency across teams

Use the same criteria, scale and timetable for everyone, and explain how they are applied. Watch for patterns, for example one team in which everyone gets the top rating. A pattern is a reason to ask questions, not proof of a mistake.

## Development and pay

The Wikipedia article says appraisals can facilitate discussion of pay, development and progression, and that appraisals are part of career development. Practice insight: keep the development conversation (what do you want to learn?) clearly separate from, or clearly explained alongside, any pay decision, and be transparent about how ratings are used. Details of pay systems and legal requirements: check current law, your works agreement or legal counsel.

## Data protection for appraisal data

Appraisal records are personal data about employees. Practice insight: collect only what you need, restrict access to those who need it, agree retention with your records rules, and check with your DPO before introducing a new tool or data field. The DPO article describes the role as ensuring within an organisation that the laws protecting personal data are implemented, including raising data privacy awareness among employees.

## Evaluating and improving the process

Without inventing benchmarks, ask: do managers and employees find the process useful? Are ratings spread plausibly across teams, or do some teams stand out? Are follow-up actions recorded and done? Collect short feedback after each cycle, make one or two changes, and say what changed.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real data. After a cycle, you see that "Team X" gave everyone the same middle rating, while "Team Y" gave almost all top ratings. You do not accuse anyone. You hold a calibration session with both managers using the same criteria, ask for concrete examples for each rating, and adjust where examples are thin. You then add a short guide on bias to the manager pack and note in the process review what to try next cycle.

## Key takeaways

- Named errors include leniency, central tendency, halo, anchoring, primacy and recency.
- Evidence, multiple inputs, calibration and clear criteria can help; they are practice insights, not guarantees.
- Consistency means the same criteria for all, with patterns treated as questions.
- Appraisal data is personal data: check with your DPO. Legal details: check current law and your works agreement.
