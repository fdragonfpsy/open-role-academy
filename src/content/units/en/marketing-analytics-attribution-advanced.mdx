## Why this matters

Attribution reports say who got the credit. Leaders ask something harder: how many conversions would not have happened without the campaign? At advanced level you design measurement that tries to answer that, and you stay honest about what remains uncertain.

## Incrementality

A conversion is **incremental** if it happened because of the marketing. Many people who see a brand search ad would have bought anyway. Last-touch reports credit the ad regardless. The question "what would have happened without it?" needs a comparison group.

## Holdout test (our own working method)

1. Pick one question, for example "Does retargeting add purchases?"
2. Randomly assign eligible people to a **test** group (sees the campaign) and a **holdout** group (does not). Random assignment avoids a hidden difference between the groups.
3. Fix the KPI, the duration and the minimum difference that would change your decision before you start.
4. Compare conversion rates. The difference is the estimated lift.
5. Check that the groups were comparable and that nothing else changed during the test (a sale, a holiday, a new competitor).

Pitfalls: groups too small to tell a real difference from chance; stopping early when the numbers look good; holdout people seeing the campaign through another channel; ignoring costs of the lost reach. Where a randomised test is not possible, compare regions or periods, and say that this is weaker evidence. Ask a data specialist for sample size and significance.

## Consent-aware measurement (EU, topic level)

Measurement based on personal data has to rest on a legal basis. The EDPB guidance on consent, which restates the GDPR definition, says that consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, that pre-ticked boxes and opt-out constructions are not suitable, and that withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it. Consequences for measurement design: some visitors will not be measurable, so reports describe consenting users only; do not treat them as identical to everyone; do not hide the choice in design. The GDPR's principles in Article 5, such as data minimisation and purpose limitation, argue for collecting only what a decision needs. Which tool setup and which legal basis is allowed in your case, including cookie and device-access rules, is a question for legal counsel and the data protection officer.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented numbers. A shop wants to know whether paid search for its own brand name is worth the spend. The team switches the ads off for a randomly chosen half of the regions for four weeks. Orders in both halves are nearly identical. Conclusion in the report: "No visible lift at this sample size; last-touch credit overstated the value. We keep a small budget and repeat with longer duration." The report names the limit instead of claiming certainty.

## Reporting rules

Our own method: one headline KPI, the method, the period, the sample, the uncertainty in words, the decision it supports, and what was not measured.

## Key takeaways

- Attribution splits credit; incrementality tests cause with a comparison group.
- Randomise, define the decision threshold first, avoid early stopping.
- Consent limits who you can measure; report that limit.
- Collect only what the decision needs and involve legal counsel.
