## Why this matters

Supplier management means steering relationships systematically: evaluating and selecting suppliers, developing their performance and deciding how deeply they are integrated into your value chain. Negotiation does not end at signature; most conflicts appear during the contract, when a price, a delivery or a quality problem needs to be solved with a partner you still need.

## Fit the relationship to the supplier

Use the segmentation from the supplier selection unit (for example the Kraljič matrix). Our own approach: for suppliers of standard goods keep the relationship transactional, with contract, order and an annual check. For important suppliers, add a named contact on each side, a fixed review rhythm, shared targets and a joint plan. Invest more where a failure would hurt more.

## Preventing conflict

Management research on conflict lists prevention techniques that carry over to suppliers:

- Emphasise shared goals, such as end customer satisfaction.
- Make tasks and responsibilities clear and well structured, so uncertainty does not breed conflict.
- Improve communication between the groups, as misperceptions of motives cause suspicion.
- Avoid pure win-lose set-ups; share scarce resources and reward results both sides care about.

## Agree an escalation path (our own ladder)

Write it into the contract or the relationship plan before problems arise:

- Level 1: the operational contacts try to solve it within an agreed number of working days.
- Level 2: the category manager and the supplier's account manager decide.
- Level 3: a steering meeting of both managements, possibly with an independent third party as mediator.
- Level 4: formal contractual remedies; involve legal counsel first.

Escalation is a way to protect the relationship, not a threat. Keep the facts and the people apart at each level.

## Price increase requests

Ask for the cost drivers, not for a counter-price. Propose an objective criterion, such as a published index, agreed in advance and applied in both directions. Check your power: a small customer cannot dictate terms to a sole supplier, and may need a volume or duration commitment to get attention. Follow your company's code of conduct: no side deals, and gifts and invitations only within the rules.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented supplier and figures. A supplier asks for an 8 percent increase because of rising raw material prices. The buyer asks for the cost structure: material is about 40 percent of the price and the relevant index rose 10 percent. The justified effect is 40 percent x 10 percent = 4 percent, not 8. The buyer proposes an index clause that moves the price with the index in both directions, reviewed quarterly, and offers a 24-month forecast of volumes in return. When the supplier hesitates, the buyer raises the issue at level 2, where both managers agree the clause and a cost-sharing review in six months.

## Key takeaways

- Match the relationship and meeting rhythm to the supplier's importance.
- Prevent conflict with shared goals, clear responsibilities and open communication.
- Agree the escalation path before the first problem, and use it to protect the relationship.
- Answer price increase requests with cost drivers and objective, two-way criteria.
- Check your own bargaining power honestly, and stay within the code of conduct.
