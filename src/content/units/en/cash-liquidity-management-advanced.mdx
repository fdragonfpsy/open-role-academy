## Forecast accuracy and variance review

A cash forecast is only useful if you know how wrong it tends to be. Each period, compare forecast and actual for inflows and outflows separately, and ask why they differ: a customer paid late, a payment run moved, a one-off item was missing, or a timing assumption was wrong. Record the cause and fix the input, not just the number. The aim is a better next forecast. Because a liquidity plan is rolling, each review feeds the next update.

## Working capital levers and trade-offs

The levers are receivables (collect faster), inventory (hold less) and payables (pay later). The working capital source states that companies strive to shorten the working capital cycle by collecting receivables quicker or sometimes stretching payables, and also warns that minimising working capital can hurt: unforeseen demand can exceed inventories, or a cash shortfall can restrict buying inputs. The cash conversion cycle source adds that a policy of strict collections and lax payments is not always sustainable. So each lever has a cost: tighter collection can strain customer relationships, lower inventory risks stock-outs, later payment can strain supplier relationships and forgo discounts. Choose levers with the people who own those relationships.

## Payment fraud controls

Spoofed emails are used to deceive companies into financial losses, including fraudulent payment requests sent in the name of a senior manager. Controls that treasury and finance teams use:

- **Callback**: confirm a request through a known channel, not the one the request came on, and record it.
- **Bank detail change checks**: treat any change request as high risk and verify before it takes effect.
- **Dual authorisation**: no single person can both prepare and release a payment. Segregation of duties means more than one person is needed for a task, to prevent fraud and errors.
- **Bank-side tools**: the cash management source mentions bank systems where managers create and authorise internal logon credentials for staff who send wires; check which options your bank offers and who holds which rights.

## Contingency when cash is tight

Define in advance who decides, with which triggers, and which steps come first. Typical options: bring in receivables, move non-urgent payments to their due date, delay discretionary spending, use an agreed credit line, or talk to the bank. Remember that liquidity risk compounds other risks: if a counterparty that owes you a payment defaults, you may have to raise cash elsewhere to make your own. Do not stretch suppliers silently; agree changes openly.

## Communication with banks and management

Tell management early, in short form: current position, the lowest expected balance in the plan, what drives the risk, the options and a recommendation. Tell banks before you need them, with a credible plan. Keep numbers consistent with the books and the plan, and say what is forecast and what is actual.

## Key takeaways

- Review forecast versus actual by cause, and improve the inputs.
- Every working capital lever has a cost; weigh it with the owners of the relationships.
- Callback, bank detail change checks and dual authorisation reduce payment fraud.
- Agree contingency steps and decision rights before cash is tight.
- Inform management and banks early, with numbers that match the books.
