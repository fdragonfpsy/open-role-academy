## Why this matters

Marketing decisions based on guesses are expensive. Market and customer research gives you evidence about the market, competitors and customers. The German Wikipedia article on Marktforschung describes it as the systematic collection, processing, analysis and interpretation of data about markets to support marketing decisions. The key word is decisions: research is not collecting facts for their own sake.

## Define the problem first

OpenStax lists defining the problem as the first of seven steps in a research plan. A good research question is specific and linked to a decision. Compare 'What do customers think of us?' with 'Why do first-time visitors to our online shop leave before checkout, and what would make them stay?' The second tells you what to look at and what you will do with the answer.

## Primary and secondary data

- **Primary data** is collected by you for this specific question, for example your own survey, interviews, observation or experiment.
- **Secondary data** was collected for another purpose but may help: sales records, website statistics, published studies, public statistics, industry reports.

OpenStax lists strengths and weaknesses: secondary data is generally cheaper and faster but may not match your question, can be outdated and is also available to competitors; primary data fits your needs and is current, but costs more and takes longer.

## A sensible order

Start with secondary data you already have, such as your own sales and customer service records. Only collect primary data when these sources cannot answer the question. This saves time and avoids asking customers things you could have known.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A small outdoor shop wants to know whether to start selling children's rain jackets. Research question: 'Is there enough demand among families in our region to justify a trial range of ten jackets?' Secondary data: last year's sales of adult rain jackets and of children's shoes, a public statistic on households with children in the region, and the shop's own enquiries ('do you have this for kids?'). The look at existing data shows steady enquiries but no numbers. The owner then adds primary data: five short conversations with parents in the shop. Result: a small test range, not a big order.

## Key takeaways

- A research question is specific and linked to a decision.
- Primary data is new and tailored; secondary data already exists and is cheaper but may not fit.
- Look at existing data before you collect new data.
- Write down the decision the research should support.
