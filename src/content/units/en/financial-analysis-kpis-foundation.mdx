## Why this matters

A set of financial statements contains many numbers, and a single number such as "profit of 30,000" says little on its own. According to the Financial ratio article, a financial ratio states the relative magnitude of two numerical values taken from an enterprise's financial statements. Putting one figure in relation to another lets you ask useful questions: how much of each unit of revenue is left as profit, can the company pay its short-term bills, how is it financed? Managers, owners and creditors all use ratios for this.

## Where the numbers come from

The article explains that the values used in ratios are taken from the balance sheet, the income statement, the statement of cash flows or sometimes the statement of changes in equity. That is why the unit on financial statements comes first. Remember that the figures depend on the accounting method and standards the organisation uses, so two companies' numbers are not automatically built the same way.

A ratio can be shown as a decimal (0.10) or as an equivalent percentage (10%). Always read the unit that is shown.

## Groups of ratios

The Financial ratio article groups ratios by the aspect of the business they measure:

- **Profitability ratios** measure how well the firm uses assets and controls expenses to generate a return.
- **Liquidity ratios** measure the availability of cash to pay debt.
- **Efficiency (activity) ratios** measure how quickly non-cash assets are converted to cash.
- **Debt ratios** measure the ability to repay long-term debt.
- **Market ratios** concern investor response to a company's stock.

In this unit we look at one example each from profitability, liquidity and capital structure.

## Three simple ratios

Always check which formula is used. Here are the ones for this unit.

- **Profit margin (net)** = net profit divided by revenue, times 100. The Profit margin article describes it as the percentage of profit a company earns in relation to its revenue: a 35% margin means 0.35 of profit from each unit of sales.
- **Current ratio** = current assets divided by current liabilities. The Current ratio article describes it as a liquidity ratio showing whether a firm has enough resources to meet its short-term obligations. A value below 1 may indicate problems meeting short-term obligations, but the article adds that this depends on how fast inventory turns to cash and how quickly bills fall due.
- **Equity ratio** = equity divided by total assets (both from the balance sheet). The Equity ratio article says it shows the proportion of a company's assets financed by equity; the rest is financed by creditors.

## Comparing over time

According to the Financial ratio article, ratios are generally not useful unless benchmarked against something else, such as past performance or another company. Comparing a company with its own earlier periods is called time-series analysis. It shows trends and year-to-year changes. For this to be fair, use the same formula and the same period length each time.

## Worked example

Illustration only; all amounts are invented. A small company shows these figures for this year: revenue 200,000, net profit 20,000, current assets 60,000, current liabilities 40,000, equity 150,000, total assets 500,000. Last year its net margin was 8%.

- Net margin = 20,000 / 200,000 x 100 = 10%. Compared with 8% last year, the margin is 2 percentage points higher.
- Current ratio = 60,000 / 40,000 = 1.5.
- Equity ratio = 150,000 / 500,000 = 0.30, or 30%.

The numbers alone do not say "good" or "bad". The next step is to ask why the margin changed and whether the current ratio fits how fast the company collects cash.

## Key takeaways

- A financial ratio relates two figures from the financial statements.
- Main groups: profitability, liquidity, efficiency, debt and market ratios.
- Always know the formula and the unit (decimal or percent) before reading a ratio.
- Net margin = net profit / revenue; current ratio = current assets / current liabilities; equity ratio = equity / total assets.
- A ratio needs a basis for comparison; start with the same company's earlier periods.
