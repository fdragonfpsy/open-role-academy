## Why this matters

In complex deals several people sign off: a business sponsor, finance, IT, procurement and legal. Advanced sellers coordinate them and decide when a deal should be reshaped or declined. The most expensive failure is the **promise gap**: the difference between what the customer believes they bought and what delivery can provide.

## Mapping approvals

Map who decides, who influences and who can block. Ask the customer for their approval path and typical lead times; do not assume it. A signature plan lists each step (security review, procurement, legal, budget approval), its owner, its date and the evidence you can offer. This turns "we are close" into checkable facts that also feed the forecast.

## Negotiating through procurement and legal

Procurement and legal often work from the customer's own templates. The OpenStax chapter on selling stresses the buyer's needs and long-term relationships; in practice, concede in a package and keep a record of every agreed change, so that the final document and your internal notes match. Do not agree on wording you do not understand. Route risk topics (liability, data protection, termination, service levels, anything unusual) to your legal team; contract and liability law differs by country, and the Wikipedia articles on contracts are general overviews, not advice. Where a deal is cross-border within the EU or involves personal data, involve legal and data protection early.

## When not to close

A signed deal that cannot be delivered becomes a dispute, a refund and a lost reference. Escalate when the customer wants a feature that does not exist, an unlimited commitment, a deadline delivery cannot meet or terms outside policy. A deal desk or finance partner can help to reshape: phase the scope, offer a pilot, or replace an open-ended promise with a defined service level. Saying no or "not like this" to a deal is part of the job and protects both sides.

## Closing the promise gap

Hold a kick-off with sales, delivery and the customer's sponsor, and walk through the contract's scope, the success criteria and the assumptions behind the price. Ask delivery to name risks they see. Record agreed changes after signature as proper amendments, not emails alone. After the first weeks, ask both sides what was unclear and improve your handover template.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented. A customer's procurement wants unlimited liability and a go-live in six weeks. The sponsor is eager; the technical lead says integration takes at least ten weeks. You do not close. You tell legal and the deal desk, propose a phased scope (core functions in six weeks, integration in a second phase) and standard liability terms with an explanation. Procurement accepts a cap after legal talks to them. At the kick-off, delivery shows the plan and the customer's sponsor confirms the phases. The promise gap is closed before signature, not after the first invoice dispute.

## Key takeaways

- Map every approval and give each step an owner and a date.
- Keep a record of all changes; send risk topics to legal.
- Do not close a deal that cannot be delivered; reshape it or decline.
- Close the promise gap with a joint kick-off and written amendments.
- Learn from handover problems and improve the template.
