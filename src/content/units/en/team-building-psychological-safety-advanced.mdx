## Why this matters

A team that worked well last year can lose that quality when members change, work spreads over locations or pressure rises. Advanced team leadership means keeping the team healthy over time. This unit is a concept-level discussion.

## Change resets the team

The Tuckman article notes that teams can return to earlier stages when new challenges or disputes arise. A new member, a reorganisation or a new goal can bring forming and storming back. Plan for it: introduce new people deliberately, revisit the norms with them, and tell the team what to expect so friction is not read as failure.

## Remote and hybrid teams

The Wikipedia article on psychological safety links to a Harvard Business Review piece by Edmondson and Mortensen on psychological safety in hybrid workplaces in its references. This unit does not summarise it; treat it as further reading. From general practice: in hybrid teams, people in the room are easy to hear and others are easy to miss. Agree norms on who speaks first, on writing decisions down and on using chat or documents so remote colleagues are equally informed. Avoid rating people by visibility.

## Inclusion as a team norm

Psychological safety is about whether everyone can speak up. Review who actually speaks, whose ideas are taken up and who is interrupted. Make inclusion explicit in the working agreements: rotate facilitation, ask for written input, credit ideas to those who raised them. If someone is excluded or treated badly, address it early and in private first; in the EU and Germany, discrimination and harassment are also legal matters, so involve HR where needed.

## Use team-building with care

The English article describes team building as a collective term for interventions improving team effectiveness and clarifying roles, and distinguishes it from team training and team recreation. Choose the intervention by the problem: unclear roles call for a role clarification session, poor decisions for a decision-making workshop, low trust for conversation formats and not necessarily for an outing. Voluntary events may exclude people with caring duties, so choose times and formats with that in mind.

## Models have limits

Tuckman's stages describe a pattern from a review of published studies; the article notes that only some of the studies identified a conflict stage. Use models to ask better questions, not to label people or teams.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. Ines leads a hybrid team of eight; two people join from another site. She notices that the remote colleagues speak little. She introduces two norms: every meeting starts with a round of short check-ins in a fixed order, and decisions are recorded in a shared document within a day. She also asks the new colleagues in one-to-ones what would make speaking easier. After a month the team reviews the norms and keeps both.

## Key takeaways

- Change in membership or context can bring earlier stages back; plan for it.
- In hybrid teams, design norms so everyone is equally informed and heard.
- Make inclusion explicit, observe who speaks and act early.
- Pick team-building by diagnosed problem and treat models as aids.
