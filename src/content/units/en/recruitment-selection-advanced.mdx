## Why this matters

At advanced level you own the quality of the selection process and you advise hiring managers. You are accountable for decisions that can be challenged, so they must rest on criteria and records. This unit contains no retention periods, thresholds, benchmarks or section numbers. For legal detail check current law, your data protection officer (DPO) or legal counsel. HR staff do not give legal advice.

## Bias and what structure can do

The German article on requirement profiles notes that when experts define success criteria from experience and intuition, this is pragmatic but carries the risk of cognitive distortions such as affinity bias. The English article on job interviews lists personal characteristics that can influence interviewer ratings although they are typically not relevant to job performance, including similarity in background and attitudes, and it describes the effect of physical attractiveness on judgments. It names structured interviewing as one possible way to decrease bias, and says multiple studies show structure improves an interview's ability to identify high performers. It does not claim that structure removes bias entirely, so treat structure as a safeguard and not a guarantee. The recruitment article adds a related risk: sourcing only through employee referrals may jeopardise the diversity of a workforce.

## Decide against criteria, and document it

Compare candidates with the requirement profile: the German article describes the gap between required and observed values as the basis for the decision. Practical steps follow from the sources: rate each answer separately, take detailed notes, let each interviewer score before any group discussion, and then combine the ratings. Record for each finalist the evidence per criterion and the reason for the outcome. Keep the record factual; leave out remarks about personal characteristics that are not relevant to the job. A clear record supports you if a decision is challenged; legal counsel decides what follows.

## Working with hiring managers

The English recruitment article describes a kick-off conversation between recruiter and hiring manager. Use it to agree the profile, the weighting of criteria and who interviews. Explain why all candidates get the same questions and why scoring happens before discussion. When a manager prefers a candidate "who feels right", ask which criterion that impression relates to and whether it was observed in the answers. If a manager asks to add or drop criteria after the first interviews, record the change and the reason, and consider whether earlier candidates must be re-assessed against it.

## Applicant data handling

Applications contain personal data. The German Personalbeschaffung article mentions that unsolicited applications are processed and archived in passive recruitment. We do not state retention periods here. How long data may be kept, who may see it, and what applicants must be told depend on current law and your company's rules. Ask your DPO, restrict access to those involved in the selection, and be careful with candidate data in notes, email and software.

## Measure the process, without benchmarks

The employer branding article lists simple ways to look at results, such as how applicants turn into hires, conversion rates after changes, and time to fill. It also says the return on such efforts can be hard to measure. Time to hire or time to fill is simply the time from opening a search to an accepted offer; define the start and end points once and use the same definition each time. Compare against your own earlier searches, not outside numbers.

## Candidate experience

The job interview article reports that applicants judged fit better when they could ask questions and add information, and that an interviewer who did not know the organisation was a strong negative. Prepare, give candidates time to ask, explain the next steps and send timely, respectful messages, also to those who are rejected.

## Worked example

Illustration only; no real figures. Two finalists score similarly. The manager prefers the one who "reminds me of the last great hire". Step 1: return to the criteria sheet and compare evidence per criterion. Step 2: ask the manager which criterion the preference reflects. Step 3: check that both candidates were asked the same questions and scored per answer. Step 4: write the decision and its reasons. Step 5: tell the unsuccessful finalist the outcome respectfully. Step 6: raise any doubt about fairness with legal counsel.

## Key takeaways

- Intuition-based criteria and similarity can distort ratings; structure is a safeguard, not a guarantee.
- Decide against the profile, score per answer, and record evidence and reasons.
- Agree criteria with hiring managers early and record any change.
- Handle applicant data in line with your DPO's guidance; do not assume retention rules.
- Measure against your own earlier searches and improve candidate experience. Check current law, your DPO or legal counsel for legal questions.
