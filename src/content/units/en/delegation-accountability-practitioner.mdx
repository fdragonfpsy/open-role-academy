## Why this matters

Handing over a task is only half of delegation. The other half is how much freedom you give and how you follow up. Too little freedom leads to micromanagement, too little follow-up to surprises. This unit shows how to find a balance at concept level.

## Delegate outcomes, not steps

Describe the result, the quality needed and the limits (time, budget, things not to change), and leave the method to the person. This respects their skills and lets them find better ways. Give more detail only where a step really matters, for example a legal or safety requirement.

## Levels of freedom

Agree explicitly how much freedom the person has. A simple illustrative ladder (not a named model): (1) do exactly what I describe; (2) research and propose, I decide; (3) decide and tell me before acting; (4) decide, act and report afterwards; (5) decide and act, report only if needed. Choose the level from experience, risk and consequences of mistakes, and revisit it as the person grows.

## Check-ins

Agree checkpoints at the start: when, in what form, and what you want to see (for example a draft after half the time, or a short message when a risk appears). Make checkpoints fit the risk: more frequent for new tasks or costly mistakes, rarer for routine. When you check, ask questions about progress and obstacles rather than taking over.

## Clarifying roles with RACI

The Wikipedia article on the responsibility assignment matrix describes the four roles responsible, accountable, consulted and informed, and states that accountable stakeholders are held individually and ultimately responsible for success or failure. A small RACI table for a shared task shows who does the work, who owns the result, who gives input and who is only told.

## Micromanagement

Wikipedia describes micromanagement as excessive focus on observing and controlling and on detail, and notes that inefficient delegation may lead to it. Warning signs: you redo finished work, you want to approve every small decision, you ask for constant updates. A closely related problem described there is giving someone accountability for results without the authority to act. Ask yourself whether the person has what they need to succeed.

## Reverse delegation

Reverse delegation means a task comes back to you, often because someone says "I'm stuck, can you do it?". Ask for their proposal first ("What would you do?"), give a hint if needed, and agree who does the next step.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented team. Priya delegates the planning of a team workshop to Jan: outcome is a one-day agenda, within a fixed budget, ready two weeks ahead. Jan may choose venue and format but must ask before exceeding the budget. They agree one check-in after the first draft. Midway Jan asks Priya to choose a venue. Priya asks for two options with pros and cons, and Jan then decides.

## Key takeaways

- Delegate outcomes and limits; leave the method to the person.
- Agree the level of freedom and check-ins up front; adjust with experience and risk.
- Micromanagement is excessive control of detail; do not hold people accountable without authority.
- Meet reverse delegation by asking for the person's proposal.
