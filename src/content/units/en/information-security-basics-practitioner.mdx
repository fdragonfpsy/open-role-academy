## From personal habits to team practice

At practitioner level you do not only protect your own accounts — you also influence how your team handles access, data and unusual requests. Three topics matter most: who may access what, how attackers manipulate people, and what to do when something has already gone wrong.

## Least privilege and need-to-know

**Least privilege** means every person and every system gets only the rights needed for the task, and only for as long as needed. **Need-to-know** applies the same idea to information: you see what you need for your work, not everything that would be interesting. Both principles limit the damage when an account is compromised or a mistake happens.

In practice this means: request specific roles instead of "the same rights as my colleague", remove access when people change teams, and never share logins. Shared accounts also destroy **accountability**, because actions can no longer be traced to a person.

## Social engineering beyond simple phishing

Social engineering exploits trust, helpfulness, authority and time pressure. A common pattern in companies is the fake request from a manager or supplier: "Please transfer the payment today to our new account, and keep it confidential." The message may come from a look-alike address or even from a real but compromised mailbox.

The defence is a **process**, not a feeling: verify unusual requests through a **separate, known channel**, for example by calling the phone number you already have — not the one in the e-mail. Changes to bank details and urgent payments should always need such a check.

## When something has happened

If you clicked a link, entered a password on a suspicious page or sent data to the wrong recipient: **report it immediately** through the defined channel. Speed matters, because IT can lock accounts, check logs and warn others. Do not forward the suspicious e-mail to colleagues, and do not try to clean up on your own.

**Availability** also needs preparation. Ransomware can encrypt everything a compromised account can write to — including copies on the same drive and sync folders. Only **separated backups** that are regularly tested by actually restoring data help.

## Worked example

Jonas in purchasing receives an e-mail from a known supplier: new bank details, please use them from today. He checks the request by calling the supplier's number from the existing contract. The supplier knows nothing about it — their mailbox was compromised. Jonas reports the case; the payment is stopped and other teams are warned.

## Key takeaways

- Ask for the minimum access you need and give back what you no longer use.
- Verify unusual requests out of band — especially payments and bank details.
- Report incidents at once; speed limits damage.
- Backups only count if they are separated and tested.
