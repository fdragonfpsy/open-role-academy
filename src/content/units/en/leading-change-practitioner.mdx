## Why this matters

Once you know why people resist, you need a plan. Several well-known models give a checklist of things leaders tend to forget. They are aids for thinking, not recipes that guarantee success.

## Three models as planning aids

The Wikipedia article on change management and the OpenStax chapter on managing change describe **Kurt Lewin's** model with three phases: unfreezing (disturbing existing routines so people see the need), changing, and refreezing (making new practices normal). The English article notes that some scholars criticise the model as too simple, while others defend it.

**John Kotter's** eight steps, as listed in the English article: create a sense of urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a strategic vision and initiatives, enlist a volunteer army, enable action by removing barriers, generate short-term wins, sustain acceleration, institute change. OpenStax describes the later steps in similar terms: communicate the vision, remove obstacles, create small wins, consolidate improvements and anchor the changes.

**ADKAR** (created by Jeff Hiatt, according to the English article) looks at one person at a time. Its five building blocks are Awareness of the need, Desire to take part, Knowledge of what to do, Ability to do it, and Reinforcement. If one block is weak, work on that one before moving on. A team member who lacks Knowledge needs training, not another speech about urgency.

Use any model for questions: Does everyone know why? Who supports it? What blocks people? What is our first visible result? How will we make it stick?

## Pilot first

OpenStax stresses that small wins show that change is possible. A small pilot creates such a win and also provides learning: a few volunteers try the new way for a few weeks, you collect what works and what does not, and then you adjust before rolling out wider. Choose a pilot that is representative enough to teach something and small enough to be safe. State beforehand how you will judge it.

## Communicate in a rhythm

Plan communication as a rhythm: a regular slot in the team meeting, a place for open questions, and a short update even when nothing has changed. Silence between announcements often fills with rumours. Listen as much as you speak, and pass concerns on to the project team.

## Reinforce

Refreezing and the "R" in ADKAR both stand for making the new way last: adjust goals, processes and recognition so they match the change, and check some weeks later whether people have drifted back.

## Employee representatives in Germany

In Germany, the German article on Betriebsänderung explains that, in companies with a Betriebsrat (works council), a Betriebsänderung such as restructuring or introducing fundamentally new work methods triggers information duties and, under conditions, consultation duties and co-determination rights. The article on the Betriebsrat describes it as an institutionalised employee representation. At topic level: if your change reorganises work, merges teams or introduces new tools that affect many people, ask HR or legal early whether the works council must be informed or involved. Do not decide this yourself and do not announce details before it is clarified. This unit gives no legal advice; the details depend on the company and the case.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company in Germany. A logistics firm plans to merge two warehouse teams. Svenja, a team lead, uses the models as questions. Awareness: she explains the reason in a team meeting. Knowledge: two people need training on the shared system. Before the announcement she asks HR whether the works council must be involved, and HR confirms that the works council is being informed. She proposes a four-week pilot with one shared shift, agrees a Friday update, and after the pilot adjusts the handover rules before the full merge.

## Key takeaways

- Lewin, Kotter and ADKAR are checklists for planning, not guarantees.
- Pilot small, communicate in a rhythm and keep listening.
- Reinforce the change until it is routine.
- In Germany, ask HR or legal early whether the works council must be involved.
