## Why this matters

One number never tells the whole story. A team can answer fast and still leave customers unhappy, or fix everything and make people wait. At practitioner level you read several measures together, check the quality of the work itself and understand why staffing decides whether targets are reachable.

## A balanced set of measures

A useful set has three views:

- Speed: first response time and resolution time, both averaged over cases.
- Outcome: the share of cases solved, and in our own working definition, first-contact resolution (the share solved in the first contact without a follow-up) and the reopen rate.
- Customer view: CSAT, and sometimes NPS.

Speed alone invites shortcuts, so always pair it with an outcome or customer measure.

## NPS in one paragraph

The net promoter score rests on one survey question: how likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague? Answers of 9 or 10 are promoters, 7 or 8 are passives and 6 or lower are detractors. NPS is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors, so it lies between minus 100 and plus 100. It is usually combined with an open "why?" question, and that text is often the most useful part.

## Service level as a percentage

In contact centres a service level is often stated as "X percent of contacts handled within Y seconds". The share is counted over a period, so a team can miss it on a busy hour and still meet it over the day. Always read the period together with the percentage.

## Quality monitoring and coaching (our own)

Our own simple method:

- Use a scorecard with four to six criteria, such as understanding the issue, correct information, tone and next step.
- Review a few contacts per person each month and score them with a second reviewer now and then, so scoring stays consistent.
- Coach with one strength and one improvement per review, using concrete examples from the contact.

Score the work, not the person, and share the criteria with the team in advance.

## Staffing basics

Callcentre planning typically starts with a forecast of contact volume per time slice. Known events, such as maintenance, billing runs and campaigns, raise the forecast. From the forecast and the service level target the number of agents per slice is derived, and real-time planning adjusts for surprises. Software called workforce management supports this because of the data volume. The practical lesson: if a service level target is missed every Monday morning, the cause is often planning, not agent effort.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented numbers. A team had 400 calls on Tuesday and answered 340 within 30 seconds, so its service level is 340 / 400 = 85 percent against a target of 80 percent. In the monthly survey, 45 of 100 customers are promoters, 35 passives and 20 detractors: NPS = 45 − 20 = 25. The first-contact resolution is only 60 percent. The lead concludes that speed is fine but too many issues need a second contact, and plans a knowledge article and two coaching sessions on checking the whole question before replying.

## Key takeaways

- Combine speed, outcome and customer-view measures.
- NPS is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors.
- A service level percentage needs its time limit and its period.
- Score work against shared criteria and coach with concrete examples.
- Forecasts and targets determine staffing; missed targets can be a planning issue.
