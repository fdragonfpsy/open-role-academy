## Why this matters

Many projects start without knowing exactly what is needed. Agile ways of working respond to this: instead of one long plan followed by one big delivery, the team delivers in small steps, shows them to users and adjusts. Agile approaches aim to raise transparency and the speed of change and to get a usable result in use sooner, with short coordination cycles with the customer.

## Iterative and incremental (our own wording)

- Incremental: the result grows in usable parts.
- Iterative: you revisit and improve parts after feedback.

Together they let you learn from real use before most of the money is spent.

## A common rhythm

Scrum is one widely used agile framework. It breaks work into goals to be finished in time-boxed iterations called sprints, each no longer than one month and commonly two weeks. The team checks progress in a stand-up meeting of at most 15 minutes, the daily scrum. At the end of a sprint there are two meetings: a review where the work is shown to stakeholders and feedback is collected, and an internal retrospective on how the team works. Scrum teams are meant to be cross-functional and self-managing. You do not need to adopt Scrum as a whole to use these ideas; they are tools.

## Backlog and boards (our own description)

- A backlog is an ordered list of the work still to be done. The most valuable or most urgent items are at the top and are described in most detail.
- A visual board shows work items as cards in columns such as "to do", "in progress" and "done". Kanban is a method built on making work visible; it helps to see tasks and bottlenecks, and it shortens lead times.
- Limiting how much work is in progress at once keeps the team from starting everything and finishing nothing.

## When agile fits

Agile fits best where requirements are uncertain or change, users can give feedback early, and the team can work closely together. Where scope, date and price are fixed by contract and little is unknown, a plan-driven approach may fit better. Many projects need both, which the practitioner unit covers.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented project. A team builds an online appointment booking for a clinic. In sprint 1 it delivers the simplest possible booking, usable by staff only. In the sprint review, reception staff say that the cancellation step is the real problem. The team moves "easy cancellation" to the top of the backlog and delivers it in sprint 2, before adding patient self-service. A planned feature "colour themes" falls to the bottom of the list.

## Key takeaways

- Agile delivers in small, usable increments and adapts after feedback.
- Time-boxed iterations, a short daily check, a review and a retrospective form a simple rhythm.
- A backlog is an ordered list; a board makes work and bottlenecks visible.
- Agile fits uncertainty; it does not remove the need for goals, priorities and discipline.
