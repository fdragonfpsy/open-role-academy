## Why this matters

Users rarely contact support because they want to talk about technology. They want to get back to their work. Your technical skill only helps if the user feels understood, can follow what you say and knows what happens next. **Customer support** is, at its core, helping people use a product or service successfully; for internal IT support the "customers" are your colleagues.

## Ask good questions

Users describe symptoms in their own words: "The internet is broken." Your first job is to find out what really happens, without making the person feel questioned.

- Start **open**: "What were you trying to do when the problem appeared?" Open questions invite a story and often reveal details you would not have asked about.
- Then get **specific**: "Which message do you see exactly?" "Did it work yesterday?" "Is anyone else in your team affected?"
- Ask **one question at a time**. A list of five questions in one message usually gets one answer.
- Use **active listening**: pay full attention, restate in your own words ("So you can open the file, but saving fails — is that right?") and check that you understood.
- Avoid questions that sound like blame, such as "What did you do?" Prefer "What happened right before?"

## Use plain language

**Plain language** means writing so that the reader can find what they need, understand it the first time and act on it. For support this means:

- Use the words the user uses. Say "sign in" rather than "authenticate", or explain the term once.
- Short sentences, one idea each. Put the most important thing first.
- Give steps as a numbered list, one action per step.
- Avoid jargon and abbreviations; if you must use one, explain it in a few words.
- Never imply the user is at fault ("You should have..."). Describe what to do now.

Some readers need especially simple text. **Easy-to-read language** such as Leichte Sprache in Germany follows stricter rules; you do not need to master it, but it shows how far simplification can go.

## Write polite, useful ticket updates

A ticket update is often the only contact a user has with you. A good update has three parts:

1. **Where we are:** what you found or did.
2. **What it means for the user:** can they work, do they need to do something?
3. **What happens next, and when:** the next step and an honest time frame.

Greet the person by name, thank them for details they gave, avoid pasting system text into the message and close with something concrete. Even "no news yet" is worth a short message if you promised an update.

## Worked example

A user writes: "My email is broken!!" A weak reply: "Please provide logs." A better reply:

> Hello Ms Weber, thank you for letting us know. To find the cause quickly, could you tell me what you see when you open your mailbox — an error message, or is it empty? If there is a message, a screenshot would help. I will look at it as soon as I hear from you and will update this ticket by 2 pm.

The reply is polite, asks one clear question, explains why it asks, and promises a next step with a time.

## Key takeaways

- Open questions first, then specific ones; one question at a time.
- Restate what you understood to check it.
- Plain language: the user's words, short sentences, numbered steps, no blame.
- Every ticket update says where we are, what it means for the user and what happens next.
