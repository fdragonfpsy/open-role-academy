## Why this matters

Single comments feel strong but are hard to act on. A pattern across 100 comments is easier to argue for and to fix. Practitioner-level work means turning feedback into counted themes and passing them on in a form others can use.

## Numbers you will meet

- Retention rate: the percentage of customers in the current period who have bought before. It is most useful where purchases recur, as with subscriptions.
- Response rate: the share of invited customers who answered a survey. In one OpenStax example, 1,500 of 15,000 invited customers answered, so conclusions rest on the 10 percent who replied.
- CSAT: the share of 4 and 5 ratings among the answers.
- NPS: the percentage of promoters (9 or 10) minus the percentage of detractors (6 or lower), usually with an open "why?" question next to it. NPS values vary a lot between industries, so compare with your own past, not with an unrelated sector.

Remember: intentions, such as "I would recommend", are only a moderate to weak predictor of what customers actually do. Pair survey measures with behaviour such as cancellations.

## Find themes in comments (our own method)

- Take a sample of comments, for example the last 100.
- Draft six to ten theme tags with a short definition each, such as "delivery information", "waiting time", "price".
- Tag each comment with one main theme, and note the sentiment (positive, neutral or negative).
- Count per theme and review a few examples to check that tags are applied consistently.
- Revisit the tags monthly; add a theme only when it appears repeatedly.

## Two loops (our own)

Our own view of closing the loop has two levels. The inner loop is with the customer: thank them, tell them what you did. The outer loop is with the company: pass the theme on so the cause is fixed. Customers who see that feedback changes something are more likely to answer again.

## An insight note for product and other teams (our own)

Keep it to five lines: the theme, how many cases in which period, one or two anonymised examples, the likely effect on customers (such as extra contacts or cancellations) and a suggestion. Do not include names or contact details; the receiving team does not need them. Do not promise changes on behalf of others.

## Worked example

Illustration only; invented company. A team tags 120 comments from a month. 42 are about missing delivery information, 30 about waiting time, 18 about price and 30 about other topics. The lead sends product a five-line note: "Missing delivery information: 42 of 120 comments in October; example: 'I did not know that it comes a day later'; likely cause extra contacts; suggestion: send a notice when the delivery date changes." Next month the same theme falls to 25 of 110. The lead tells the customers who had mentioned it what changed.

## Key takeaways

- Tag and count comments to find themes; check tags for consistency.
- Look at response rates, and compare NPS with your own past.
- Survey intentions are not the same as behaviour.
- Close the loop twice: with the customer and with the owner of the cause.
- Insight notes are short, counted and anonymised.
