## Why this matters

Finance staff handle money, records and sensitive data, so mistakes and misuse can be costly. **Internal control** is described as a process for assuring that an organisation reaches its objectives in three areas: effective and efficient operations, reliable financial reporting, and compliance with laws, regulations and policies. It also plays an important role in detecting and preventing fraud and in protecting the organisation's resources. You do not have to design a control system as a beginner, but you will operate controls every day, so you should understand why they exist.

## Controls are everyone's job

According to the COSO framework described in the Internal control article, everyone in an organisation has some responsibility for internal control, and all personnel should communicate problems upward. Controls can be manual or automated, and the article lists typical kinds: segregation of duties, authorisation of transactions, retention of records, supervision, physical safeguards and reviews of actual results against plans. The article also notes that internal control gives *reasonable*, not absolute, assurance: controls cost effort, so they are balanced against benefit.

## Segregation of duties

Separation (or segregation) of duties means that more than one person is required to complete a task. It is an administrative control used to prevent fraud, theft and errors. The Separation of duties article names four types of function that should ideally not sit with one person: **authorisation**, **custody**, **record keeping** and **reconciliation**. The classic example is the requirement of two signatures on a cheque. The same article stresses that extra protection has to be balanced against extra cost and effort, so small teams often compensate in other ways, for example with a review by a manager.

## The four-eyes principle

The four-eyes principle is one form of this idea: an action or decision is checked by a second person who is independent of the first. In practice, whoever prepares a payment is not the person who approves it. The second person must really look; a signature without checking is not a control.

## Documenting what you did, and keeping vouchers

An **audit trail** is a chronological record that provides documentary evidence of the sequence of activities affecting an operation. In accounting, the article says, it refers to documentation of detailed transactions supporting summary ledger entries, on paper or electronically. As a beginner this means: attach the supporting document (voucher) to each posting, note who did what and when, and write down any decision that is not obvious. How long records must be kept depends on the rules of your country and your organisation; check them rather than guessing.

## Worked example

Illustration only; the scenario and amounts are invented. In a small company, Sam receives supplier invoices, enters them in the system, releases the payment and posts the bank statement. One day Sam enters an invoice for 4,800 twice by mistake and nobody notices, because nobody else sees the process.

Improved set-up: Sam enters the invoice and attaches the invoice and delivery note. Alex, who does not enter invoices, checks the amount against the delivery note and releases the payment. The system keeps a record of who entered and who released. Now the duplicate would be seen by a second person, and the audit trail shows who did what.

## Key takeaways

- Internal control gives reasonable assurance about operations, reporting and compliance.
- Segregation of duties spreads authorisation, custody, record keeping and reconciliation over different people.
- The four-eyes principle means a second, independent person genuinely checks.
- Attach vouchers and record who did what and when; this is your audit trail.
- Retention rules differ by country: check yours instead of assuming.
